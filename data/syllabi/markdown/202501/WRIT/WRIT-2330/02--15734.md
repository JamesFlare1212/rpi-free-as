## Learning Outcomes

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes (CLOs):

- • Students will demonstrate knowledge of fiction writing in general and the short story in particular by utilizing appropriate terminology to analyze and discuss published fiction.
- • Students will demonstrate craft-based reading strategies through annotations, short analytical responses, and discussions.
- • Students will establish a consistent creative practice by regularly producing short creative writings and craft analyses.
- • Students will demonstrate engagement in the writing process through observing, drafting, peer critique, and revision.
- • Students will actively engage in a creative community of peers through participation in workshop and discussion.

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes Assessment Measures:

**Reading.** In this course, you will learn to read like a writer, which is somewhat different than the reading you may be used to in English classes. The expectation is that as you engage with these texts you are “looking behind the curtain” to determine how the artistic effects were achieved and preparing to discuss your theories with others. Reading should be an active endeavor, and your preparedness will be evaluated bi-weekly under through class participation.

**Short Exercises (SEs).** These are short creative writing prompts designed to give you practice in one particular skill. There is no minimum length so long as the prompt is thoughtfully addressed. Maximum length is one double-spaced page. Short exercises should be posted in your unit **SE journal**.

**Craft Analyses:** Once per unit (3 times over the semester), you will submit a two-page craft analysis in which you showcase your ability to read like a writer. You will be given a choice of relevant prompts to which you will respond. See rubric below.

**Drafts:** You will write at least three pieces of fiction this semester: a micro fiction (up to 300-500 words), a work of flash fiction (500-1000 words), and a short story (1200- 2000 words). For full credit, drafts must be polished, free of errors and reflect your attempts at engaging the craft elements we will be studying in class. For this purpose, you are responsible for articulating how your draft meets our class’s assignment criteria. **Drafts submitted without this explanation are eligible for no higher than a “C” grade.** Drafts should be submitted as a PDF (preferred) or MS Word Doc (not as Pages or RTF). They should be ***double-spaced*** in 11- or 12-point font with 1-inch margins and no extra white space between paragraphs. Please do not post a Google Drive link. You are solely responsible for ensuring your submission is uploaded legibly in the proper format before the due date and that all links are working. Submissions that are illegible or otherwise unreadable will be considered unsubmitted.

**Participation:**

*Class Discussions & Activities:* I expect that each of you contribute to discussions, in large and/or small groups, every class. I will routinely evaluate your preparation for class in the following ways: observing small groups, posing questions, requesting questions, informal presentations of group or individual work, in-class writing, and informal quote analyses. I may also give the occasional pop quiz. I will note your contributions weekly and numerically evaluate your class participation at the end of each course unit. Students with more than two absences will see a 1% penalty per absence in their participation grade.

*Workshops***:** I will weight your verbal contributions to workshops heavily in your class participation grade. Instruction in this course follows the guidelines established by the Association of Writers and Writing Programs (AWP) for the teaching of creative writing, which include a “challenging writers’ workshop” as a hallmark. They define this as*…a seminar in which students critique one another’s work under the mentorship of an accomplished writer-teacher. The workshop is writing intensive, offering each student multiple opportunities for submission and revision of creative work.* This method of instruction is considered the gold standard for developing writers at all levels of expertise, and workshopping is a key learning tool. Workshopping teaches you to read and respond to written work from a variety of perspectives, and hearing critique of your own writing will help you understand how successfully your work achieves your goals. Every student is expected to participate fully in workshopping activities, which include:

- Uploading or providing copies of your work for peers
- Arriving to workshop prepared with end comments on peer drafts.
- Participating verbally in workshops during class time.

-Note: In order to receive credit for workshop draft submission, you must also attend your workshop. Students who miss their own workshops, therefore, do not earn draft credit even if a draft is submitted (barring emergencies).

**Peer End Comments.** You will write peer feedback letters for the flash fiction and short story assignments. Good feedback can be short or long, so I won't impose an arbitrary length on yours, but you must include all components. Your stance should be that of a careful and interested reader. You're making suggestions or observations the writer can take into the next draft or into other pieces. In my feedback, I will assume your work is a draft in progress and gear my comments toward improvements you can make in future drafts, even though you may not actually choose to rewrite that particular piece. **Revision Plan:** At the end of the term, you will reflect on course learning and include detailed revision *plans* for your flash fiction and your short story based on a thorough reading of your peer and instructor end comments.

**Literary Citizenship:** All students *are encouraged* to submit a work of fiction to RPI’s McKinney Writing Contest. All students ***are required*** to attend the McKinney Awards ceremony, which celebrates creative writing students at Rensselaer (you may even be a winner!). **Please mark your calendars.**

## Policies

### Attendance Policy:

This is a small seminar class, the success of which is entirely dependent on active student presence and participation. Everyone has two “freebie” absences to use for sleep, self-care, or other obligations without point penalty. Therefore, I do not routinely distinguish between excused and unexcused absences. Otherwise, I expect that you will come to class every class (school athletes see me at the beginning of the term), barring extended illness. Each absence beyond the second one will drop your grade by ten points (1%). If you miss more than five classes, you will receive no credit for the course even if you are otherwise passing. Note that three tardies of half hour or more accrue to one absence. Attendance is calculated into your participation grade.

### Academic Policies:

- ### Academic Integrity

  Student-teacher relationships are built on trust. For example, students must trust that teachers have made appropriate decisions about the structure and content of the courses they teach, and teachers must trust that the assignments that students turn in are their own. Acts that violate this trust undermine the educational process. The Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities and The Graduate Student Supplement define various forms of Academic Dishonesty and you should make yourself familiar with these. In this class, all assignments that are turned in for a grade must represent the student’s own work. In cases where help was received, or teamwork was allowed, a notation on the assignment should indicate your collaboration.

  Violations of academic integrity may also be reported to the appropriate Dean (Dean of Students for undergraduate students or the Dean of Graduate Education for graduate students, respectively).

  If you have any question concerning this policy before submitting an assignment, please ask for clarification. In addition, you can visit the following site for more information on our Academic Integrity Policy: [Students Rights, Responsibilities, and Judicial Affairs](https://info.rpi.edu/dean-students/student-rights-responsibilities-and-judicial-affairs).

### Additional Academic Integrity Course Policy and Penalty Information:

Failure to indicate accurately the extent and precise nature of one's reliance on other sources, including AI, is also a form of plagiarism. Students are responsible for understanding legitimate use of sources, the appropriate ways of acknowledging academic, scholarly, or creative indebtedness. All assignments that are turned in for a grade must represent the student’s own work **completed for this class in particular**. In cases where help was received, AI was used, or teamwork was allowed, a notation on the assignment should indicate your collaboration. If you have a compelling reason for wanting to use previous work of your own for the class, it must be substantially and fundamentally revised, and you must get approval from the instructor first.
