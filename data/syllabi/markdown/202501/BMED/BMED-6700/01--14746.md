## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

**Course Description:** This course is also open to all students at Rensselaer, particularly those supported by NIH grants. This 14-week course includes lectures, discussion sessions, readings, and public forums (see below for Syllabus). Predoctoral Training Program (T32) faculty and external ethicists are invited to participate in the modules. The diverse array of research areas in which the Training Program faculty focuses will provide an enriched student experience. Valuable real-world topics representing challenges that the students will face in the academic and industrial workplaces will be presented. The results of these discussions will be included in the assessment of the course learning objectives. Topics discussed in the course will include the acquisition and use of human tissues or medical data, the use of vertebrate animals in research, and conflicts of interest that can arise with industrial collaborations. Students will benefit from this experience by understanding how ethical challenges may arise in real research programs.

## Course Goals

### Goals:

**Learning Objective:** The goal of the course is to provide broad exposure to the students in the Responsible Conduct for Research while staying updated on latest developments and case studies involving research conduct.

## Course Content

### Content Details:

**Lecture Topics**

Ethics and compliance concepts in research: historical perspective and philosophy; personal vs. societal beliefs;

Environmental and societal impacts of scientific research

How rise of AI (and Chat GPT) impacts biomedical research

Interpersonal responsibility and scientific research ethics: mentor/trainee responsibilities and relationships,

How the research works – proposing research, data acquisition and laboratory tools;

Publication and dissemination of findings; authorship; data management, sharing and ownership;

Peer review and grantsmanship; fraud/research misconduct and policies for handling misconduct

IACUC-IACUC-supervised scientific research - Process, Compliance monitoring etc

Animal use in research and education: animal use, sex and age as biological variables,

Institutional responsibility: University/Institutional process regarding allegations; conflicts of Interest and conflict of commitments;

Common methods of managing conflicts of Interest/commitments within academia/public-private partnerships (individual/institutional);

Genetic technologies: recombinant DNA research, transgenics; gene therapy

Animal welfare, and “rights”; activism and liberal movements; animal uses: pharma production in animals: product testing

Human cell research: Henrietta Lacks' cells; Stem Cells: Embryonic, adult, iPSCs etc (Reference IRB)

Human participation in research: regulations and training; informed consent (Tuskegee); inclusions of age/sex/ race; (Reference IRB)

Genetic technologies: Reproduction & fertility, donation & informed consent, and use of genetic data

Varia: organ donation and organ sales (human and animal issues); bioart: GMO Foods

Academic freedom; modern ethical challenges related to medicine and technology.

### Academic Policies:

- ### Academic Integrity

  Student-teacher relationships are built on trust. For example, students must trust that teachers have made appropriate decisions about the structure and content of the courses they teach, and teachers must trust that the assignments that students turn in are their own. Acts that violate this trust undermine the educational process. The Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities and The Graduate Student Supplement define various forms of Academic Dishonesty and you should make yourself familiar with these. In this class, all assignments that are turned in for a grade must represent the student’s own work. In cases where help was received, or teamwork was allowed, a notation on the assignment should indicate your collaboration.

  Violations of academic integrity may also be reported to the appropriate Dean (Dean of Students for undergraduate students or the Dean of Graduate Education for graduate students, respectively).

  If you have any question concerning this policy before submitting an assignment, please ask for clarification. In addition, you can visit the following site for more information on our Academic Integrity Policy: [Students Rights, Responsibilities, and Judicial Affairs](https://info.rpi.edu/dean-students/student-rights-responsibilities-and-judicial-affairs).

### Additional Academic Integrity Course Policy and Penalty Information:

## \*\*Academic Integrity:\*\* Student-teacher relationships are built on trust. For instance, students must trust that teachers have made appropriate decisions regarding the structure and content of their courses, while teachers must trust that the assignments students submit are their own work. Actions that violate this trust undermine the educational process. The Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities defines various forms of academic dishonesty, and you should familiarize yourself with these definitions. In this class, all assignments submitted for a grade must reflect the student’s own work. If you received help or if teamwork was allowed, you must indicate your collaboration on the assignment. Submitting any assignment that violates this policy will result in an "F" grade for the course. If you have any questions about this policy before submitting an assignment, please ask for clarification. Being found guilty of any form of academic dishonesty described in the Rensselaer Handbook will result in an "F" grade in the course, along with other disciplinary actions deemed appropriate as outlined in the Handbook. Examples of academic dishonesty include: academic fraud, collaborating during an exam, copying during an exam, cribbing during an exam, fabrication, plagiarism, sabotage, and substitution.
