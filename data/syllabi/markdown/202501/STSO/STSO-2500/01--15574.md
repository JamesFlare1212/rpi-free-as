## Learning Outcomes

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes (CLOs):

- Recognize the role storytelling plays in history and appreciate the political and social factors that shape historical storytelling.
- Analyze and interpret historical sources.
- Generate questions and construct narratives about the past.

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes Assessment Measures:

*Meetings*: Our meetings are opportunities to discuss the materials and engage in a variety of exploratory and experimental exercises designed to facilitate learning and build a sense of community. Be prepared to participate.

*Journals*: You’re invited to submit journals prior to each of our meetings. While you should feel free to complete them however you find most useful, you might consider …

- Reflecting on your experience. What was it like to study these materials? How would you describe your experience? How did it make you feel?
- Highlighting quotes you found meaningful or significant.
- Identifying and explaining vocabulary, keywords, or concepts that seem significant.
- Keeping track of questions to discuss in class, topics to explore, and ideas to wrestle with.

*Other Endeavors*: These endeavors are intended to help us better comprehend the material, connect with our curiosities, and generate meaningful questions…

- Primary Source Projects
  - o historical map interpretation
  - o historical letter transcriptions
  - o exploratory historical newspaper research project
  - o family oral history collection (possibly)
- 2-Minute Historical Theater Performances
- Epistolary Exchanges
- Historical Essay

*Final Reflections*: At the end of the semester, you’ll be asked to submit a portfolio of your work as well as a final reflection on your experience in the course.

## Grading Criteria

### Criteria Details:

*Assessment*: There is no standardized grading rubric for this course. We will discuss your personal goals at various points throughout the semester and determine how best to apply them to the coursework. You’ll receive feedback (but not a letter grade) largely in the form of questions and comments to generate a constructive dialogue about (rather than simply evaluate) your work. You’ll have ample opportunities to self-evaluate, revise, and resubmit any work you choose. At the end of the semester, you’ll submit a portfolio and have significant input in determining your final grade.

## Policies

### Academic Policies:

- ### Academic Integrity

  Student-teacher relationships are built on trust. For example, students must trust that teachers have made appropriate decisions about the structure and content of the courses they teach, and teachers must trust that the assignments that students turn in are their own. Acts that violate this trust undermine the educational process. The Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities and The Graduate Student Supplement define various forms of Academic Dishonesty and you should make yourself familiar with these. In this class, all assignments that are turned in for a grade must represent the student’s own work. In cases where help was received, or teamwork was allowed, a notation on the assignment should indicate your collaboration.

  Violations of academic integrity may also be reported to the appropriate Dean (Dean of Students for undergraduate students or the Dean of Graduate Education for graduate students, respectively).

  If you have any question concerning this policy before submitting an assignment, please ask for clarification. In addition, you can visit the following site for more information on our Academic Integrity Policy: [Students Rights, Responsibilities, and Judicial Affairs](https://info.rpi.edu/dean-students/student-rights-responsibilities-and-judicial-affairs).
