## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

This course teaches basic historical, anthropological, and sociological concepts for making sense of how psychoactive drugs have been used in historical and contemporary societies. We analyze how licit and illicit drugs serve as “technologies” in specific settings and subcultures; how these technologies are produced, distributed, and consumed; what meanings are attached to them and how these change over time; how drug policy governs them; what these technologies and their use tell us about society and culture in different times and places; and impacts of biomedical knowledge on drug users, including persons with addictions.

We focus on representation of drug use and drug users in popular culture, science, medicine, and social science, tracing how public and private values are translated into drug law and policy. We study how social and cultural change affects specific communities of drug users. You will learn how scientific knowledge generated about drug addiction affects policymaking, and how evidence-based policy works within this arena. Policy is defined as state management of populations defined in response to social problems.

As an upper-level interdisciplinary humanities and social sciences course, this course stimulates critical thinking by making ethical and political relations visible in public policy texts, court cases, the policymaking process, and social outcomes. Although we center U.S. drug policy because it has greatly influenced regulation of the global drug trade, we discuss the “new drug history” emerging all over the world. Meanings attributed to drug use vary historically and culturally; comparative examples abound and each student is asked to present a comparative case in addition to a US-based presentation as a way to build awareness of drugs into understandings of global citizenship. As a Communication Intensive course, students will receive and give feedback that helps them develop reading, writing, discussion, and presentation skills.

## Course Goals

### Goals:

**COURSE LEARNING OUTCOMES**

Students who successfully complete this course will demonstrate the following:

- \- Ability to think critically about and discuss—in oral and written form—contemporary social problems related to drugs and drug policy, recognizing the matrix of factors (economic, technical, biophysical, cultural, etc.) that frame problems and constrain solutions.
- \- Ability to respond to complex, content-related questions concerning historical events in the regulation and control of legal and illegal drugs
- \- Ability to analyze and present findings on particular historical, cultural, and social contexts
- \- Specific knowledge concerning events that led to the current regulatory regime governing global drug production, distribution, and consumption.

**Thursday,** **January 9**

Drug policy debates evolve from media panics marking boundary conflicts between social groups—some “normal,” others “deviant,” a boundary Becker likens to that between “weeds” and “plants.” These lines are social constructs. Sociologist Craig Reinarman considers how drug ‘scares’ (or ‘moral panics’) demarcate boundaries between social order and disorder. The interest groups that police these boundaries **frame** drugs in society. Collect examples of **how drugs and drug scares are framed;** bring them to class today for an activity that can only be as exciting and interesting as **your** examples.
