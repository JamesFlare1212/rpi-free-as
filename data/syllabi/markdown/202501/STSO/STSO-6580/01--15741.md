## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

“History and Ethnography” is an introductory/intermediate graduate theory and methods seminar that outfits students to undertake original interpretive work based on empirical observation and analysis. Although pitched to students in the interdisciplinary field of Science and Technology Studies (STS), the course will be useful to Ph.D. students in other programs. This course is geared to graduate students who want to combine historical and ethnographic approaches with discourse analysis or other qualitative analytic approaches that may be mixed with quantitative approaches. There are substantial fieldwork and archival components. The course supports students in crafting historical and ethnographic sections of the dissertation proposal and literature reviews that comprise their candidacy exams.

Technoscientific practices are central to political and social life, yet conceptual methodologies for “studying up” place students in the position of “reinventing the wheel” with inadequate tools. This course provides a conceptual basis for studying how technoscience is socially constructed from discursive material; how meaning is collectively negotiated and enacted throughout research enterprises and advocacy environments (both governmental and nongovernmental); and how interpretive work proceeds. Both historical and ethnographic methodologies can provide a means for capturing the data that provides the evidence base for your analysis.

Historians and ethnographers—who combine elements of each practice—conduct empirical investigations into social situations, and then frame, interpret, and analyze what they find. This course begins as all such courses must—by posing the question of how we can do empirical in the wake of the postmodernist critique of positivist practice. We center pragmatic questions designed to demystify the research process: What do historians *do* in the archives? What are ethnographers listening or looking *for*? What kinds of *reflexivity* are useful to pay attention to in the field?

Exemplary texts relevant to your study of historiography and ethnography will focus our reading in the early part of the semester. We then turn more purposefully towards research design. The practical goal of this course will be for each student to emerge from it having mapped out a project and written an historical and ethnographic analysis, having mapped specific routes for the project to proceed. This course represents a “down payment” on future research and offers the opportunity for “pilot” studies that may find their way into dissertation proposals and even into dissertations.

## Course Goals

### Goals:

*Students will closely study one or two advanced practitioners of historical and ethnographic methods to discover how exemplary texts are constructed.*

There is no single “right way” to go about designing historical or ethnographic projects. There are multiple strategies for doing so. We study strategies used by advanced practitioners of their crafts through close study of one or two exemplary texts of the student’s choice.

*Students will learn to think purposefully and strategically about research design for historical and ethnographic projects.*

The challenge of original scholarship lies with assembling your own strategies for pursuing your research. How will you go about selecting a topic and reviewing extant historical and ethnographic literatures? How will you learn what relevant historical work has been done on that topic? How will you identify archives in which to spend time? What will you do once you get there? How will you evaluate your sources? Will you use oral history, intensive interviews, or ethnographic interviews? If so, how will you conduct such interviews? How will you interpret and analyze them? Will you employ ‘participant observation’? If so, how will you go about it? We will entertain these and other questions.

*Students will be supported in producing concise statements of ‘background’ and/or ‘prior work’ for the dissertation proposal or dissertation project, one a ‘history’, the other an ‘ethnography’.*

Assignments are meant to be short enough to be incorporated into dissertation proposals. All writing assignments in this course also convey certain aspects of the ‘craft’ of academic writing.

Week 12 Proposing Dissertation Research

**Thursday, April 3**

We will review the parts of a dissertation proposal, which is a comprehensive scoping review of the proposed dissertation topic. The proposal should begin with a 500-word abstract/overview of the problematic; the intellectual merit of investigating it; and the potential broad social impact of your dissertation. Then go into a more extensive Background summary that situates your work relative to current and historical research. Research Questions should be clearly stated in research-able form. You should then expand the Conceptual Framework and/or Methodologies by which you plan to conduct the research that will lead to the answers to your questions. Include Prior Work (including the work in this course) and indicate how you have amassed the experience that makes you the best person to conduct this research. Indicate the potential broad social impact and intellectual merit of your work, and the contribution that it will make to the scholarly literatures that converge in the arena to produce your problematic. Finally, you will need to include a timeline for the research and a chapter outline so that readers can get a sense of what a dissertation in this domain will look like (yes, read other people’s dissertation proposals and dissertations).

In STS the literature reviews are \*separate\* from the dissertation proposal; these should discuss at least three domains of literature and contain a thorough bibliography demonstrating the student’s grasp of the domain.

**Reading required:** At least two dissertation proposals from your program.

## Policies

### Academic Policies:

- ### Academic Integrity

  Student-teacher relationships are built on trust. For example, students must trust that teachers have made appropriate decisions about the structure and content of the courses they teach, and teachers must trust that the assignments that students turn in are their own. Acts that violate this trust undermine the educational process. ### Additional Academic Integrity Course Policy and Penalty Information:

Student-teacher relationships are built on trust. You must trust that I have made appropriate decisions about structure and content; I must trust that the assignments you turn in are your own. Acts that violate this trust undermine the integrity of the educational process. The Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities defines various forms of Academic Dishonesty and procedures for responding to such violations of trust between students and teachers.

The Rensselaer Graduate Student Supplement to the Rensselaer Student Handbook of Rights & Responsibilities indicates several forms of Scholarly Misconduct, including plagiarism, defined as: “representing the work or words of another as one’s own through the omission of acknowledgment or reference; for example, using sentences verbatim from a published source in a term paper without appropriate referencing, or presenting as one’s own the detailed argument of a published source, or presenting as one’s own electronically or digitally enhanced graphic representations from any form of media.” That would seemingly also apply to so-called generative AI. If you interact with generative AI, you will need to \*verify\* every reference included and be transparent about exactly what interactions took place. This will take you at least as long as actually writing the piece yourself, because I will want a map of \*every\* interaction—in addition to the assigned writing—and you will need to “train” the AI not to use its pattern recognition capacities to \*make stuff up,\* ensure that everything it produces is verifiable.

There are real consequences for acts of academic dishonesty. Please do not place either of us in the position of having to pursue them. Submission of any assignment in violation of this policy will result in an academic (grading) penalty. You cannot earn a grade for work that is not your own; you forfeit the entire percentage of your grade for that assignment if you do submit work that is not your own. There are also judicial processes to determine further sanctions beyond my control. Please be aware that I consider academic dishonesty to erode student-teacher relationships past the point of rehabilitation. Just not worth it, especially in graduate school.
