## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

This course is an advanced introduction to “the policy-relevant side of STS” (Hess 2012). We will examine three broad topics:

1. How states shape scientific research and the development of technologies and infrastructures.
2. How legislators and regulatory bureaucracies use scientific knowledge and expert opinion in decision-making processes.
3. How publics, particularly social movements, engage in political processes that are dominated by scientific and technological experts.

Additionally, students will learn how to analyze public policy and write a policy paper, or “white paper,” on a topic of their choosing. This will be a research writing project with several stages throughout the semester.

# Assignments and Grading

Readings and Discussion

This is a discussion-centered course, and all students will be expected to actively lead and participate in discussions about the assigned readings. Each week in which there are assigned readings, you must turn in a brief response to these readings (approx. 300 words) and a question to initiate discussion. Please note that a discussion question is an open-ended question that is likely to prompt diverse responses from the participants in this course. Discussion questions should be grounded in specific passages from the readings (e.g., “on page 25, the author says…”) but should provoke students to think beyond the text (e.g. compare to another text, apply to a relevant example, evaluate from a different perspective, reflect on the broader themes connecting the assigned readings). Submit your response and question by email to the entire class no later than 8 pm the evening before the class meeting.

40% of your course grade will be based on the quality of your engagement with the readings and class discussions. I will use the following grading scale for this portion of your grade:

A = All written responses to the readings are turned in regularly and on time and demonstrate thoughtful engagement with the authors’ ideas. Contributions to class discussions are frequent, follow the guidelines below, and cultivate a productive learning community.

B = All or most written responses are turned in, but engagement with the authors’ ideas and quality of discussion questions have room for improvement. Contributions to class discussions are generally thoughtful but could be more frequent.

C = More than a quarter of written responses are not turned in or are incomplete. Contributions to class discussions are infrequent.

D = Written responses show little engagement with the readings. Contributions to class discussions are frequently off-topic.

F = Most written responses are not turned in or are incomplete. Repeatedly makes statements that are disruptive or disrespectful of others.

White Paper

Students in this course will research and write a long-form policy paper, or “white paper” on a topic related to science, technology, or medicine. White papers are written and used by policymakers to examine a policy problem and consider an array of solutions. In some white papers, the author recommends a specific solution; in others, the author presents a number of possibilities without identifying a preferred option. In this course, a crucial part of writing a white paper will be to interrogate the process of “problematization,” asking “what is the problem represented to be?” in different contexts and by different stakeholders.

White papers are meticulously researched, providing facts to support their claims and citing the sources of those facts. The author of a white paper is expected to be an expert on the policy issue the paper addresses. We will work collaboratively throughout the semester to ensure that each student in this course is able to produce a white paper that meets these expectations. Research and writing will be done in several increments (memos), with opportunities for peer review and instructor feedback.

60% of your course grade will be based on the quality of your white paper and the writing increments that lead up to it.

The value of each assignment is:

1. Presentation of two possible topics to investigate (10% of final grade)
2. Memo 1: Policy Explainer (10% of final grade)
3. Memo 2: Policy Critique (10% of final grade)
4. Memo 3: Policy Proposal (10% of final grade)
5. Final white paper (20% of final course grade)

### Additional Academic Integrity Course Policy and Penalty Information:

Any instances of plagiarism will result in a failing grade for the assignment. Repeated instances of academic dishonesty will be grounds for failing the course. Plagiarism includes purchasing term papers; copying or handing in the writing of another student (current or former); using sentences verbatim from a published source without appropriate referencing (when in doubt, cite the source); and presenting as one’s own the detailed argument of a published source. “Recycling” papers written in other courses is also forbidden.
