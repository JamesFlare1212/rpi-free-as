## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

This course will provide an overview of the field of linguistics, the study of human language. As linguistics is a relatively diverse and interdisciplinary field (as we will see), we will cover many different topics and sub-fields in an attempt to discover and discuss the various avenues linguistics has to offer. This broad approach will introduce students to a range of language-related topics.

The exams will be non-cumulative; later exams will necessarily contain concepts included in earlier ones, but these concepts will not be tested directly. The “assignments” category consists of an assortment of activities to be turned in throughout the semester. ## Policies

### Other Course Policies:

Exams will be split into two parts - a closed note portion in class and then an open-note portion to take home. For the take home part, you may consult outside resources (e.g., textbook, lecture slides) during the exam. You still must put everything into your own words – copying directly from any source is plagiarism and you’ll get a 0 on the entire exam if you do this.

You may not discuss anything with other students during the exam day. Any communication with other students about the exam will be considered cheating. So again, consulting materials (textbook, lectures, etc.) = fine; consulting other people = cheating.

If you have technical difficulties during the exam, you must email me as soon as you possibly can and we will work together to find a solution.

Assignments will appear throughout the semester when appropriate and are listed with (tentative) dates in the schedule below.

Attendance/Participation is assigned along the following guideposts: 10 – almost always in class, frequent contributions; 9 – almost always in class, infrequent contributions; 8-7 – often/sometimes in class, frequent/infrequent contributions; 6-1 – varying levels of not really in class very often; 0 – never in class.

### Academic Policies:

- ### Academic Integrity

  Student-teacher relationships are built on trust. For example, students must trust that teachers have made appropriate decisions about the structure and content of the courses they teach, and teachers must trust that the assignments that students turn in are their own. Acts that violate this trust undermine the educational process. The Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities and The Graduate Student Supplement define various forms of Academic Dishonesty and you should make yourself familiar with these. In this class, all assignments that are turned in for a grade must represent the student’s own work.
