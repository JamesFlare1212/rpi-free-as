## Learning Outcomes

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes (CLOs):

- Students will learn the importance of proper organic laboratory experiment set-up, including pre-laboratory activities, note taking, data analysis and reporting of results.
- Students will show proficiency in handling organic laboratory separation and purification techniques including liquid-liquid extraction, recrystallization, simple/fractional/vacuum distillations, column chromatography and gas chromatography (GC).
- Students will be able to demonstrate proficient knowledge in carrying out organic reactions safely, purifying and analyzing reaction products.
- Students will be proficient in elucidating the structure of organic molecules using spectroscopic methods such as Infrared (IR) and Nuclear Magnetic Resonance (NMR).
- Students will be able to search for answers about chemistry or chemical systems in the chemical literature.

**A. Quizzes:** A brief quiz will be given at the beginning of each Discussion. Each 15–20-minute quiz will be made up of three or more questions to test your understanding of the techniques and concepts connected with the experiment that you just finished, or perhaps on interpreting related spectra. Typical source material for these brief questions will come from: (a) the assigned operations readings for the experiment, (b) the “Before You Begin” questions for the experiment, (c) the experimental description, (d) the exercises at the end of each experiment, and (e) the lecture material (including relevant information from the videos). The main purpose of this course is **NOT** to train you as a laboratory technician. Organic Chemistry is a hands-on discipline and actually seeing crystals grow, color changes, etc. and how certain chemicals react under different conditions is essential to help you develop higher order thinking skills (see Bloom’s Taxonomy) and move away from mere memorization. Integrating the topics discussed in lecture with experiential knowledge is key to developing a base upon which you will be able to formulate new chemical knowledge. To that end, the quizzes are designed to help you recognize where your **comprehension** of important concepts requires additional effort.

**The role of the laboratory notebook**

Pre-lab reports as well as the lab notes that are recorded as you do the experiment must be written in the lab notebook, which allows you to turn in a carbonless copy of your written record. If you make a mistake in your notebook, draw a single line through the mistake and continue writing. The purpose of doing these reports is to learn how to use a laboratory notebook effectively (See Appendix II of Lehman text).

The pre-lab document is where you plan precisely what chemicals (and amounts) that you need to use and how you will carry out the different steps of the experiment.

As you do the experiment, however, the lab notes that you record, document exactly what you did plus all salient observations.

The post-lab report is where you summarize and draw conclusions. **It must be typed**. Please append copies of all spectroscopic and GLC data. (See below for further details.)

Together, the combination of the pre-lab, lab notes, and post-lab write-ups correspond to the steps followed in maintaining a research laboratory notebook.  
**B.** **Pre-Laboratory Reports:** The pre-lab requires students to prepare in advance and know precisely what he/she will be doing after entering the lab. This requirement is a safety issue; a student will not be allowed to remain in the lab without submitting an acceptable pre-lab. Upon arrival at the lab, rip out copy #1 of the pre-lab and give it to a TA, who will briefly check it for completeness. **This pre-lab must be complete before the TA will unlock your equipment drawer.** An acceptable (i.e., complete) pre-lab earns you 15 out of 25 points. Further inspection may result in up to 10 points being added if there are no errors in the equation(s) and structures, or in the detailed procedure (2c, below).

1\. To provide enough details on how the experiment will be conducted, the pre-lab, on average, should require at least two pages. The following data should be incorporated:

2. a) Your name and experiment number & title followed (if appropriate) with a balanced (set of) equation(s) using correctly drawn Lewis structures, b) A complete table of reagents – including molecular weights – amounts – number of moles used, densities, mp/bp numbers, etc. follows. This information should be sufficient for a limiting reaction calculation … which reagent is used in excess (Appendix IV), c) The detailed experimental procedure that you plan to use accounts for most of this report. (See Appendix V.) Flow charts often are an excellent way to organize your plan, especially when sequential isolation or purification steps are involved. Sketches of your glassware also are useful in visualizing (organizing) steps in a synthetic sequence. Also indicate the physical techniques and spectroscopic procedures that will be used in identifying a material and determining its purity.

3. Your objective is to incorporate tersely all operational information that you will need to run the experiment: **lab texts will not be permitted in the lab**. In addition to reducing lab congestion, this policy encourages students to prepare pre-lab documents that will contain sufficient detail to do the experiment. Where appropriate, an exemplary setup of glassware or other equipment will be available for student inspection (and discussion with TAs) in the laboratory. One or more copies of the lab text will be made available during those experiments requiring identification of an unknown via comparison of physical properties or spectroscopic data with that of a list of standard compounds.

**C. Post-Laboratory Reports**

These reports are due within one week of finishing your lab. For some labs we will allocate an extra week if you (as a group) need a little more time during the next lab period for weighing a product, determining a mp, or recording a spectrum. A typical post-lab report (2-5 pages) will be typewritten. Make sure that the top of each page bears your name(s), the experiment number and title.

1. The submitted post-lab report contains the conclusions that you make. These may include:i) the percent yield or percent recovery of a product, showing all calculations (Look at Appendix IV in your textbook, ‘Calculations for Organic Synthesis’, for help). ii) identification of an unknown. Clearly show your reasoning. iii) append copies of NMR & IR spectra (fully interpreted) and of gas chromatograms (fully labeled) used to establish identity or purity.

2. We then will combine our copies of your pre-lab, lab notes, post-lab, and appended material as your final lab report. Grading rests primarily on the post-lab report plus appended data (if any), although this may be reviewed in the context of the other information present in your packet. Both your conclusions and how you arrived at them are important grading criteria, whereas the yield and purity of product will already have been graded as a separate entry, Product Yield and Purity (15%). The actual point allocation for the 25% contribution of your post-lab report necessarily will vary for each lab experiment.

3. If the writing/printing for any of your reports is illegible, they will not be evaluated. Late lab reports not supported by a written medical or other valid excuse (from a Physician, Coach, or Dean of Students) will receive a penalty of losing one point per day late. **Lab reports will not be accepted more than one week late.**

**D.** **Laboratory Technique**

This subjective portion of the grade also will be assigned in the laboratory for each experiment and recorded on separate grading sheets, one for each experiment. These grade sheets also will be used to record grades for items **A** and **B** during lab for each experiment and for **C** as soon as possible. Two aspects of laboratory technique will be assessed:

1. **Product Yield and Purity** (15%). Both quantity and quality of products will be judged. Your product should be submitted at the end of the laboratory period in a vial **legibly** labeled with your name, product name or structure, boiling point/melting point, product mass, and date. If you lost your sample or used your entire product for some purpose, you must submit a note explaining the absence of sample for some credit. **Both weight and physical properties of submitted samples will be monitored.**

2. **General Laboratory Technique** (10%). This evaluation is arrived at via discussion of all involved staff about your attention to safety issues and general cleanliness, as well as your effectiveness as a laboratory worker. This includes our observations of your composure, grasp of principles and techniques, and independence of safe operation. For example, it is permissible for you to talk with your neighbor to “compare notes/observations” and perhaps to resolve a technical problem. It is not acceptable for you simply watch and copy your neighbor’s running of the experiment. If you are unsure of what to do next or something doesn’t seem to be working, ask a neighbor, TA, or other available staff present. In general, asking questions and engaging in discussions with other students and staff is encouraged; coming unprepared is both unacceptable and unsafe. If an individual experiment does not work well in your hands, it does not mean that the experiment was poorly designed. All the experiments were tested by several teaching assistants and were found to function well, when properly performed.

### Additional Academic Integrity Course Policy and Penalty Information:

**CHEATING**

*The Rensselaer Handbook defines various forms of Academic Dishonesty and procedures for responding to them. All forms are violations of trust between students and teachers. Students should familiarize themselves with this portion of the Rensselaer Handbook and should note that the penalties for plagiarism and other forms of cheating can be quite harsh.*

For the purposes of this course, Academic Dishonesty most certainly includes” fudging” laboratory data such as weights, melting points, etc. The fabrication of scientific data and information is an egregious violation of the underpinnings of scientific inquiry and the scientific method. Students in this course also are expected to carry out all experimentation and data analysis independently, i.e., without recourse to information collected or conclusions deduced by others. This includes collecting (and interpreting) your own spectral and GLC data. In rare cases we may resort to supplying examples of this data due to time or instrumental issues.

Any student caught cheating in this course will automatically receive a grade of F for the entire course.
