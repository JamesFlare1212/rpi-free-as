## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

An introductory course on the fundamentals of radiation detection for alpha, beta, gamma and neutron radiation. It addresses the nature of each form of radiation and its interaction with matter. In addition, this course provides insight into the production of electronic signals from radiation interaction, which are processed and interpretated to quantify the initial radiation source. Key radiation detection applications investigated include, but are not limited to, health physics, reactor analysis & control, and radiation technology. This course will help you to understand – and implement – nuclear engineering workflows related to radiation detection that are needed in research and industry today.

## Course Goals

### Goals:

This second year required undergraduate course is the middle point of a sequence of understanding the different forms of radiation, their applications and how to protect against them.

- \- Nuclear Phenomena for Engineering Applications

è Introduction to Atomic & Nuclear Radiation Physics

- \- Nuclear Instrumentation & Measurements

è You’re here!

è Measurement of radiation to determine the form(s) and quantities present.

- \- Radiological Engineering

è Understanding the impacts of radiation on biological tissues

è Assessing radiation limits and implementing protection measures

From these three courses, you should be able to understand the differences between the major forms of radiation, how to measure the quantities present and what – if anything – should be done to protect biological organisms (people).

## Course Content

### Content Details:

The materials for the course will draw largely from the required textbook, covering chapters 1-11, 14 and 15. There are three core concept areas that this course will cover:

- \- Radiation Physics:
  - o Underlying physical principles associated with radiation, radioactivity, interactions with matter and the transfer of radiation energy.
- \- Radiation Detection:
  - o Methods used to quantify radiation forms present, their associated uncertainty and limits of detectability.
- \- Applications:
  - o Computational methods for detector modeling associated with specific industries such as power reactors, medical facilities and accelerators.

These three core concept areas can be broken down further into the following topics, which will tie into our lecture schedule. Additional class time will be devoted to in-class discussions and/or presentations.

1. Quantifying Radiation, Nuclear Data, and Radiation Interactions with Matter
2. Radiation Detector Operation
3. Ionization Chambers, Proportional Counters, and Geiger-Muller Counters
4. Scintillation, Photomultipliers
5. Radiation Spectroscopy, Semiconductor Detectors
6. Neutron Detectors
7. Statistical Models, Error Propagation, and Limits of Detection
8. Computational Modeling for Detector Analysis and Design

In addition to publicly available computational tools, students will be expected to develop their own computational tools for relevant homework assignments and projects.

## Learning Outcomes

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes (CLOs):

- 1\. Describe the mechanisms by which radiation interacts with matter in radiation detectors
- 2\. Explain the method of operation for each of the major classes of radiation detectors
- 3\. Calculate and interpret the uncertainty associated with radiation measurement systems
- 4\. Differentiate between radiation detector types, selecting the most appropriate detector for a given situation
- 5\. Predict the output of a given radiation detector based upon given radiation input conditions
- 6\. Design radiation detector experimental setups

### Additional Academic Integrity Course Policy and Penalty Information:

***Academic*** ***Integrity******:***

Student-teacher relationships are built on trust. For example, students must trust that teachers have made appropriate decisions about the structure and content of the courses they teach, and teachers must trust that the assignments that students turn in are their own. Acts, which violate this trust, undermine the educational process. The Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities defines various forms of Academic Dishonesty and you should make yourself familiar with these.

In this class, all assignments that are turned in for a grade must significantly represent the student’s own work unless it is specifically stated that multiple authors are permitted. In cases where help was received, or teamwork was allowed, a notation on the assignment should indicate your collaboration and, if requested or if you choose to, their individual contributions.

That said, I strongly encourage collaboration in studying and conducting assignments together to maximize your learning. At the end of each collaboration process, students must have their own copy of the deliverable in which you identified the collaboration. Prior to submission, you should review your deliverable to match your own unique style and approach to organization and communication.

Specific to this course, academic dishonesty can take the following forms:

1. Taking an exam with any assistance from another person, regardless of who they are, unless the instructor specifically states that it is a group activity (e.g., a group oral exam).
2. Using any materials (such as a crib sheet or a calculator) that you did not bring for you and you alone to use on an exam, unless given explicit permission by the proctor (e.g., typically for extraordinary circumstances such as the failure of your calculator during an exam, etc., though such assistance should not be expected or relied on).
3. Using any materials (such as a text, extra crib sheets, a laptop, a cell phone, any electronic device – wireless or otherwise, etc.) that are not explicitly permitted during an exam.
4. Interaction with a wireless device (e.g., a cell phone or tablet) during an exam (except when explicitly allowed by the proctor) will be considered an illicit data exchange and will result in a zero for the entire exam.
5. Exiting the exam room without permission, or not returning in a prompt and timely manner when given permission. “Prompt and timely” is at the discretion of the instructional team.
6. Submission of a deliverable that, except for short phrases or properly referenced material, consists of any content not created by and original to you.
7. Indicating someone as “present” (e.g., on an attendance sheet or submitting an in-class exercise) who is not present in class at the time the indication is made.
8. Collaboration is encouraged. However, assisting someone else with work where they intend to turn in the work as if it was done on their own will itself be considered a serious violation of academic honesty. Give credit for any assistance received, and have sufficient content of your own making according to the expectations of the deliverable.

The penalty for submission of an exam that is in violation of this policy is failure of the course and referral to the Dean of Students.

Submission of a deliverable that is in violation of this policy will result in a grade of zero on the assignment for the first offense. If there is a subsequent infraction the student fails the course and is referred to the Dean of Students.

If you have any questions concerning this policy before submitting a deliverable please ask for clarification.
