## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

SCRIBA ALBANIAE: Designing the William Joseph Kennedy Research Center (WJKRC)

Vertical Studies pose specific opportunities for both students and professors. Operating outside of—but supporting—the core curriculum, verticals offer glimpses into specialized research, entre into the language and philosophical path of thesis and can often lead to projects involving fabrication, public display and first-time encounters with real clients—and real sites—local and/or remote.

It is rarer still that one gets to work directly with the subject of a design studio. In this case, we will be in dialogue with one of America’s best-known writers, the Albany-born William J. Kennedy. Turning 97 on January 16, 2025, Kennedy has been a journalist in Puerto Rico as well as Albany. Stemming from his work at the Albany Times-Union, his 1969 novel, The Ink Truck, depicted life in Albany, NY around the very newsroom that he inhabited during his day job. Later, his Albany Cycle (Legs, Billy Phelan’s Greatest Game, Ironweed) would set the stage for Kennedy establishing the Writer’s Institute at the University at Albany in 1984. When Ironweed won the Pulitzer Prize AND a MacArthur Genius grant, he used the funding to set up that now 40+ year-old staple of the American literary scene. Several years ago, the SUNY Library acquired Bill’s papers, and this project imagines a standalone research center for his work.

This semester we will meet with Bill several times, at his home/studio in Averill Park and here at the SoA. We will screen two films: The Cotton Club (1984, screenplay by Kennedy and Francis Coppola; directed by Coppola) and Ironweed (1987, screenplay by Kennedy and directed by Hector Babenco). In addition we will read “Billy Phelan’s Greatest Game (1978) and annotate it as a class, looking for insights into what must be called, “Kennedy’s Albany.” That is, we will explore various locations mentioned in his writings to determine a location for the WJKRC.

- 2d. ability to design with ethical reasoning– students will learn to make informed decisions that materially, culturally and environmentally support a sustained presence for their center within the region. Examples include a preference for locally sourced materials with an eye toward sustainability and providing local employment. Students will likewise imagine hypothetical daily, weekly, monthly and yearly programming schedules that will make the center relevant to the largest number of users.
- 2e. ability to design with qualitative fluency – students will learn to make decisions by evaluating the cost-effectiveness of specific constructions strategies, maximizing the effectiveness of multiple programs per space and designing those with ‘right-sized’ square footages.
- 2f. ability to design with communicative fluency– students will learn to design for multiple constituents thereby maximizing the accessibility for the project, despite the singularity of the subject matter of an archive/library/residency for a specific subject. As “the poet of Albany,” Bill’s work is very inclusive of all peoples, all economic classes and diverse audiences. Important questions to answer are: how does this building represent YOU (the people of Albany and its neighborhood in particular); how does it reflect the historical periods (pre Civil-War to the Cold War) that his stories reflect, without being nostalgic;
- OUTCOME 3 Collaboration 3a. ability to collaborate – students will demonstrate the ability to work in 2-3 person teams on both research and design phases. Students will demonstrate the ability to develop a strategies for group work including knowledge and information sharing, workflow, project management etc.) that recognizes different group members’ strengths, documents the strategy so that others may understand it, implements the strategy and communicates and evaluates the results, and modifies the strategies as needed.
- 3b. ability to collaborate with the subject of the study – the students will demonstrate the ability to work effectively with our subject, writer Bill Kennedy. Given his health and schedule limitations, students will have to create efficient questions for Bill and communicate clearly and efficiently. We may have as little as four hours in total to ask questions and get his feedback on the project. The group will have to collaborate on questions that do not acquire duplicate information or feedback. How can we maximize efficient without compromising the richness or poetry of the dialogue?
- OUTCOME 4: Civic and Global Engagement 4a. ability to engage civic realm – students will demonstrate the ability to engage issues of public interest/concerns, specifically the rich yet often contentious history of the specific ethnic, social and religious groups that comprise Albany, NY. As a former journalist, Bill strived to report on the American Experiment through daily contact with a diverse group of people leading into the turbulent 1960s, arguably the era of the greatest social changes in American history, in terms of mores, laws and legislation. Students will develop these reporter-like skills via dialogues with Bill, interviewing local residents and reading additional texts related to Albany’s diverse urban (and formerly rural) populations.
- 4b. ability to engage the global realm – students will demonstrate the ability to engage complex global Issues via the mission of the center. As Bill is heralded as a regional American writer with global impact and audience, he is also seen as a writer grappling with some of the most persistent and troubling issues of this or any era: crime, ethnic violence, misogyny, class disparity, low employment and substance abuse. How can these students’ design efforts on furthering Bill’s work better resonate locally and globally? It will, naturally, serve some of the same audiences as does its sister organization, The Writers Institute. It will also cater to writer-in-residence from the world’s diverse and numerous literary traditions.

1\. Precedent Analysis:

This will involve the careful study of historically significant examples of the intersection of text and architecture, standalone developments (worldwide) of written language, archives, libraries and scholarly residencies. The analysis will include both graphic and written elements and will serve to inform future design decisions in the studio.

2\. Site Questing and Analysis:

A main site for the William Joseph Kennedy Research Center in Albany, NY, will be identified via the collective investigations of the class, based on locations proximate to setting in his novels and non-fiction writing. This analysis will include a careful study of the existing qualities and features of the location: nearby resources, suitable (buildable terrain), relationship to Kennedy narratives (historical and fictional) and potential for success within the urban fabric of the state’s capital city. This will include an understanding of the dimensional, material, spatial, and programmatic aspects—and potential viability—of the researched sites.

3\. Accumulation and Documentation of Historical Content:

Bill Kennedy’s books, papers, historical artifacts, memorabilia and awards will be studied and documented (as their conditions allow) during crucial stages of research.

4\. Three-Dimensional Imagery:

We will rely upon the efficiency and effectiveness of three-dimensional images such as axonometric drawings and perspectival renderings to clearly covey and express spatial and curatorial ideas.

5\. Project Manifesto:

Writing and other forms of language will allow each student to engage Bill’s writing to shape the conceptual framework of the project. Select readings will also be made available to inform and stimulate a critical lens around the questions of exhibition, public presentation, academic scholarship and the research/display logics of the contemporary archive.

6\. Physical Models:

Study models and final physical models will be employed to capture the volumetric, material, and scalar dimensions of design proposals. Study models should be built to so that they enable possibilities of play, transformation, and discovery.

7\. Preliminary Design Proposals:

A crucial part of the design process will involve the testing of multiple design proposal and strategies to arrive at an ideal proposition. This process will involve quick and extended studies towards the final.

8\. Final Design Proposal:

The final design proposal of the William Joseph Kennedy Research Center will serve as a conceptual signpost towards the possible realization of the facility. There is already some interest on the part of regional and cultural leaders toward these student projects inspiring potential investment. Regardless of that interesting possibility, each student proposal must convey a detailed and concentrated understanding of both the conceptual aspects of the project as well as the construction methods, basic engineering and use of regional materials

## Policies

### Attendance Policy:

STUDIO POLICY:

Pin-ups

Periodically students will be required to present their work publicly in a formal review process. These presentations are a critical part of the architectural education. It is an opportunity for the student and the faculty to judge the project and critically evaluate the process of design. It may be appropriate at points in the semester to evaluate only selected projects to convey information relevant to the entire class.

Desk Critiques

The design studio environment involves two-way communication. The role of the studio critic is to evolve and strengthen the student’s design process. Every time the student meets with the faculty, he or she must have new iterations of work based on previous conversations. If the student has not produced new work, the faculty reserves the right to skip the critique and continue with other students who have produced new material.

SoA Lectures

Attendance is MANDATORY at all School of Architecture’s lectures regardless of whether lectures occur in-person or on-line.

Attendance

Attendance is required at all class meetings during the semester. Two (2) unexcused absences from class will result in the lowering of the grade by one (1) letter. Four (4) unexcused absences from class during the term result in an automatic F. Whether an absence (health, immediate family death, officially approved field / sport trip, etc.) is excused or unexcused is determined by the Students Experience Office (4th Floor Academy Hall, x8022, se@rpi.edu), upon presentation of required documentation, according to established policies and procedures (see http:// http://se.rpi.edu/policies/ea/).

Students are required to work in studio during studio hours and have the required materials and basic tools to develop the assignments. Work includes participation in site visits, design and development, pinups, presentations, discussions, and field trips. Failure to work in class with undivided attention and disruptive behavior (tardiness, leaving early, lack of participation, general socializing, etc.), as well working on assignments from other classes during class, will count as absences.
