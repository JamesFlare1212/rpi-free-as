**Overview**

A semester long course in viewing: how to be an active viewer, a rested viewer, a curious viewer, a critical viewer. A viewer willing to be immersed, overwhelmed, and alert. A reader of cinema. A course for architects and makers who are eager to connect to cinema as a driving force behind modernity and contemporary architecture: as an engine of spatial ideas, time and place. This course utilizes frequent viewing of the cinema of Alfred Hitchcock (and related artists) in a big screen setting, with relevant readings and discussions, at least one field trip and projects in writing, storyboarding and film analysis.

**Acknowledgement/Honoring**

This semester sees a change in title for the course, the first time in 15 years. In honor of my former HASS colleague, Professor Pauline Oliveros, the course now has the prefix “Deep Viewing”. Oliveros (1932-2016) was a pioneering American composer accordionist and electronic musician who founded the Center for Deep Listening (CDL) in Kingston, NY and taught her Deep Listening course here at the Institute. **Course Outline**

It may seem ironic to invoke processes of meditation in order to study the cinema of one who has been branded (including by himself) as the Master of Suspense. It seems a complete contradiction. And yet when asked about his idea of perfect happiness, Hitchcock replied “Open skies. Not a problem on the horizon. I’m free to work on my creative process.” In the spirit of Hitchcock, Oliveros and Lynch (a practitioner of Transcendental Meditation) to create extraordinary experiences for their audiences, we too will learn from their traditions, policies and innovations. Building audience is a matter of patience, practice and sacrifice. As Hitchcock once said, “Style is self-plagiarism.”

**On Cinema**

What does it mean to participate in cinema in 2025? We are now deeply entrenched in the era of the small screen. But for a medium, industry and art that began small (Edison Videolas, private viewing booths and small-scale projections) cinema quickly became a group effort, both in the making and the viewing. As vaudeville theaters converted to projected cinema, screens grew, formats increased—and changed proportions—and the experience of the medium became about being in a group, in a grand space, alone in a crowd. In other words, there was a social aspect of watching moving pictures, aided and abetted by architecture.

Carrying certain traditions over from theater, organists accompanied silent films, as did small musical groups and the occasional orchestra. Acoustics for cinemas advanced quickly when Al Jolson’s “the Jazz Singer” was introduced in 1928. These advances in acoustics accompanied newly proportioned formats like VISTAVISON (between 1.66 and 2.00:1) CINERAMA (from 2.59:1 and 2.65:1) and later IMAX. To see a film with a periphery-filling screen and an advanced sound system (*Dolby* was introduced in 196*5, THX* in 1983, *DTS* in 1990) makes for a sensory overload; IMAX puts you in the eyepiece of the DP.

Media has shrunk to smaller and smaller screens, a more personalized, portable experience. Cinema still means social encounter, not only with the content but with the possibility of post-screening dialogue. Of course, there are countless on-line fora for film, but the experience of the big screen means literally seeing MORE. This semester we will try to understand why architecture is so essential to the experience, and why so many directors (and especially Hitchcock) value the role of architecture as something essential to the human story, be it comedy, tragedy or drama.

**Why Architecture? Why Hitchcock?**

In this course I argue for Hitchcock’s cinema as a sophisticated and inclusive architectural practice.

This seminar will explore the art of Alfred Hitchcock via the urban condition, modes of architecture and technical advances that make for his, the most *architectural* of cinematic practices. The narrative structures of Hitchcock’s films often move the characters from pastoral settings to urban contexts, and vice versa. These allegories track naive or innocent characters as they move into self-awareness, a transition always reflected in the costumes, music, lighting, editing and direction. Famously averse to shooting on location, Hitchcock invented and refined techniques for controlling shifts in scale, perspective and space—all part of his reliance on the studio for a kind of ‘world building’. For example, as a way to save on location costs, Hitchcock developed backlit film transparencies at the scale of architecture.

His techniques of sonic and visual abstraction, defamiliarization, continuous takes, color saturation and disorienting perspectives all have analogs in the operations of the modern city. His themes of voyeurism, doubling, mistaken identity and paranoia are hallmarks of the modern human condition. He made the first film to address psychoanalysis as a subject (*Spellbound,* 1945), shot four “single set” films (*Lifeboat,* 1944; *Rope,* 1945; *Dial M for Murder,* 1950; and *Rear Window,* 1956), and his dark comedy *Frenzy* (1972) looked at the urban phenomenon of serial murder. From the *39 Steps* to *I Confess* to *Psycho,* Hitchcock torqued the city grid as a symbol for both freedom (anonymity) and oppression (chaos).

The Master of Suspense has also been seen as a misogynist, sadist, humorist and cultural critic. We will critically engage his works via screenings, writing and our own attempts at storyboarding and set design.

In *The Poetics of Space (*1957) Gaston Bachelard asserts that “if one would ask us what is the most precious benefaction of the house, we would say: the house accommodates dreaming, the house protects the dreamer, the house allows us to dream in peace”. The claim can easily be made that the primary role of housing is safety – including protection from the elements, but also as an affront, literally, to society. It is an emblem of the culture but can also be seen as being apart from it. This is Hitchcock’s main premise: that the most sinister thing is not a far off, foreign threat, but is actually right next door.

## Policies

### Attendance Policy:

**Participation** It is expected that all students will participate in a productive and meaningful way. This includes being on time for class sessions, having completed all assignments in a timely manner, being actively engaged in group discussions, presentations and crits. There is ample evidence in my 40 years of teaching that a class can excel or falter depending on the overall group dynamic. With at least one project in dialogue with a classmate, it is self-evident that good, creative and clear communication is essential.

**Attendance** Attendance is essential to the successful completion of the course and will be taken at each class. The primary source of the course material is class sessions, and each student is responsible for all of the material presented, discussed and reviewed in class. As life insists, there are occasional challenges to our schedules, and absences will evaluated on an individual basis, providing the student makes up the required viewing and the work that was due on the date of the absence. This may mean a discussion of the content of the work with professor and fellow students.

### Additional Academic Integrity Course Policy and Penalty Information:

As a member of the SoA Artificial Intelligence Subcommittee, we are currently in the process of examining the reasonable use of A.I. in conducting research and the subsequent use of A.I. in creating scholarly papers and design strategies. The policy of this instructor is to pursue penalties for outright plagiarism, that is, "old-fashioned" plagiarism, and to allow for the acknowledged use of A.I.in generating new insights into cinema, particularly that of Alfred Hitchcock. Each aper that utilized A.I. will be subject to full citations of the prompts used and a critical analysis of content. What is accurate? What is false? What has been invented?  
A grade of 0 (zero) will be given (for that assignment) for the first violation of the plagiarism policy. The student will receive a grade of F for subsequent violations. However, the use of A.I. as a research/writing tool is allowed, if acknowledge within the paper, and does not constitute the bulk of the intellectual content.  
Group work is encouraged in at least one assignment and the students will qualitatively and qualitatively assess each others' participation.
