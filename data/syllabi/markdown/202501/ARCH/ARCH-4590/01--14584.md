New Venture Progression Using Design Thinking

# Overview

As an entrepreneur, your job is to bring ideas to life by solving real problems for real people. To do that well, you need to deeply understand the needs of your users and then tailor your skills and work to those needs. This experiential course will introduce you to design thinking (DT). DT is a structured process for designing innovative solutions to ambiguous problems in ways that really meet people's needs. Design thinking can be broken down into four stages that are meant to be part of a rapid cycle of learning and experimenting:

1. Empathize with the user
2. Define the problem
3. Ideate possible solutions
4. Prototype solutions with quick experiments

Entrepreneurs need to not only make sure their solution solves a real problem for real people, but also that it has a viable business model (aka makes money). People need to care enough about the problem that they are willing to pay for your solution, and your solution needs cost you less than they are willing to pay. Entrepreneurs use a business model canvas to strategize about the ways they create and deliver value to their users. The design thinking process can be applied to solve problems relating to each part of the business model canvas.

You'll work with a small "founder" team to apply design thinking and entrepreneurial skills to design innovative solutions to real problems. Each week your team will work on one of the stages of design thinking to make progress toward your solution. As real entrepreneurs you'll have to think critically about what you are learning and whether you are ready for the next step or need to reassess your assumptions and repeat an earlier step. Ready? Let's go!

# Course structure

You are fearless explorers in the open-ended world of entrepreneurship. There is no clear path forward to success. But there are tools and processes that can help you along the way. We want to model the real experience and have some fun together along the way. That's why we built our course around the New Venture Game Board (detailed instructions below). You'll progress back and forth along the game board depending on what you learn through your research and experiments. Each week you'll present your progress to another team and get their feedback. We'll use class time to share examples, bring in guest speakers, and give you project time with your team.

The weekly journal:

Each week, your team needs to use this template to write/draw what you did for each step you carried out, why you moved from any step to any other step, and how you’re each feeling. For this last part, remember that your goal is to feel like an intrepid hero, exploring the unknown with pride, courage, and good humor. It should be fun and funny. If not, that’s ok. There’s nothing wrong with you; just think about what’s missing and what actions you can take yourself to fill the gaps.

The weekly review:

Each week, your team will put on a show for another team. When you put on your show, you want it to be engaging and comprehensible. They should like it, want to follow, and understand what you’re trying to communicate when they do follow. Interactive is good. Your content for your show is your weekly journal.

They’ll assess what you did and assign scores based on adherence to the guidelines below. To score well, communicate clearly to them what progress you made (in any directions) and how you followed the below guidelines in order to make them. In addition, show them that you did solid work. For example, accomplishing a single quick step implies that not a lot of work was accomplished, and accomplishing the entire method in one week might cause incredulity about the depth of work.

Recommended timing: (~20 min)

1. Team A presents (3 min)
2. Team B provides feedback to Team A / discussion (5 min)
3. Team B presents (3 min)
4. Team A provides feedback to Team B / discussion (5 min)
5. Each team enters scores for the other team (2 min)

# New Venture Game Board

Each week your team needs to complete at least one of the steps in the New Venture Game Board. Details about each step are found below along with criteria to help you decide whether you are ready for the next step or whether you need to circle back to a previous step. There is absolutely no shame in going through steps more than once, in fact this is an expected part of the journey. Your team should end on a different part of the game board than the week before. Every week you will need to share what you did and what you learned in your weekly journal. You must complete all the steps before the last day of class. There is no due date for the individual steps, but your team must do the activities on each part of the game board at least once. Some steps should be very quick, so we recommend pairing these with a more substantial step in the same week.

- Select Vertical (Quick)

From here, there’s nowhere to go but the next step. What aspects of the human experience are interesting to focus on? Here's a short and list of examples:

- Living spaces
- Entertainment
- Dating
- Sleeping
- Working out
- Learning
- Health
- Food
- Transportation
- Housing
- Safety

This is your choice. An ideal vertical is something general enough to explore so you can make discoveries, but not so general that you don’t know where to start exploring. “Happiness” is too general, and “Standup comedy for freshmen in Dorm 2 on Wednesdays” is too specific.

Do you have reason to believe that people are suffering from problems in this area? State your assumptions about who interact with this vertical, what problems they’re facing, and what are the causes of those problems.

Gut check: do you have access to these people so that you can explore? If you don’t, you’ll have trouble with part B and beyond!

Other ways to find problem ideas to explore:

- Look at best idea lists and riff on ideas. For example, if you see a cardboard bike, you could brainstorm about what other unexpected items might be made out of cardboard and what problems/needs that might solve. Don't get too attached to the idea. Use it as a springboard to get fresh thinking about problems/needs you might want to explore.
- Without thinking of a vertical, just do a big problem brainstorm. Focus on quantity over quality. Try to quickly list 20 common problems faced by you or by individuals who you can fairly easily talk with. Then pick your favorite three to discuss with your team
- Quote from Einstein: “If I had an hour to solve a problem I’d spend 55 minutes thinking about the problem and 5 minutes thinking about solutions.”

Journal entry:

- Create or update the slide about your vertical and the problems you brainstormed

- Empathize (Hard!)

Here are some activities to build empathy In order of top down to bottom up:

- Market Research
- Written User Records
- Surveys
- Observations
- Interviews
- “Walk a Mile in Their Shoes”

Remember, when it comes to utility for the design thinker, bottom up beats top down every time. Top down matters when you’re thinking about scale. Entrepreneurs wanting to build large successful businesses will need to consider both. In this course, bottom up is more important.

Your goal is to discover problems and get at the heart of who in particular is experiencing them and what are the main causes. This is the most nebulous part of the process. Good luck!

You’re ready for part C when:

- You have character personas/archetypes for the different folks in the vertical. What are the attributes that matter, intrinsic (who are they [“teenager”]) and extrinsic (how are they interacting with the vertical [“skateboarding”])? Use the attributes as dividing lines so you can form groups. Draw your caricatures, with each being right down the middle of that group.
- You have strong hypotheses about what they think and feel based on what they do and say. If you had assumptions, you tested them. Think: did you miss any assumptions?
- You’ve discovered passion in more than one place (you notice that they quit something in frustration, they’re leading the conversation and they won’t shut up about X, you try something while pretending to be them and it’s a really bad experience, etc).

You should go back to part A if any of the above are true:

- You don’t have access to people in this vertical.
- No one seems to have any feelings about this vertical.
- An entire week has gone by and you aren’t able to move to part C.

Journal entry:

Hypotheses tested:

- What are some likely problems users in your chosen vertical may have?
- How do you think you can best access these users and empathize with their needs?

Actions taking:

- Who did you talk to? What questions did you ask (sentence starters)?
- Did you observe users? Did you try to “walk a day in their shoes”?
- What else did you do to build empathy? Some ideas here.

New insights:

- What did you learn through your empathy activities? Any surprises?

- Narrow (Quick)

Here, you start to define problems from your expansive empathy research in step B. Each problem is a potential focus for your new venture. For each problem, assign the caricature archetypes (they may be experiencing more than one). How strongly does each feel the problem (0-5)? Are they aware of the problem in a way that makes them receptive to trying out solutions (0-5)?

For each problem, assign an overall score by multiplying the two component scores.

You’re ready for part D if you have a score higher than 18.

If you don’t have a score higher than 80, nothing is wrong with you. Go back to part B or part A, depending on whether you think you haven’t been able to define a compelling problem yet (part B) or whether you haven’t found a promising vertical yet (part A).

Journal entry:

Hypotheses tested:

- Which problem statements did you consider?

Actions taking:

- What problem statement did you ultimately decide on?

New insights:

- What insights from your empathy activities were most relevant in deciding which problem to focus on?

- Point of View (Medium)

You need to go back through your research from part B and organize the related parts into a visual map with each part pointing towards one or more of the blanks in the below sentence. How you organize it and make it visual is up to you. Feel free to process and transform the research however you like to help your synthesis. Then, fill in the parts of speech with specific words in this sentence that are most appropriate from the research:

“An (Adj) (Adj) (Who) needs a way to (Action) because (Insight) and (Insight).”

- Adjectives are single words, not phrases. Be very deliberate. You can add a third if you really need to.
- A ‘Who’ is not necessarily a single word. It could be a noun phrase like “guy behind the counter on the night shift.”
- An ‘Action’ is a verb or verb phrase. Be careful here! You want to avoid being prescriptive. “Lock her skateboard up outside the building” implies a specific solution that might not work, whereas “feel secure that her last mile mean of transportation will be there for her” is appropriately general about the solution. The reason you want to stay appropriately general is you will be able to more freely brainstorm solutions and have better odds of coming up with something really innovative. Think: how can you lean into how the person wants to feel, here?
- Insights are important revelations from your research, like maybe “she feels annoyed and belittled that she’s not allowed to bring her skateboard into the office” and/or maybe “she takes pride in embracing the rebellious efficiency of non-traditional transportation devices like a skateboard”.

Are you making untested assumptions here? If you aren’t confident, point out where in the Point of View statement your visual supporting evidence feels weak. You may need to go back to a previous step in order to fix any problems. There’s nothing wrong with that. Remember, you are an explorer bravely venturing into a new frontier. There’s no way to know until you explore. Deliberation before evidence is procrastination!

If you do feel confident about your POV, move on to step E.

Journal entry:

Hypotheses tested:

- Which point of view statements did you consider?

Actions taking:

- What point of view statement did you ultimately decide on?

New insights:

- What insights from your empathy activities were most relevant in deciding which point of view statement to focus on?

- Ideate (Very Quick)

Linus Pauling said: “If you want a good idea, start with a lot of ideas”. Here’s where you get a lot of sacrificial ideas so that, in the next step, you can get a good idea. In order for a really good idea to float to the top, you need a lot of ideas.

Use your POV statement from step D as your guide. It is your tether. You should be able to feel every part of that sentence without having to read it. You should understand it intuitively. Set a timer for five minutes (or 10 if you really need it).

In the time you set, come up with as many solution ideas as possible. Go for quantity instead of quality. Don’t think critically at all about any of the ideas during this exercise. That will come later. Instead, focus on being as creative as possible. The only boundaries to put on your ideas are to make sure that each suggestion is a new independent idea and that all of them address your POV statement. If any don’t meet these criteria, just keep going and move quickly.

When you come up with an idea, state it outloud in a brief headline so it can inspire your teammates. Write down the 1-3 key words or draw a quick sketch and then move on.

Don’t get attached to your ideas.

If you finish your ideation exercise and realize that you have solutions that are addressing more than one problem, that might be a sign that it’s worth going back to revise your POV. There’s a difference between multiple solution categories (encouraged) and multiple problems to solve (discouraged). It’s also perfectly fine to have multiple considerations in a problem: “we need to protect the groceries from spilling and protect the safety of the elderly person carrying them”.

Journal entry: \*Combine Steps E & F together\*

Hypotheses tested:

- Put your refined point of view statement here

Actions taking:

- What are some of the top ideas the emerged from your ideation session? Link to an image, doc, or other artifact that captures your team ideation process.

New insights:

- What top idea(s) do you plan to test and what are your initial ideas for how you could test them quickly?

- Narrow (Quickish)

Spend 5 minutes and group the ideas based on the first categories that come to mind. For example, “ideas having to do with the color blue”, “ideas involving standing up”, etc.

Then, each teammate places three votes on categories and can vote on the same idea more than once. Try to place votes based on the likelihood of success, the easiness to prototype, and how engaging the category is to you. The category with the most votes wins. If there’s a tie, either issue each teammate one more vote or else start the exercise over and choose different categories.

Once you have a winning category, it’s time to pick a winning idea. Each teammate gets 5 votes. Try to place votes based on the likelihood of success, the easiness to prototype, and how engaging the idea is to you. The idea with the most votes wins. If there’s a tie, either issue each teammate one more vote or else start the exercise over and choose different categories. Before moving on to step G, each teammate should write their assumptions regarding the likelihood of success of the idea and the easiness to try the idea, and then write down why the idea is as engaging as it is (it might not be). Remember: You don’t do this so that you can position yourself to be right in the future; you do this because you’re a humble scientist and you want to get better at understanding your own assumptions in this problem space.

There’s nowhere to go but forward from here.

Journal entry: \*Combine Steps E & F together\*

Hypotheses tested:

- Put your refined point of view statement here

Actions taking:

- What are some of the top ideas the emerged from your ideation session? Link to an image, doc, or other artifact that captures your team ideation process.

New insights:

- What top idea(s) do you plan to test and what are your initial ideas for how you could test them quickly?

- Prototype/Test (Kinda Tough!)

Create a prototype that you can try out on your users. Don’t spend a lot of time. It will fail. Get it “into their hands” (or equivalent) as fast as possible and let them misunderstand it, misuse it, and break it. Treat it like a lab experiment. What did you learn?

Write how it went the first time around, including what was surprising and educating to you. Now try again applying remedies for lessons learned.

Continue in this way. As you progress through rounds of prototyping/testing, you can start to think and write more: what do you predict is the most likely thing to fail? What will you try to do to stop that in your next prototype? Then, once they’ve tried it out, note down whether you were right or wrong and what you learned. Remember: You do this because you’re a humble scientist and you want to get better at understanding your own assumptions, especially in this solution space.

Once you have something your target users understand and want, collect evidence showing that and move on to the next step.

If a whole week goes by and you aren’t seeing compelling evidence, you should move back to a previous step. How far do you move back? That’s up to you. If you missed something big, you might need to move further back. That’s less likely.

Journal entry:

Hypotheses tested:

- Create one or more hypotheses about the solution idea(s) you want to test. What assumption are you making? What must be true for your solution to really work in addressing your users’ needs? How might you try to disprove these hypotheses (helps you avoid confirmation bias).

Actions taking:

- What did you do to test your hypotheses? What experiments did you run?

New insights:

- What data did you collect from your experiments? Did you validate or disprove your hypotheses? What are the next most critical hypotheses to test? You may end up repeating this stage several times or realize that you need to go back to a previous step. How did you decide what to do next?

- Lean Business Model Canvas (Kinda Tough!)

Congratulations! You’re out the end of the design thinking method. Now, it’s time to try and make a business. Businesses are hard, and they mostly fail. That’s true even if they’re founded on successful design thinking endeavors. The big jump here is that you not only need to solve the problem; you need to demonstrate that you can make money doing so. This Lean Business Model Canvas helps you think about the different interconnected parts of your business and how they tie together as an engine that makes more money than it spends.

Lean Business Model Canvas Template

Lots of examples for well-filled ones in the link, and lots of short explanations on Youtube for how to fill.

If you can stitch together something in this LBMC that makes a lot of sense to you, to your users, and to your weekly reviewers, you’re ready to make a formal pitch.

Journal entry:

Add a link to your team’s business model canvas to the title slide of your weekly journal.

Hypotheses tested:

- What are the biggest assumptions you are making in your business model canvas? If you turned this into a business, where do you think you are most likely to fail?

Actions taking:

- What could you do to de-risk your biggest assumptions made in the business model canvas? What research / experiments could you do?

New insights:

- What new ideas came out of creating the business model canvas with your team?

- Pitch

Time to put on an epic show. Be creative! Are you going to have role-plays along with your slides? Who’s going to say what? Who will answer what types of questions from the audience following your pitch? In the pitch itself, you want to cover:

- What is the problem that you’re solving, and why is it so important to those who experience it?
- What is your solution, and why do the people it’s made for want it so much?
- Pretending you have 100% full time availability to work on this new venture, what are you going to do to make it work? Reference your LBMC.
- Why do you need money, and how much? Pretend your audience is solely composed of people with money. Why should they give it to you instead of to someone else? How do you convince them with all the evidence of your efforts that your venture is going to be successful?

Is this falling flat? When you try putting on your show for others, do they like it? If not, consider whether you need to go back to part H or before.

Journal entry:

- Add a link to your team’s final presentation slides and pitch video to the title slide of your weekly journal.

# ACADEMIC INTEGRITY

Intellectual integrity and credibility are the foundation of all academic work. A violation of Academic Integrity policy is, by definition, considered a flagrant offense to the educational process. It is taken seriously by students, faculty, and Rensselaer and will be addressed in an effective manner.

If found responsible for committing academic dishonesty, a student may be subject to one or both types of penalties: an academic (grade) penalty administered by the professor and/or disciplinary action through the Rensselaer judicial process described in this handbook.

Academic dishonesty is a violation of the Grounds for Disciplinary Action as described in this handbook. A student may be subject to any of the following types of disciplinary action should disciplinary action be pursued by the instructor: disciplinary warning; disciplinary probation; disciplinary suspension, expulsion and/or alternative actions as agreed on by the student and hearing officer. It should be noted that no student who allegedly commits academic dishonesty will be able to drop or change the grade option for the course in question and is not eligible to request an F examination for the course.

The definitions and examples presented below are a sampling of types of academic dishonesty and are not to be construed as an exhaustive or exclusive list. The academic integrity policy applies to all students, undergraduate and graduate, and to scholarly pursuits and research. Additionally, attempts to commit academic dishonesty or to assist in the commission or attempt of such an act are also violations of this policy.

Academic Fraud

Academic Fraud is defined as the alteration of documentation relating to the grading process; for example, changing exam solutions to negotiate for a higher grade or tampering with an instructor's grade book.

Collaboration

Collaboration is defined as deliberately facilitating an act of academic dishonesty in any way or form; for example, allowing another student to observe an exam paper or allowing another student to "recycle" one's old term paper or using one another's work in a paper or lab report without citing it as another's work.

Copying

Copying is defined as obtaining information pertaining to a graded exercise by deliberately observing the paper of another student; for example, noting which alternative a neighboring student has circled on a multiple-choice exam.

Cribbing

Cribbing is defined as the use or attempted use of prohibited materials, information, or study aids in an academic exercise. Examples include using an unauthorized formal sheet during an exam.

Fabrication

Fabrication is defined as the unauthorized falsification or invention of any information in an academic exercise. Examples include the use of "bought" or "ready-made" term papers, or falsifying lab records or reports.

Plagiarism

Plagiarism is defined as representing the work or words of another as one's own through the omission of acknowledgment or reference. Examples include using sentences verbatim from a published source in a term paper without appropriate referencing, or presenting as one's own the detailed argument of a published source, or presenting as one's own electronically or digitally enhanced graphic representations from any form of media.

Sabotage

Sabotage is defined as the destruction of another student's work. Examples include destroying a model, lab experiment, computer program, or term paper developed by another student.

Substitution

Substitution is defined as utilizing a proxy, or acting as a proxy, in any academic exercise. Examples include taking an exam for another student or having a homework assignment done by someone else.

## Learning Outcomes

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes (CLOs):

- Outcome 1: BUSINESS OPPORTUNITIES: Students will discover and define business opportunities via a user-centered approach. (a) Utilize tools and frameworks to empathize with users and identify customer needs in a market to discover ""customer insights."" (b) Deploy the basic tools of primary research including user interviews questions, surveys and ethnographic studies. (c) Concisely define a user persona and problem statement. Assessment Measure: Weekly journal documenting business hypotheses, experiments, and learnings; weekly presentations of business progress; midterm and final pitch presentations.
- Outcome 2: PRODUCT DEVELOPMENT: Students will design, test, and refine products/services to address an ambiguous problem or opportunity. (a) Lead and participate in ideation exercises to generate a wide set of options with a team. (b) Design and implement prototypes to test key risks and assumptions for an entrepreneurial venture. (c) Apply the practice of iteration within each step of the design thinking process and across the steps. Assessment Measure: Weekly journal documenting business hypotheses, experiments, and learnings; weekly presentations of business progress; midterm and final pitch presentations.
- Outcome 3: BUSINESS MODELS: Students will create and evaluate sustainable business models. (a) Apply the Lean Business Canvas to analyze business ideas/opportunities to identify key assumptions within a proposed business and articulate the path to verifying assumptions. (b) Know the basic meaning of Feasibility, Viability and Desirability for Entrepreneurs and the relationship among these three things. (c) Evaluate the impact of revenue streams and cost drivers to overall business profitability. Assessment Measure: Business model canvas, weekly journal documenting business hypotheses, experiments, and learnings; weekly presentations of business progress; midterm and final pitch presentations.
- Outcome 4: BUSINESS COMMUNICATION: Students will communicate business opportunities to motivate investors, employees, and customers. (a) Apply business marketing basics to create a customer acquisition strategy. (b) Effectively present your entrepreneurial process and pitch your venture concept in a compelling way. Assessment Measure: Business pitch presentations, evidence of customer acquisition strategy in weekly journals.
- Outcome 5: TEAM DYNAMICS: Students will contribute to successful startup team dynamics. Assessment Measure: Teammate rating and reflection

## Grading Criteria

### Criteria Details:

Per RPI policy, students must complete the self-assessment survey in order to receive a grade

Your team needs to end every week on a different part of the game board than the week before. Your team must do the activities on each part of the game board at least once.

45% of your grade is from your weekly reviewers, with each review weighted based on the average review score of the reviewing team. Each member of the reviewing team will give your team a score of 1-10 in the following areas:

1. Hypotheses: Clear hypotheses that address the most important risks and assumptions of the venture.
2. Experiments: Experiments are thoughtfully designed, address the hypotheses, and demonstrate creativity and effort.
3. Insights: The team extracted interesting learnings from their experiments that drive their presentation forward.
4. Presentation: The presentation was clear, interesting, and engaging.

5% of your grade is from your midterm team presentation, and 25% of your grade is from your final team presentation. Both cover the following:

1. Clarity of your story (1-5)
2. Insights (1-5)
3. Creativity (1-5)
4. Adherence to 8 min goal (1-5)

Problem. The problem you've discovered and how you've proven a market cares enough to want a solution

1. Who (1-5)
2. Why (1-5)
3. When (1-5)
4. Where (1-5)
5. How much does it cost (1-5)

Solution. Your proposed solution to the problem and how you've proven it could satisfy the target market

1. Are the users and other stakeholders aware of the problem? (1-5)
2. Do they care? (1-5)
3. Are they actively seeking a solution? (1-5)

Traction. What traction you've had already and what that shows about future growth. Think product-market fit, not necessarily revenue.

1. How compelling is the evidence that the solution solves the problem? (1-5)
2. What is the TAM (total addressable market)? (1-5)
3. How will the market be accessed? (1-5)
4. What is the expected effectiveness? (1-5)
5. Any special experience in the market or special access to it? (y/n)

Competition. What unfair advantages / defensibility your startup can boast over competitors.

1. How focused is the startup solution on a particular niche market? (1-5)
2. How hard would it be for someone else to enter the market and compete or for existing powers in the market to adopt this approach? (1-5)
3. How quantifiable is this evidence? (1-5)

Ask. What are you asking for?

1. Valuation and its derivation? (1-5)
2. Use of capital? (1-5)

Few words and good visuals on slides? (y/n)

Good representation from across the team? (y/n)

1. Sharing your journey: What steps did you follow?
2. Startup pitch

15% of your grade is from your individual participation

1. Did you speak up in class?
2. Did you submit reviews of another team every week?
3. Did you complete your teammate ratings at the end of the course?

10% of your grade is from teammate ratings

1. At the end of the course and at least once in the middle of the course, each team member will rate each teammate using this question: "This teammate did their fair share to help our team be successful." More important than the grade, group work can be one of the best opportunities to make lasting personal and professional relationships with your peers.

### Additional Academic Integrity Course Policy and Penalty Information:

Intellectual integrity and credibility are the foundation of all academic work. A violation of Academic Integrity policy is, by definition, considered a flagrant offense to the educational process. It is taken seriously by students, faculty, and Rensselaer and will be addressed in an effective manner.

If found responsible for committing academic dishonesty, a student may be subject to one or both types of penalties: an academic (grade) penalty administered by the professor and/or disciplinary action through the Rensselaer judicial process described in this handbook.

Academic dishonesty is a violation of the Grounds for Disciplinary Action as described in this handbook. A student may be subject to any of the following types of disciplinary action should disciplinary action be pursued by the instructor: disciplinary warning; disciplinary probation; disciplinary suspension, expulsion and/or alternative actions as agreed on by the student and hearing officer. It should be noted that no student who allegedly commits academic dishonesty will be able to drop or change the grade option for the course in question and is not eligible to request an F examination for the course.

The definitions and examples presented below are a sampling of types of academic dishonesty and are not to be construed as an exhaustive or exclusive list. The academic integrity policy applies to all students, undergraduate and graduate, and to scholarly pursuits and research. Additionally, attempts to commit academic dishonesty or to assist in the commission or attempt of such an act are also violations of this policy.

Academic Fraud

Academic Fraud is defined as the alteration of documentation relating to the grading process; for example, changing exam solutions to negotiate for a higher grade or tampering with an instructor's grade book.

Collaboration

Collaboration is defined as deliberately facilitating an act of academic dishonesty in any way or form; for example, allowing another student to observe an exam paper or allowing another student to "recycle" one's old term paper or using one another's work in a paper or lab report without citing it as another's work.

Copying

Copying is defined as obtaining information pertaining to a graded exercise by deliberately observing the paper of another student; for example, noting which alternative a neighboring student has circled on a multiple-choice exam.

Cribbing

Cribbing is defined as the use or attempted use of prohibited materials, information, or study aids in an academic exercise. Examples include using an unauthorized formal sheet during an exam.

Fabrication

Fabrication is defined as the unauthorized falsification or invention of any information in an academic exercise. Examples include the use of "bought" or "ready-made" term papers, or falsifying lab records or reports.

Plagiarism

Plagiarism is defined as representing the work or words of another as one's own through the omission of acknowledgment or reference. Examples include using sentences verbatim from a published source in a term paper without appropriate referencing, or presenting as one's own the detailed argument of a published source, or presenting as one's own electronically or digitally enhanced graphic representations from any form of media.

Sabotage

Sabotage is defined as the destruction of another student's work. Examples include destroying a model, lab experiment, computer program, or term paper developed by another student.

Substitution

Substitution is defined as utilizing a proxy, or acting as a proxy, in any academic exercise. Examples include taking an exam for another student or having a homework assignment done by someone else.
