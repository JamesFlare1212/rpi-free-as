## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

What narratives, language, theories, and precedents exist to discuss contemporary literature and culture of the United States? How can multicultural U.S. literature help us think, see, and know “America” in alternate and unexpected ways? This course introduces students to contemporary U.S. literatures within a transnational American framework and in conjunction with diverse cultural, historical, political, and aesthetic contexts in order to explore these and other questions using an interdisciplinary approach. Students will learn to closely read literary texts in their transcultural and transnational contexts as tools for exploring the challenges in situating an American literary canon (and perhaps the nation itself) in the twenty-first century. Therefore, the course will encourage interrogation and reflection on the field of multicultural American literature primarily as it intersects with issues of individual and national identities. Additional intersections might include history, economics, politics, philosophy, science, or technology. Assignments focus on helping students refine critical analysis and writing skills, as well as practicing creative, open-ended responses. This course emphasizes experimentation, creativity, collaboration, and student-centered learning.

## Course Goals

### Goals:

- To foster enjoyment of literature.
- To introduce a range of works in American literature representing diversecultures and identities.

- To help students recognize that literature does not occur in isolation but is part of a complex dialogue within cultural and historical contexts.
- To disclose and discuss which books have been historically taught and why.
- To provide students with opportunities for research in the Humanities.
- To facilitate team building, small group-dynamics, and interpersonal communication skills by fostering a learning community.

## Learning Outcomes

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes (CLOs):

- Produce strong analyses of literary texts, supported by scholarly evidence, in relation to the material, economic, social, and political, and (trans)national conditions of textual production.
- Utilize theoretical knowledge from literary and cultural studies to situate discussions of historically underrepresented American cultures and worldviews.
- Demonstrate improvement in oral communication skills through class participation and presentations.
- Articulate, formally and informally, how works of literature from discrete cultural traditions speak both within and against broader American and world contexts, as well as develop arguments on how social constructions of identity shape contemporary literature.
- Practice alternative methods of critical response, including but not limited to creative writing, graphic arts, and digital storytelling.

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes Assessment Measures:

**Reading:** Your primary assignment in this course is to read course texts actively and critically. The expectation is that as you engage with these texts you are taking notes, making connections, and forming opinions that you are prepared to articulate to others. Please note that this is a literature class. Your registration in this class indicates that you are prepared to engage in substantial reading assignments and that you have set aside appropriate time to do so.

**Participation:** Active participation demonstrates you recognize your responsibility for your education and reflects common professional tasks beyond the undergraduate classroom. To do well in this category, you must first and foremost maintain a positive attitude and open mind. You must read carefully, take notes, and engage in class activities regularly, prepared to interact with your peers. **Note that effort and timeliness in peer reviews are a crucial part of this category.** This is not a course in which you will read for information and pass an exam. Instead, the primary mode of learning will be dependent on consistent, active preparation (reading) and participation—discussion, interrogation, visits to office hours, and other modes of interactions. Reading quizzes and graded in-class activities may also be included at the instructor’s discretion. **At a minimum, you should keep up with all readings, be prepared to engage with peers in small groups, make substantial efforts in peer review, and ask one specific** **question or analyze one specific passage in the assigned reading for every class.** Expect that students will be called upon regularly to do this.

**Call and Response Boards.** These are your initial questions and thoughtful, early comments about the assigned selection of text. The purpose is to help you organize your thoughts

individually so that we can explore them more productively as a community, and to give you some lower stakes practice in textual analysis. You can think of these as educated questions and reading reflections (as opposed to informal opinions or product reviews, for example) that set the tone for fruitful in-class discussions and more developed seminar papers. You will be assigned to participate in call and response posting five times over the term. Questions (calls) should be posted Sunday by midnight and responses are due Monday by midnight. All students should peruse calls and responses before class on Tuesday, with students assigned to call and response prepared with observations to share about the posts. The length requirements here are flexible; I am simply interested in seeing you engage with the texts using course terminology and frameworks and hearing your questions and ideas. The writing style need not be formal, but it must engage with the reading in some depth (HINT: avoid both generalizations and opinions not grounded in close readings) and reference specific pages, passages and/or elements of the text.

**The Alt-essay:** This assignment has the same rudimentary functions as a typical essay in that you must 1) express an analysis of a text and 2) provide evidence to support your conclusions. However, the form of the alt-essay is entirely variable, asking you to consider media and form deliberately and thoughtfully. The alt-essay allows you to do rather than describe. The idea is to explore intellectual passions using whatever style is most exciting to you. The only evaluation criteria are on how well you communicate your analysis in your chosen format. There are no formal, stylistic requirements (of course, do maintain academic integrity). Your alt-essay can be written in five paragraphs or twenty-six. If you decide you need to cite something, you can do that any way you want. If you want to use lists, use lists. If you want to write in the first person, write in the first person. If you prefer to present the whole thing as a video or a game or a web page, go for it. Use slang. Or don’t. Write in sentence fragments if you think that would be effective. Mix languages if you are multilingual. In other words, in an alt-essay you have complete freedom of form: you can use whatever style of writing, presentation, citation, or media you want. What is important is that the format and presentation helps rather than hinders your analysis of the text.

**Summary-Critique & Discussion Facilitation**: The objective of this assignment is to help you become familiar with literary and cultural scholarship: how to recognize it, where to find it, and how to make your way through it. First you will choose a work of scholarship (selections provided, though students may alternatively seek their own resources) you feel helps you interpret a course text in new and important ways. After reading the article several times to understand it fully in all its nuance, you will present a brief overview of the article in which you identify the critic’s thesis and the supporting evidence for that thesis using your own words and choice quotes from the article. Then you will offer an oral critique of the scholarship in which you answer: How does the critical piece add to my experience of reading the primary text? How does it conflict with my understanding of the text? Am I convinced? Swayed? Doubtful? In partial agreement? In total disagreement? What did the critic fail to account for? Present your summary and critique in such a way that your peers can 1) understand the basics of the article 2) follow your critique. After the presentation, be prepared with questions to open a thirty-minute (or so) discussion that places the critical article in conversation with the primary text. Most students will work in pairs for this assignment, and discussion questions should be submitted.

**The Essay** (7-9 pages)**.** Write a thesis-driven analysis in which you a) give a close reading of one or two course texts; b) situate the work(s) in the context of historical/

cultural production and any theoretical orientation of interest to you based on scholarship we’ve read; and c) explore the theories of multicultural American literature in relation to the text(s). Successful papers will be graded on your ability to a) offer focused, close, readings of carefully selected passages, b) relate specific textual details to historical/cultural conditions of production, and d) make analytical claims as to the meaning and significance of the text(s) in relation to the intersection of cultural and national identity. Include proper MLA (8th edition) style citations and a Works Cited page.

- ### Academic Integrity

  Student-teacher relationships are built on trust. For example, students must trust that teachers have made appropriate decisions about the structure and content of the courses they teach, and teachers must trust that the assignments that students turn in are their own. Acts that violate this trust undermine the educational process. The Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities and The Graduate Student Supplement define various forms of Academic Dishonesty and you should make yourself familiar with these. In this class, all assignments that are turned in for a grade must represent the student’s own work. In cases where help was received, or teamwork was allowed, a notation on the assignment should indicate your collaboration.

  Violations of academic integrity may also be reported to the appropriate Dean (Dean of Students for undergraduate students or the Dean of Graduate Education for graduate students, respectively).

  If you have any question concerning this policy before submitting an assignment, please ask for clarification.
