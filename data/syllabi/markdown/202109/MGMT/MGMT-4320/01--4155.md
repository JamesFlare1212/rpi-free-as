## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

Investments I provides students with a theoretical and practical background in the field of investments. The course reviews various financial instruments, examines their historical performance for evidence of risk-return trade-off, presents basic portfolio theory and its implications for diversification, discusses the capital asset pricing model, and empirical estimation of beta riskiness. Other topics include mutual fund performance, behavioral biases influencing investors, and the theory of efficient markets.

This course emphasizes the development of tools required for investment decision-making and covers major topics of financial markets, security valuation, and capital market equilibrium. Debt and equity instruments, and where and how they are traded, will be introduced. We will also briefly look at tools used to value these instruments. The course will conclude with the discussion of active portfolio management strategies, including alternative assets such as hedge funds and private equity, and the performance evaluation of such strategies. The course will make extensive use of Microsoft Excel, intended to enhance student skills for practical financial application of technology.

## Course Goals

### Goals:

Investments I follows the 6 sections and 22 chapters of Bodie, Kane, and Marcus’ Essentials of Investments. The goal of this text and classroom instruction is to provide students with a broad understanding of financial markets, portfolio construction, securities analysis, and active/passive investment strategies. The course sections are as follows:

Part One: Elements of Investments (Chapters 1 through 4) – These introductory chapters provide a bird’s eye view of the investing environment. Students will tour the major types of markets in which securities trade, the trading process, and the major players in these arenas. Students will also observe how both markets and securities have evolved to meet the changing and complex needs of different participants in the financial system.

Part Two: Portfolio Theory (Chapters 5 through 9) – This section begins by providing tools needed to interpret the history of rates of return and the lessons that history offers for how investors might go about constructing portfolios using safe and risky assets. It aims to accomplish efficient diversification across asset classes like bonds and stocks and across individual securities within these asset classes. The Capital Asset Pricing Model (CAPM) and Arbitrage Pricing Theory (APT), as well as index and multi-index models, are introduced. The question of whether portfolio managers can outperform simple investment strategies such as a buy and hold index fund is examined along with important issues in behavioral finance.

Part Three: Debt Securities (Chapters 10 through 11) – Provides students with an introduction to debt markets and securities. Students will learn how to value such securities and why their values change with interest rates. Features determining the sensitivity of bond prices to interest rates and how investors measure and manage interest risk will also be examined.

Part Four: Security Analysis (Chapter 12 through 14) – These chapters begin with a “top-down” approach to security analysis, starting with an overview of international, macroeconomic, and industry issues, only then progressing to the analysis of particular firms. These topics form the core of fundamental analysis. Students will develop a good sense of the various valuation techniques used to analyze stocks and the stock market.

Part Five: Derivative Markets (Chapter 15 through 17) – Derivatives provide a means to control risk that is qualitatively different from the techniques traditional considered in portfolio theory. Derivatives allow investors to change the shape of the probability distribution of investment returns. An entirely new approach to risk management follows from this insight. Students will explore how derivatives can be used as parts of a well-designed portfolio strategy, examine some popular portfolio strategies utilizing these securities, and take a look at how derivatives are valued.

Part Six: Active Investment Management (Chapter 18 through 22) – Active managers believe that markets are not efficient and that bargains can be found in security markets by application of asset valuation and portfolio theory. First, performance evaluation and techniques to achieve superior performance are examined. The discussion then turns to international investing and hedge funds. A framework to formulate an effective household savings/investment plan, including taxes and inflation, is then introduced. The text concludes with a broad framework for active investment management provided by the CFA Institute.

## Learning Outcomes

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes (CLOs):

- The course focuses on investment analysis, which allows students to learn practical applications of investment theory and to convey insights of practical value. A systematic collection of Microsoft Excel spreadsheets gives students tools to explore concepts more deeply and improve their overall Excel skillset. These spreadsheets are available both from the text and the instructor and provide a taste of the sophisticated analytical tools available to professional investors. Investments I will introduce students to the major issues of concern to all investors. It gives students the skills to conduct sophisticated assessment of current issues and debates covered by both the popular media and more specialized finance journals. Whether a student plants to become an investment professional/Chartered Financial Analyst managing institutional portfolios or simply a sophisticated investor aiming to produce an ample retirement, students will find the skills in this course essential. In summary, Investments I allows students to discover what type of investor they are (passive, active, some combination, etc.) that aligns risk tolerance with investor behavior and provides frameworks and knowledge to implement the appropriate strategies based on these individual preferences.

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes Assessment Measures:

Attendance and Class Participation (10% of Course Grade)

Attendance for each class will be taken informally using digital imaging. A picture of the classroom (and/or WebEx videos) will be taken at the beginning of each class and following any mid-class breaks. Additionally, students will receive credit for participation via interaction with the class hashtag #rpiINV on Twitter.

Quizzes (10% of Course Grade)

Approximately every two chapters, a four question, multiple choice quiz will be given during class time. Completing this quiz during the class will earn full credit regardless of the number of correct/incorrect answers. The purpose of the quizzes is for students to self-evaluate their progress during the course and ask questions/request clarification regarding specific topics, concepts, and/or formulas. ***Please Note***: quizzes must be completed within 24 hours of the end of classes in which they are assigned or no credit will be received.

Assignments (4 Assignments, 10% Each)

Students will complete four assignments throughout the semester. Each assignment is worth 10% of the course grade. Students will work *independently* on the multiple choice and Excel portions of these assignments and the instructor will provide review sessions and/or additional office hours before each assignment deadline (see course calendar for tentative assignment deadlines). The assignments will include a multiple choice (25 questions) within Blackboard/LMS and a Microsoft Excel component (3 questions) that is to be downloaded, completed, and uploaded back into Blackboard before the assignment deadline. The instructor will provide feedback through comments within the Excel assignment documents and upload the comments back to Blackboard/LMS. *Students are strongly encouraged to review this feedback for Microsoft Excel and data exercises.*

Midterm and Final Exam (20% Each)

The midterm and final exams will have a very similar format to the assignments, but the midterm (final) exam will be administered in/during (take home) class. Each student will be required to bring/use a laptop computer with network connectivity and the required software (Microsoft Excel, web browser to access LMS, etc.). For the midterm exam, no external resources (e.g., non-LMS websites, chat programs, electronic or paper documents, etc.) will be permitted for use during the final examination with the exception of a single page, double-sided paper document with formulas and/or notes of the student’s choosing. The final exam will be open-book.
