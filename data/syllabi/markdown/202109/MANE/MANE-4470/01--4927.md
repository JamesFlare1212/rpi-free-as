## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

Course Summary

An introductory course on the principles of radiation and radiation protection (health physics). Provides a fundamental understanding of radiation interactions in matter, the biological effects of radiation, internal and external dosimetry, occupational and environmental radiation protection, health physics instrumentation, regulations, waste management, risk assessment, and radiation shielding.

The course is organized into three parts (with references to chapters in the required textbook).

- **Radiation Physics** (Chapters 2-6): underlying physical principles associated with radiation, radioactivity, interactions with matter, and the transfer of radiation energy.

- **Radiation Protection** (Chapters 7-9): biology and the effects of radiation interactions, measures and quantities of radiation risk, and the principles and standards of radiation protection.

- **Applications to Health and Safety** (Chapters 10, 11, 13, and supplementary materials): radiation protection systems and regulations; computational methods for shielding design; issues associated with specific industries such as power reactors, medical facilities, and accelerators.

## Course Goals

### Goals:

Course Objectives

This upper level undergraduate course is one part of Rensselaer’s nuclear engineering curriculum triad:

Physics of Nuclear Reactors -- manage nuclear energy production

Nuclear Power Systems Engineering -- manage energy transport and conversion

Radiological Engineering -- manage the beneficial use of radiation

This course begins to prepare you to manage the beneficial use of radiation. You will further embrace the aspects of safety, security, and quality that are part of the culture of nuclear engineering. You will become proficient in applying technical skills and standards to protect and promote health, safety, and well-being when radiation is involved.

## Grading Criteria

### Criteria Details:

Grading and Deliverables

Final exam (final exam week): 5% total

Three written exams: 20% each 60% total

Two project reports: 10% each 20% total

Homework and exercises: 15% total

The exams will generally be closed book, but may have open book components. Additional exam details will be covered prior to each exam.

Two projects involving the use of Monte Carlo simulations for shielding design and analysis will be assigned during the semester.

Homework will be assigned approximately weekly and will be due in class one week from the date assigned. Homework may be covered in the class in which it is due so late homework will not be accepted. In-class exercises must be completed in the class or classes they are conducted in. Evaluation of the homework and exercises is based on good-faith effort. The exams will determine if you have learned from any mistakes you made in the homework and exercises.

The grade thresholds are: A ≥ 94 A- ≥ 90

B+ ≥ 87 B ≥ 84 B- ≥ 80

C+ ≥ 77 C ≥ 74 C- ≥ 70

D+ ≥ 67 D ≥ 64 F < 64

This system establishes a common base grade scheme for the entire class. However, the course team reserves the right to “curve up” at the end of the course by applying one or more alternative grading schemes to determine each student’s final course grade:

1. A) As long as each scheme is applied equally to every student in the class; and
2. B) Each student receives the highest grade determined by all schemes so that no student’s grade will be lower than the base grade scheme, only the same or higher.

Grade Disputes

Grade disputes for exams must be made in writing to the instructor and teaching assistant within 14 days of the exam being returned in class. For each problem being disputed please identify the question and why you believe the grade should be different. For disputes of homework assignment and exercise grades please meet with whoever was responsible for grading the particular assignment.

### Additional Academic Integrity Course Policy and Penalty Information:

**Academic Integrity**

Student-teacher relationships are built on trust. For example, students must trust that teachers have made appropriate decisions about the structure and content of the courses they teach, and teachers must trust that the assignments that students turn in are their own. Acts that violate this trust undermine the educational process. The Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities and The Graduate Student Supplement define various forms of Academic Dishonesty and you should make yourself familiar with these.

In this class, all in-class exercises and homework assignments (that are turned in for a grade) must be significantly representative of your own work. No “group” hand-ins are permitted: as an individual you will submit your own work with your own substantial and significant individual content and style.

In cases where teamwork is allowed, a notation on the assignment should clearly identify all collaborators and their individual contributions.

We encourage you to collaborate in studying for this course to maximize your learning and to broaden your experiences in how one might apply what you learn to solving challenging radiological engineering problems. However, at the end of the collaborative process you must have your own copy of your deliverable in which you identify your collaboration (e.g., in comments and/or references clearly indicated in the assignment). Prior to submission you should review and revise your deliverable to match your unique style and approach to organizing and communicating your information.

Specific to this course, academic dishonesty can take the following forms.

1\) Taking an exam with any assistance from another person, regardless of who they are, unless the instructor specifically states that it is a group activity (e.g., a group oral exam).

2\) Using any materials (such as a crib sheet or a calculator) that you did not bring for you and you alone to use on an exam, unless given explicit permission by the proctor (e.g., typically for extraordinary circumstances such as the failure of your calculator during an exam, etc., though such assistance should not be expected).

3\) Using any materials (such as a text, extra crib sheets, a laptop, a cell phone, any electronic device – wireless or otherwise, etc.) that are not explicitly permitted during an exam.

4\) Interaction with a wireless device (e.g., a cell phone or tablet) during an exam (except when explicitly allowed by the proctor) will be considered an illicit data exchange and will result in a zero for the entire exam.

5\) Exiting the exam room without permission, or not returning in a prompt and timely manner when given permission. “Prompt and timely” is at the discretion of the instructional team.

6\) Submission of a deliverable that, except for short phrases or properly referenced material, consists of any content not created by and original to you.

7\) Indicating someone as “present” (e.g., on an attendance sheet or submitting an in-class exercise) who is not present in class at the time the indication is made.

8\) Collaboration is encouraged. However, assisting someone else with work where they intend to turn in the work as if it was done on their own will itself be considered a serious violation of academic honesty. Give credit for any assistance received, and have sufficient content of your own making according to the expectations of the deliverable.

The penalty for submission of an exam that is in violation of this policy is failure of the course and referral to the Dean of Students.

Submission of a deliverable that is in violation of this policy will result in a grade of zero on the assignment for the first offense. If there is a subsequent infraction the student fails the course and is referred to the Dean of Students.

If you have any questions concerning this policy before submitting a deliverable please ask for clarification.
