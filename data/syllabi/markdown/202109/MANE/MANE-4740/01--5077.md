## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

This laboratory course offers a culminating experimental experience in the field of thermal and fluids engineering for MANE students. The theoretical background gained in ENGR-2250 (TFE I) and MANE-4010 (TFE II) is now consolidated and expanded by hands-on experiments and analysis of thermal and fluids systems. This lab course is intended to introduce and familiarize students to a variety of thermodynamic, heat transfer, and fluid flow phenomena in the context of the operation of major pieces of equipment. Both fundamental concepts as well as practical/applied ideas will be explored.  
You will work together in teams, and there are a total of four laboratory experiences. Three weeks will be spent on each laboratory experience. Your team starts preparations a week ahead of a scheduled experiment by reviewing the theoretical background and studying the experimental setup and procedures. You will be given a description of a problem that needs to be solved, and tasked with writing a proposal to address this problem. Your proposal is due the moment you walk into the lab to perform the experiment. Each laboratory experience consists of two experimental investigations. You will present your results and preliminary conclusions in one week, and then submit an extensive laboratory report after another week. The laboratory reports will be similar to technical reports produced by practicing engineers. Your proposals, presentations, and reports will be evaluated by your lab section instructor.

## Course Text(s)

### Text Details:

There are no required texts for this class since most of the theoretical material is covered in Thermal and Fluids Engineering I (ENGR-2250) and in Thermal & Fluids Engineering II (MANE-4010).  
• The textbook and lecture notes used in ENGR 2250 and MANE 4010 (Introduction to Thermal and Fluids Engineering by Kaminski and Jensen) are the most recommended references for refreshing the theoretical aspects.  
• The Library also contains the following books which may be used to reinforce fundamentals of heat transfer, thermodynamics and fluid mechanics: Fundamentals of Thermal-Fluid Sciences by Çengel and Turner, Brief Introduction to Fluid Mechanics by Young, Munson and Okiishi, Fundamentals of Engineering Thermodynamics by Moran and Shapiro, Fundamentals of Heat and Mass Transfer by Incropera and DeWitt, and Fluid Mechanics by White. In addition, you may find the book by Figliola & Beasley (Theory and Design for Mechanical Measurements 2nd Edition, 1995, Wiley) to be a useful reference.  
• Materials specific to the four experiments performed in this class will be provided through handouts and lecture notes posted on LMS. Extensive handouts have been prepared on the first three experiments. These handouts include an introduction to the project, theory, specific objectives, equipment description and operating instructions, and lab report requirements. Other handouts have been prepared to provide information on i) uncertainties in experimental data and ii) the philosophy behind large-scale experimental investigations. These two handouts should be studied as carefully as the ones on the individual experiments.

## Course Goals

### Goals:

The topics to be covered will be a combination of new material specific to this lab course, material previously covered in Thermal and Fluids Engineering I, and material covered in Thermal and Fluids Engineering II.  
The specific objectives of the course are:  
1\. To demonstrate thermodynamic, heat transfer, and fluid flow principles;  
2\. To integrate theory and practice through the operation of experiments and through the acquisition and analysis of experimental data;  
3\. To familiarize students with complex, realistic systems and the interactions among thermodynamics, heat transfer, and fluid flow;  
4\. To introduce some new concepts and techniques in the three topic areas;  
5\. To develop abilities to understand, experiment with, and analyze thermal and fluids systems, including analysis and decision making in the face of experimental uncertainty;  
6\. To exercise technical communication skills in a variety of modes.

## Course Content

### Content Details:

The experiments (in the order in which they will be performed) and the general concepts associated with each are listed below:  
1\. Hydraulic flow loop -- instrument calibration, friction factors (CII 5118)  
2\. Wind tunnel -- field concepts including velocity field, hydraulic boundary layers, transition to turbulence, heat transfer coefficients, multimode heat transfer (CII 5222)  
3\. Air conditioning system -- refrigeration cycles, psychrometrics, heat transfer, energy balances, refrigerant properties (CII 5118)  
4\. Critical Heat Flux – convective heat transfer in a liquid, material science (CII 5222)  
Notes on Data Intensive Tasks:  
For all laboratory experiments: After collecting the data during the laboratory session, students are expected to explain their methods for collecting the data, how the data was reduced, and what the results were. They are expected to provide an assessment of experimental uncertainty and a discussion of the validity of their results. Finally, the students write a formal engineers report in which they present all of the findings discussed above. The grades given on the proposal, presentation, and report are aggregated to give an overall grade for a given experiment.

## Grading Criteria

### Criteria Details:

The course grade will be based on the scores earned on the following items:  
• Reporting activities (group) 4 Reports 70%*  
This grade portion includes three components:  
\- a short proposal (15% of report grade),  
\- an oral presentation of results (15%),  
\- written report (70 %) which includes a “cohesiveness/quality factor” (0.0 – 1.0 multiplier)  
• Peer assessment 10%\*\*  
• Online quizzes (individual) 4 total 20%\*\*\*  
TOTAL 100%  
*Lab reporting details  
Each experiment has an imaginary scenario associated with it that details a problem that is to be solved. Your group is to write a proposal (approximately 2 pages in length) that details the experimental project that you will undertake to solve that problem. This proposal is due the moment you walk into the lab to perform the experiment. Please submit it as a PDF.  
You will report on the laboratory experiment twice. The first report will be an oral presentation, including visuals, one week after the experiment is performed. This will be conducted via WebEx with your instructor. Consider this a formal presentation: lack of preparation, organization, or meaningful content will have significant grade consequences. (Imagine you are presenting to your manager – someone who wants you to succeed, but faces serious risks if you don’t.) You are expected to present the results of your analyses, including uncertainties, through data tables and plots. You will also present your tentative conclusions based on your results. The instructor will then ask questions about your collective understanding of the experiment, its theoretical background, the deeper meaning of some of your results, and any lessons learned, including your recommendations for how you might improve your results in the future. This presentation should better prepare you to more easily “put your thoughts on paper” when writing a formal laboratory report over the next week, hopefully with the confidence that your results and conclusions are valid.  
The written laboratory experiment report is typically due two weeks after performing the lab. The report will be graded principally on its technical content, but also on the quality of the writing, clarity, appearance, and format. Please submit it as a PDF.  
There will be a Cohesiveness/Quality Factor assigned to the overall report. This factor is an overall grade multiplier that ranges from 0 to 1. Reports that contain no errors in spelling, grammar, sentence structure, or formatting, are reasonably organized, easy to read/understand, and contain language and an amount of information that is appropriate for a technical report, will score highly in this area. Reports that are lacking in any/all of the areas mentioned will score poorly. The Cohesiveness/Quality Factor is then applied to (multiplied by) each student’s grade for the report.  
Writing Responsibilities  
Each report will be split up into 3 sections. Students should distribute themselves as evenly as possible between the 3 sections. One grade will be assigned to all authors of each section. As the semester progresses, the students must rotate through responsibilities for each section. When you submit each lab report, please also submit a Word copy of the lab report grading rubric (available on LMS) with the section authors filled in. The 3 sections are:  
Opening: Title Page, Abstract, Introduction, Background, Apparatus, & Procedures  
Body: Data Reduction & Results Section  
Closing: Discussion, Conclusion, Sample Calculations, Appendices  
The Cohesiveness/Quality Factor is multiplied to each student’s total grade for each report.  
For example:  
Say the grades for the first report are as follows:  
Proposal (Grade: 95%)  
Presentation (Grade: 100%)  
John Doe helps write the Body portion of the report (Grade: 70%)  
But the overall report had some minor errors that would have been solved with a simple proof-read. For example, there are graphs in black and white but the authors write about them as if they are in color and John’s name is spelled wrong on the title page. So, the report earns a Cohesiveness/Quality Factor score of 0.90. Ergo:  
John’s grade: 15%(95% proposal) + 15%(100% presentation) + 70%(0.90 overall report quality factor)(70% section) = 73.35/100  
\*\*Peer assessment, and deficient team participation penalty  
You and the other members of your team are expected to participate fully in each laboratory experience and in the preparation of all team work products (proposal, presentation, and report). You will be given the opportunity to evaluate yourself and your peers on your levels of contribution to your team work products (proposal, presentation, and report). Trends in less than full participation will be reflected in deductions from an individual’s peer assessment score.  
Pre-lab Reading  
Much more can be gained from an experiment if before an attempt to perform the experiment the student has the background knowledge about the objectives and concepts (theory) governing the processes to be studied. It is also critical to understand the objectives and the plan of work for each lab before coming to the lab. This information is provided in the extended handouts for each experiment. Please read carefully all of the handouts. Some supplemental materials may also be provided on LMS for those wishing a deeper understanding of the theory, instrumentation, and/or standards related to the experiments.  
\*\*\*Online Quizzes  
Throughout the semester, there will be a series of quizzes administered via LMS on topics covered during lectures and in the laboratory. The primary intent of the quiz is to draw attention to details in both the lab manual and the laboratory procedures manual that may be important during the lab, and to be sure students understood the underlying physical principles of the experiments. You may use any materials you want during the quiz, but you must take it alone, without any assistance from anyone else. The quizzes will be timed multiple-choice.  
Attendance: Attendance is expected for the weekly lecture. Attendance is mandatory for the lab sessions. Attendance in the group presentations (through WebEx) is expected for all group members. Please communicate with your section instructor if you need an accommodation.  
Final Exam: There is no final exam. (A timeslot for this course might appear on the finals schedule, but that is just a placeholder.)  
Grade Curving: The difficulty of the material may require curving the class grade, but this is seldom needed given that students have a high degree of control of their level of performance on the proposals and lab reports. Equalization of the sections will be performed to account for differences in report grading between instructors.  
Grade modifiers: Grade modifiers (+, −) will be used in assigning the grade in this class.

### Additional Academic Integrity Course Policy and Penalty Information:

Academic Integrity and Rules of Behavior  
Rensselaer’s policy on academic dishonesty is explained in the Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities. You are expected to review and understand that information. In addition, the following policies apply specifically to this course.  
• Committing, or attempting to commit an act of academic dishonesty, or assisting in the commission or attempt of such an act, is a flagrant offense to the process and culture of education; expect our reaction to it to be harsh.  
• You are expected to refrain from any academically dishonest act, and to be vigilant in protecting your work from others who might attempt to commit an act of academic dishonesty.  
In this course, academic dishonesty – cheating -- can take the following forms.  
• Submission of a lab proposal or report that, except for short phrases or properly referenced material (e.g., quotes and figures from the lab manual, etc.), consists of any content not created by and original to the report authors listed. Plagiarism may be penalized according to the severity and deliberateness of the act, from reduction of report grade to failure of the course and referral to the Dean of Students for dismissal from Rensselaer.  
• Taking a quiz with any assistance from another person, regardless of who they are, or the medium of communication for assistance (talk, phone, texting, etc.).  
• Using any materials (such as a cell phone, camera) that are not explicitly permitted during a quiz. (Quizzes are open book, open notes, open lab reports, open laptop, open internet searches, but closed communication.)  
Mobile Devices: During a quiz all mobile devices that are not your laptop (e.g., cell/smart phones, pagers, cameras, etc.) must be stored securely away and are not be used unless specifically directed otherwise by the instructor. The use of, or any interaction with, a mobile device during an quiz without explicit permission of the instructor will be interpreted as the illicit transfer of quiz data, which will be considered an act of cheating and treated as such.  
The penalty for violating these policies is at the discretion of any or all members of the instructional team. In general, the penalty for any violation is  
• failure of the course and referral to the Dean of Students  
Collaboration  
You are expected to collaborate with the members of your laboratory group with respect to the conduct of your experiments and the preparation of your reports. However, as stated above, your report, including the experimental data and observations described therein, must be those of your own group. In rare cases (e.g., lab equipment failure) your instructor may provide you with experimental data to use as if you had obtained it yourselves; this will be considered “your data”, regardless of the actual source.
