## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

This is an introductory course in biological systems focusing on major topics in evolution, human genetics & medicine, and ecology. It is an undergraduate level course. It is intended for non-majors and majors and covers a variety of topics that impact our daily lives and the grand challenges of this decade and your generation. Activities, such as online labs and simulations enable students to experience the fundamental biological concepts hands-on. The goal is to provide an understanding of fundamental principles, to develop analytical thinking skills in the context of modern biology, and to develop data dexterity for understanding and solving grand challenges in the life sciences.

## Course Goals

### Goals:

## Course Goals / Objectives

1. Principle Concepts: Students will be able to grasp and master introductory biological principles underscoring the Scientific Method. Students at a technological university will gain basic biological literacy in laboratory techniques.
2. Predict and Propose: Students will be able to connect and link core biological concepts, and apply them to the bases of protocols used in the laboratory course. Students will gain experience in formulating and testing hypotheses, using appropriate *in silico* and computational materials and methods, and analyzing data sets quantitatively and/or qualitatively to arrive at conclusions, and predict and propose possible solutions.
3. Pre-requisites: Students will be able to satisfy prerequisites necessary in order to take advanced courses in the biological sciences. Students will be prepared to take the second introductory biology course, BIOL 2120 and Biostatistics, BIOL 4200. BIOL 1010 serves as the first course for life science majors and for students interested in the health professions. We have separate sections for majors and non-majors in this introductory BIOL 1015 laboratory course.

Student-teacher relationships are built on trust. For example, students must trust that teachers have made appropriate decisions about the structure and content of the courses they teach, and teachers must trust that the assignments that students turn in are their own. Acts that violate this trust undermine the educational process. The Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities and The Graduate Student Supplement define various forms of Academic Dishonesty and you should make yourself familiar with these. In this class, all assignments that are turned in for a grade must represent the student’s own work. In cases where help was received, or teamwork was allowed, a notation on the assignment should indicate your collaboration.

1. Learning is interactive, and we encourage this. However, when you are writing anything for submission, answers to questions on Lecture Tests, you must write your answer in your own words. You must not copy from the websites that you used for research.
2. You may not copy verbatim from any source or peer submission. Plagiarism will be assessed 0 Points. The penalty for plagiarism or cheating or not following the Honor Code is as follows: A grade of zero will be given on the first assignment where a violation is detected. If there is a subsequent infraction the student will receive a grade of F for the course.
3. NOTE: If you have any doubts or concerns of whether an action constitutes academic dishonesty, please discuss it with any of your instructors or TAs.

Violations of academic integrity may also be reported to the appropriate Dean (Dean of Students for undergraduate students or the Dean of Graduate Education for graduate students, respectively).
