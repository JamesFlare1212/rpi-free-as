## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

This course involves a philosophical analysis of some of the basic moral issues raised by recent and anticipated developments in the areas of biology and medicine. The general question “What are moral problems, and how does one resolve them?” is examined in the context of concrete cases involving issues such as racial healthcare disparities, abortion, euthanasia, organ transplants, experimentation on human patients, patient rights and emerging ethical issues in the biomedical field. Students will be introduced to some of the prominent ethical theories in philosophy (Utilitarianism, Kantian theory, Virtue ethics, etc.) and will use these theories to examine contemporary issues in medicine. This is a communication-intensive course.

## Learning Outcomes

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes (CLOs):

- Students will recall the content and make inferences on key important points.
- Students will be able to demonstrate critical thinking and create solutions to problems based on learned content.
- Students will demonstrate ability to compare and contrast differing perspectives in the cases of particular ethical issues.
- Students will be able to evaluate multiple perspectives concerning bioethical issues and recognize that different value systems may lead to different ethical decisions.
- Students will be able to assess complex bioethical issues and the processes used to build resolution.
- Students will have the ability to express philosophical ideas and defend them effectively in argument, both in writing and orally.
- Students will be able to understand the process of writing and revision for an academic audience.

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes Assessment Measures:

## Course Assessment Measures

Course requirements consist of the following:

1. **Tests (3)** **(45% --- 15% each):** These open book tests (you may use your notes and textbook, NO COMPUTERS) will consist of multiple choice and short answer questions on reading and lecture material. **Test 1 – 6/7/21**, **Test 2 – 6/21/21 and Test 3 – 7/2/21.** There is no final exam for this course.
2. **Reflection Paper (****5%):** The student will find two different articles in the media regarding contemporary ethical issues and write an analysis of the position presented in the article. More information on this assignment will be distributed during the second week of class. This must be submitted to LMS by **6/28/21**. The sooner you hand them in the sooner you will have your grade.
3. **Journals (10%):** At the end of each lecture students will be given a prompt to reflect on the material covered that day. These reflections will be submitted to the instructor on **7/1/21** through LMS.
4. **Final Paper (35%)**: The student will submit a paper of approximately 2500 words that examines an ethical issue of particular interest to them. The student will explain the ethical issue and evaluate the issue using Utilitarian and Kantian theory. An extensive explanation of each theory and thorough application of the theories is required. Basically, the student will explain the issue, explain the two theories, apply the two theories to the issue and then explain which theory best analyzed the issued including a discussion of the strengths and weaknesses of each theory. More information on the assignment will be provided in class. There will be five steps to be completed for this paper:
   1. **Peer Conference (3%) – 6/3/21 (in class assignment) –** bring at least two articles you are considering using for your paper**.**
   2. **Consultation at The Center for Global Communication and Design (COMM-D) (****http://www.commd.rpi.edu** **) (3%) – Due: 6/14/21**
   3. **Submission of Draft for Instructor’s Review (4%) – DUE: 6/22/21**
   4. **Final Paper (25%) – 7/1/21**
5. **Class Participation (5%) :** I cannot emphasize enough how important class participation is. The online classroom environment will be a safe and stimulating place for the critical examination of the topics we cover. Through these discussions we will delve deeper into the subject matter of the readings. Participation includes coming to class prepared, which includes doing the assigned reading(s), and actively and thoughtfully participating in class discussions. Participation may also consist of contributions to the daily discussion board questions. We will discuss this requirement on our first day of class. Please do not mistake the participation grade as a “freebie.” Many people mistakenly believe that they can throw anything out there, no matter how illogically construed, and it counts as “doing philosophy.” This is not the case. Your discussion contributions must convey a thoughtful analysis of the material being covered.

### Other Course Policies:

***Inclusivity Statement***

This course is intended to reach students from diverse backgrounds and perspectives - all are welcome here. The diversity students bring to class, across gender, sexuality, age, disability, socio-economic status, ethnicity, race, religion, and learning needs is valued as an important asset and strength in the learning process.

We will have many opportunities in this class to explore challenging issues and increase our understandings of differing perspectives. Our conversations and structured dialogues may not always be easy, and we will need patience and compassion, and a foundation grounded in respect for others. Therefore, an additional aim of this course is to increase our capacity for these kinds of conversations, as this is a valuable skill in both scholarship and in life. I am committed to providing an atmosphere for learning that respects diversity. While working together to build this community I ask all members to:

- Share their unique experiences, values, and beliefs.
- Be open to the views of others.

- Honor the uniqueness of their colleagues.
- Appreciate the opportunity that we have to learn from each other in this community.
- Value each other’s opinions and communicate in a respectful manner.
- Keep confidential discussions that the community has of a personal (or professional) nature.
- Stories stay, lessons leave
- Use “I” statements
- Take Space, Make Space
- Accept that things may remain unresolved
- Embrace discomfort, but take a moment if you need it
- If you feel yourself getting angry or defensive, ask yourself why.
- You will make mistakes and apologize if you do (it is not about your intent it is about your impact)
- Take ownership of your words and actions. This is a good way to act with more intention and consideration of others in the classroom.
- Use this opportunity together to discuss ways in which we can create an inclusive environment in this course and across the Rensselaer community.

- ### Academic Integrity

  Student-teacher relationships are built on trust. For example, students must trust that teachers have made appropriate decisions about the structure and content of the courses they teach, and teachers must trust that the assignments that students turn in are their own. Acts that violate this trust undermine the educational process. The Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities and The Graduate Student Supplement define various forms of Academic Dishonesty and you should make yourself familiar with these.
