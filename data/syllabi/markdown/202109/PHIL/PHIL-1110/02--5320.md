## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

This course invites you into the world of philosophical ideas and reasoning -- to join a great conversation that has unfolded over the past 2,500 years since philosophy began in Ancient Greece. We will explore such issues as the nature of Philosophy, whether some ways of acting and living are morally better than others, the relationship that exists between mind and body, and whether philosophy has anything to contribute to discussions about the existence and nature of God. This course will aim to help you develop your skills in reading, discussing and evaluating philosophical texts.

## Learning Outcomes

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes (CLOs):

- 1\. Students will be able to explain and communicate the essential characteristics of Philosophy and the uses and value of Philosophy.
- 2\. Students will be able to identify the main types of arguments and explain how arguments are used in Philosophy.
- 3\. Students will be able to articulate and explain major approaches within philosophical ethics, including Utilitarianism, Deontology (duty-based ethics), Virtue Ethics, and Ethics of Care.
- 4\. In the context of ethics, students will be able to explain and utilize the distinction between intrinsic value and instrumental value.
- 5\. Students will be able to articulate such concepts and categories within Philosophy of Mind as Substance Dualism, Property Dualism, and Physicalism.
- 6\. Students will be able to identify and discuss some of the most influential issues and arguments within Philosophy of Religion having to do with religious experience, religion-science interaction, the existence of God, and the Problem of Evil.

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes Assessment Measures:

## Course Calendar

### Calendar Details:

The course calendar has been provided to you in a separate document which is also available on the LMS site for the course. However, a condensed version of the Calendar is as follows:  
Week of 8/30: Overview of course; Basics of arguments, necessary and sufficient conditions.  
Week of 9/6: Knowledge and how it might be acquired, difference between knowing and thinking, nature and benefits of Philosophy.  
Week of 9/13: Nature and benefits of Philosophy, continued; Plato's Allegory of the Cave as classic image of the role of Philosophy; Intro to Ethics; Intrinsic vs. instrumental value; begin coverage of Utilitarianism.  
Week of 9/20: Bentham's and Mill's versions of Utilitarianism, strengths and criticisms of Utilitarianism.  
Week of 9/27: Review for Exam 1; EXAM 1.  
Week of 10/4: Kant and duty-based ethics  
Week of 10/11: Trolley Problem and ethics settings for autonomous vehicles; Virtue ethics and ethics of care.  
Week of 10/18: Virtue ethics and ethics of care; Martin Luther King, Jr. on building social conscience through non-violent tension.  
Week of 10/25: Survey of positions on Mind-Body relationship; Descartes, knowledge, and interactive dualism.  
Week of 11/1: Neuroscience, Physicalism, and disorders of mind and perception; Review for Exam 2.  
Week of 11/8: EXAM 2; Nagel's "Knowledge Argument" and Jackson's "Qualia Argument" against Physicalism; Artificial intelligence and issue of whether computers could have minds.  
Week of 11/15: Artificial intelligence and the issue of whether computers could have minds. Overview of topics and positions in Philosophy of Religion.  
Week of 11/22: Overview of Philosophy of Religion, continued. Thanksgiving Break!  
Week of 11/29: Hume and criticism of classical cosmological and teleological arguments; Problem of Evil; Pascal's "Wager."  
Week of 12/6: Buddhism as non-Western religious/philosophical tradition; Review for Exam 3.

### Academic Policies:

- ### Academic Integrity

  Student-teacher relationships are built on trust. For example, students must trust that teachers have made appropriate decisions about the structure and content of the courses they teach, and teachers must trust that the assignments that students turn in are their own. Acts that violate this trust undermine the educational process. The Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities and The Graduate Student Supplement define various forms of Academic Dishonesty and you should make yourself familiar with these. In this class, all assignments that are turned in for a grade must represent the student’s own work. In cases where help was received, or teamwork was allowed, a notation on the assignment should indicate your collaboration.

  Violations of academic integrity may also be reported to the appropriate Dean (Dean of Students for undergraduate students or the Dean of Graduate Education for graduate students, respectively).

  If you have any question concerning this policy before submitting an assignment, please ask for clarification. In addition, you can visit the following site for more information on our Academic Integrity Policy: [Students Rights, Responsibilities, and Judicial Affairs](https://info.rpi.edu/dean-students/student-rights-responsibilities-and-judicial-affairs).
