## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

This course provides a set of genealogies, discourses, situations, and trajectories that serves as a conceptual basis for comprehending current configurations and past practices in Science Studies (STS). Science Studies is a critical interdisciplinary formation that promotes self-awareness and reflexivity about how science is done, what sciences are undone, and how both done and undone science may be studied. Your engagement with and contributions to the premises of this course will be assessed through four arcs (detailed below). Graduate courses are designed around particular skills designed to broaden your commitments to and engagement with the field of STS.

Science studies has been pursued from myriad disciplinary and adisciplinary perspectives, each representing conceptual frameworks used in the fields of STS. These fields include but are not limited to anthropology, cultural studies, history, policy studies, and sociology of science and technology. We must navigate the various streams of scholarship that comprise STS: laboratory studies, sociology of scientific knowledge (SSK), social construction of technology (SCOT), actor-network theory (ANT), and the new political sociology of science (NPSS), among others. Part of the work we do together is tracing these approaches and the concepts and methods on which they are based through multiple possible STS futures, including feminist STS, postcolonial STS, and other, as yet unnamed STSs.

This course steers clear of naming “core” concepts or “canonical” texts. Your professor’s disciplinary agnosticism is a commitment to ensuring that you acquire a comprehensive grasp of our interdisciplinary field, which works in synergistic relationship with other knowledge formations. My attempt is always to clarify the genealogies, configurations, discourses, situations, and trajectories of STS without prescribing yours. We read “foundational”/“classic”/“postfoundational” texts. Our practical goal is intense immersion in a variety of science studies that you might encounter in our field.

**ARCS OF ASSESSMENT**

**Arc 1: Structured Provocation and Discussion Facilitation**

Provoking others to insight is one the most valuable skills practiced in graduate school.

On the date for which you sign up, you are responsible for introducing the reading materials for the day so as to provoke discussion, which you will facilitate for approximately one half of the 2 ½ hour class period (factoring in a 20-minute break). Facilitation should draw out main points, but beyond that you may contextualize the texts; apply concepts raised therein to a specific analytic situation of your choosing; provide background or genealogy by drawing upon additional material; or generously critique the reading by drawing on other texts and contexts. Provocations integrate texts with *different* sources, intentions, or characteristics than those under “official” discussion. Provocations may include video, performance, installation, artistic, or digital projects that provide substantive examples of the work of STS in the world.

**Arc 2: STS in the Present: 4S Report**

You will each have 10 minutes in which to report your insights from attending the 4S conference. Please turn in a short (3-5-page) paper on these reflections on October 15, when you present your insights as part of our collective conversation about 4S. Many of the authors we are reading this semester present at 4S. You may also report on insights generated at the 6S pre-conference meeting.

**Arc 3: Implosion**

This assignment is a 7-10-page paper turned in on November 19 based on Joe Dumit’s “Writing the Implosion.” For this assignment, the student selects a specific artifact, ‘fact’, ‘thing’, or process, and researches the non-obvious relationships of connection between “labor, professional, material, technological, political, economic, symbolic, textual, bodily, historical, and educational” dimensions of that artifact, ‘fact’, or process. Based on the conceptual work of Gilles Deleuze, Michel Foucault, and Donna Haraway, implosion projects offer a unique way into thinking about science and technology.

**Arc 4: Synthesis**

The ability to improvise and synthesize by “thinking on your feet” is essential to success in graduate school. This arc requires that you draw connections across the components of each class, and provide an oral synthesis in the last 20 minutes of class that draws our streams of discussion together and raises questions for us to consider during the week we are apart. The goal is to create continuities and convergences across sessions.

**Arc 5: Final Written Assignment: Science Studies: What’s It to Me?**

Your final written assignment for this course is a position paper summarizing your current understanding of the breadth of science studies that comprise the field of STS, which by now you realize is comprised of interlocking “science studies.” Choose a theme around which to arrange your characterizations; add texts and contexts to those discussed in the course (you may also include your provocation); but reflect as fully as possible upon the body of knowledge in this course. This reflection should be 12-15 pages, typed, double-spaced, polished, and documented--written with your portfolio in mind.

***Week 4* Situating Science Studies I: Place Matters**

**Friday, September 24**

The feminist transformation of science studies came early enough that it may be thought of as foundational, despite the ongoing marginalization implied by the label. Feminist scientists and historians and philosophers of science, the latter schooled in logical positivism, began a critique of science studies and feminist movements even as they produced both. Their critical endeavor emphasized that the places where science is done were largely masculine domains dominated by modes of expertise and objectivity that foreclosed accounts founded in tacit knowledge, proximity and respect between researchers and researched, and situationally specific elements. They became targets during the “science wars.”

**Readings:**

1. Hilary Rose, excerpts from *Love, Power, Knowledge: Towards a Feminist Transformation of the Sciences;*
2. Donna Haraway, “Situated Knowledges” or excerpts of *Primate Visions* (discussed by Hilary Rose). LMS

## Learning Outcomes

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes (CLOs):

- 1\. Students will demonstrate comprehensive grasp of key conceptual and theoretical developments in the field of Science and Technology Studies through reflexive reading and discussion of key texts reviewing recent literature in the field.
- 2\. Students will demonstrate fluency by facilitating discussion to contextualize, compare, contrast, and think critically about approaches in STS.
- 3\. Students will demonstrate ability to research and write an “implosion” of their own, using approaches generated by advanced practitioners of historiographical, sociological, and anthropological concepts and methods in STS.
- 4\. Students will demonstrate the ability to synthesize and reflect upon (ruminate upon) assigned readings, lectures, and classroom discussion in written formats
- 5\. Students will individually create a “Science Studies: What’s It to Me?” essay useful for documenting breadth in STS portfolio exam, or for the literature review that forms part of the dissertation proposal and grant proposals.
