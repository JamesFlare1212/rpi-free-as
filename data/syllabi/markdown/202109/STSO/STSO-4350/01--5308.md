## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

This course is a research seminar where we broadly explore the *politics* of *design*, drawing on the interdisciplinary field of Science and Technology Studies. We approach *design* as the process of conceiving and creating material or intangible things, and recognize that it occurs within and is informed by particular social and political contexts. In turn, these deliberate creations can influence, limit, or expand our potential actions and experiences, and inform those social and political contexts. Examples of material things include buildings, cities, tools, instruments, technological systems, infrastructure for transportation, energy production, etc. Intangible things include patterns in social rules, roles, relationships, institutions, and political constitutions.

The course is divided roughly in half, with the first meeting of each week dedicated to a discussion of assigned readings and the second meeting of each week dedicated to a semester-long research project. This division allows us to cover a range of topics in collective discussion as well as to investigate particular topics that interest us individually in more detail. We will use a class Discord server to help facilitate any discussions that continue between class meetings.  
COVID-19 Note: If we need to shift to remote learning at some point this semester, we will move to a combination of Discord and Webex to hold class meetings.

## Learning Outcomes

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes (CLOs):

- Recognize social, political, and cultural origins of designs we find around us.
- Interpret ways in which political ideas are represented in design.
- Discuss how design affects individual and collective orientations towards power, authority, community, and freedom.
- Synthesize new ideas for (re)design that supports sustainable, democratic structures and enables human dignity.

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes Assessment Measures:

- [20%] Active participation in class: All students are required to make regular, sustained, thoughtful contributions to class discussions and to contribute to building a classroom culture that is respectful, stimulating, and supportive. **COVID-19 Note: If you feel sick, stay home!**
- [10%] Leading discussion one class period: Pairs of students will be responsible for guiding and structuring class discussion of the assigned readings for 60 minutes once during the semester. Your main goal in this assignment is to orchestrate a rich, fruitful discussion. Therefore, you will have to read the assigned texts and come up with a list of provocative questions to help guide, nurture, and stimulate the conversation of your classmates. By 12 noon on the day prior to the class meeting, the discussion leaders should post to the class Discord 6-10 proposed discussion questions and a list of terms/concepts (with definitions) that are important for understanding the text. I will provide feedback and suggestions in time for you to make changes before the class meets. Some questions should be factual, asking fellow students to summarize main point(s) of the reading or to ponder the usage of key terms/concepts. The others should be open-ended, asking students to share their interpretations and opinions of the reading. Your grade for this assignment will be based half on the questions and definitions that you prepare and half on your facilitation of the discussion. More guidance for this assignment will be provided in class.
- [20%] Journal reflections: You will maintain a weekly reading response journal and turn it in for review at designated intervals. For this assignment, you should not worry about achieving a “proper” or “elegant” writing style. Use your own voice. The goals are analysis, communication, and making connections among issues, theories, data, and experiences. The length is up to you, but you should aim to write at least 300 words each week that respond directly to the assigned readings. **You will hand in your journal (all entries) on the following dates: 9/27, 10/25, and 11/22.** Each entry should have a title and a date. Each time you hand in the journal, include the previous journal entries, including my comments, so that there is an ongoing conversation in the document. I recommend that you use Google Docs for this assignment so that you can maintain one continuous document and allow me to comment on it.
- [5%] 60-second pitch for research project: **You will briefly present your research question and its significance to the class on 10/7** using the format proposed in *The Craft of Research*: “I am studying \_\_\_\_\_\_ (topic) because I want to find out what/why/how \_\_\_\_\_\_ (question) in order to help my reader understand \_\_\_\_\_\_ (significance).”
- [5%] Annotated bibliography and peer review: After reviewing the sources you collected, refine your term paper topic by posing a more specific research question. Turn your preliminary research bibliography into an annotated bibliography that summarizes the value of each source for answering your research question. Eliminate sources that turned out not to be useful. Add sources that you discovered while taking notes on your initial list of sources (e.g. books cited by a source you read). Recommended: organize your annotated bibliography into themes and alphabetize within those themed sections. **We will peer review annotated bibliographies in class on 10/21.**
- [5%] Project outline and revision plan: In a short outline (maximum 1.5 pages), state the argument that you can make with the evidence you have considered so far. Outline the key ideas that support your thesis and cite the sources that provide supporting evidence of each of those points. **We will peer review outlines in class on 11/4, and you will submit your outline and revision plan on 11/11.**
- [5%] Project draft and peer review: Turn in a complete draft of your paper for peer review. Word count should be between 3,500 and 4,000 words (15 double-spaced pages), excluding bibliography. Cite all sources using Chicago author-date style. This draft will be peer reviewed by a classmate. **We will peer review complete drafts in class on 11/18.**
- [10%] 7-minute presentation of final project: Explain your research question, thesis, and the evidence that led you to this conclusion. Listen attentively to questions and feedback of the class. **Presentations will be in class on 12/2, 12/6, and 12/9.**
- [20%] Final project report: **Revise and proofread your paper and turn it in for a final grade on 12/9.**

### Other Course Policies:

**Guidelines for Discussion**

- The purpose of the seminar is not to debate or prove a point but to more deeply understand what the authors are trying to express in the texts.
- Talk to each other, not just to the discussion leader or professor.
- Listen and respond to other statements before introducing a new idea.
- Refer to evidence from the text to support your ideas.
- Ask questions if you do not understand what someone has said, or you can paraphrase what another student has said for clarification (“I think you said this; is that right?”).
- Don’t “put down” the ideas of another student. Without judging the student you disagree with, state your alternate interpretation or ask a follow-up question to help probe or clarify an idea.
- Effective statements or questions to use in discussion:
  - Where does that idea come from in the text?
  - What does this word or phrase mean?
  - Can you say that in another way?
  - Is this what you mean to say...?
  - What do you think the author is trying to say?
  - What else could that mean?
- Pay attention to your “airtime”—how much you have spoken in relation to other students.
- Don’t interrupt.

**Expectations for Writing**

Papers should be typed, double-spaced, 12-point font, Times New Roman, with standard one-inch margins. ### Additional Course Information:

**Inclusive Language Guidelines**

The Department of Science and Technology Studies aspires to create a learning environment in which people of all identities are encouraged to contribute their perspectives to academic discourse. This course has guidelines on language and terminology that aim to foster an inclusive and welcoming environment, strengthen academic writing, enrich discussion, and reflect best professional practices.

Gender-inclusive and non-sexist language: Language is gender-inclusive and non-sexist when we use words that affirm and respect how people describe, express, and experience their gender. Gender-inclusive/non-sexist language acknowledges people of any gender (e.g. first-year student versus freshman, chair versus chairman, humankind versus mankind). It also affirms non-binary gender identifications, and recognizes the difference between biological sex and gender expression. Students may share their preferred pronouns and names, and these gender identities and gender expressions should be honored.

Non-racist language: The labels and definitions of racial and ethnic categories vary across history, societies, and even situations. In this classroom, we will avoid racial and ethnic slurs and strive to use words that affirm and respect how people describe, express, and experience their race, ethnicity, ancestry, or cultural heritage. Students may share the terminology that they prefer to describe their racial and ethnic identities, and these preferences should be honored.

Non-ableist language: Ableist language, which devalues people with physical and mental disabilities, permeates everyday language. However, there are many options for replacing common ableist rhetoric with more thoughtful alternatives. You are encouraged to read this guide and reflect on what you are communicating when you use ableist terms. [https://www.autistichoya.com/p/ableist-words-and-terms-to-avoid.html](https://www.autistichoya.com/p/ableist-words-and-terms-to-avoid.html)

**Title IX**

Title IX is a federal law prohibiting sex discrimination. The Title IX section of the Education Amendments Act of 1972 states, “No person in the United States shall, on the basis of sex, be excluded from participation in, be denied the benefits of, or be subjected to discrimination under any education program or activity receiving Federal financial assistance.” Under Title IX, sexual misconduct is considered a form of sex discrimination.

Sexual misconduct includes, but is not limited to: sexual harassment, sexual assault, dating and domestic violence, intimate partner violence, and stalking. Sexual misconduct is not tolerated at Rensselaer, and it is a violation of Institute policy, and state and federal law.

Faculty and other employees are considered mandated reporters when it comes to experiences of interpersonal violence (sexual assault, sexual harassment, dating or domestic violence, and stalking). Disclosures of interpersonal violence must be passed along to Rensselaer’s Title IX Coordinators who can help provide support and academic remedies for students who have been impacted. For more information or to report sexual misconduct, see [https://sexualviolence.rpi.edu/](https://sexualviolence.rpi.edu/).

**Sensitive Topics**

It is my intent that students from all diverse backgrounds and perspectives be well-served by this course, that students' learning needs be addressed both in and out of class, and that the diversity that the students bring to this class be viewed as a resource, strength and benefit. It is my intent to present materials and activities that are respectful of diversity: gender identity, sexuality, disability, age, socioeconomic status, ethnicity, race, nationality, religion, and culture. Your suggestions are encouraged and appreciated. Please let me know ways to improve the effectiveness of the course for you personally, or for other students or student groups.

In this course, we will routinely engage with the social and political contexts relevant to the designs we investigate. For example, we study and work at Rensselaer Polytechnic Institute. Our university sits on a hill in Troy, NY, overlooking the Hudson River within the boundaries of the United States. This river also runs through the ancestral lands of the Mohican and Haudenosaunee people, who call it Muhheakantuck. RPI and the United States sit on Indigenous lands that are central to disputes about sovereignty and settler colonialism ([http://therednation.org/10-point-program](http://therednation.org/10-point-program)). The founder of RPI, Steven van Rensselaer, profited by the displacement of Indigenous peoples from these lands and the enslavement of Africans to work it ([https://poly.rpi.edu/2017/03/22/rensselaers_ties_to_slavery_revealed](https://poly.rpi.edu/2017/03/22/rensselaers_ties_to_slavery_revealed)). We in this course did not choose these circumstances, but this history permeates and shapes our present reality. It is something that we must face and engage.

I will attempt to foster an environment in which each class member is able to hear and respect each other. It is critical that each class member show respect for all worldviews expressed in class. Please let me know if something said or done in the classroom, by either myself or other students, is particularly troubling or causes discomfort or offense. While our intention may not be to cause discomfort or offense, the impact of what happens throughout the course is not to be ignored and is something that I consider to be very important and deserving of attention. If and when this occurs, there are several ways to alleviate some of the discomfort or hurt you may experience:

- Discuss the situation privately with me. I am always open to listening to students’ experiences, and want to work with students to find acceptable ways to process and address the issue.
- Discuss the situation with the class. Chances are there is at least one other student in the class who had a similar response to the material. Discussion enhances the ability for all class participants to have a fuller understanding of context and impact of course material and class discussions.
- Notify me of the issue through another source such as your academic advisor, a trusted faculty member, or a peer. If for any reason you do not feel comfortable discussing the issue directly with me, I encourage you to seek out another, more comfortable avenue to address the issue.
