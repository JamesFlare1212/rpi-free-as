## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

Welcome! This course will examine questions surrounding issues such as the following. (1) The degree to which science allows us to predict and manipulate our environment suggests that our best theories are getting the world right. However, a brief reflection on the history of science shows that many successful theories were discarded. Should we believe that our current best scientific theories are approximately true, or will future theories look substantially different? (2) Scientists categorize species, chemical elements and phenomena of various kinds. Should we think our classification schemes depend on how humans happen to cognize the world, or can we say that these divisions represent real categories in the world? (3) Science is thought to yield objective knowledge. Yet which research gets funded and how observations are interpreted inevitably introduces human interests and biases. In what sense then is scientific knowledge objective? Has male bias influenced science, and could there be a more feminist science? (4) With scientific and technological progress come new risks and uncertainties, and with new risks and uncertainties comes regulatory policy. What should be the role of science in the policies of a democratic government? (5) Scientific theories ought to explain why certain things are the way they are. But what is an explanation? (6) Should we think that the task of science is to tell us about the underlying structure of the world or to devise useful models and tools for prediction? (7) What is a scientific theory? What makes a theory scientific? What is a theory? (8) Do scientists look for causes? What even is a cause?

## Course Goals

### Goals:

1. Students should develop an explicit rather than tacit understanding of the philosophical problems concerning the procedures, practices, methodology and fundamental assumptions of sciences.
2. Students should be able to develop some views about the philosophical and moral issues of scientific research. While some of these problems are very abstract, others present (almost daily) dilemmas and so students ought to leave the course better prepared to think effectively about these problems.
3. Develop the ability to communicate, in both oral and written form, clear and effective argumentation.
4. Distinguish and evaluate different philosophical arguments.
5. Be able to plan and work with other people on abstract and difficult projects.

### Additional Academic Integrity Course Policy and Penalty Information:

Collaboration on all assignments for this class is encouraged. As always, your writing must reflect your own thoughts unless you are quoting others.

Student-teacher relationships are built on trust. For example, students must trust that teachers have made appropriate decisions about the structure and content of the courses they teach, and teachers must trust that the assignments that students turn in are their own. Acts that violate this trust undermine the educational process. The Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities defines various forms of Academic Dishonesty and you should make yourself familiar with these. In this class, all assignments that are turned in for a grade must represent the student’s own work. In cases where help was received, or teamwork was allowed, a notation on the assignment should indicate your collaboration. Submission of any assignment that is in violation of this policy will result in a penalty of failure for the course and a letter sent to the Dean for further action. If you have any question concerning this policy before submitting an assignment, please ask for clarification. It is my duty to protect the students who play by the rules. I will penalize all cases of discovered cheating to the fullest extent I possibly can. Remember, it is easier to catch cheating than most students think.

Among the requirements for both the student and the instructor in this course is that we do our best to respect the dignity of all persons and ourselves. Words or deeds directed against a person or group where it is obvious that the sole intent is to insult or otherwise do them harm will not be tolerated. In a similar vein, we should do our best to sensitize ourselves to the unfair and derogatory racial, religious, and sexual connotations of various English expressions and eliminate those expressions from casual use. (A guide to gender-fair language can be obtained from the Writing Center and from the Library). Having said this, however, it must also be said that it is crucial to Rensselaer’s mission that there be placed no undue constraint on the free expression of ideas and opinions. Thus, the mere fact that someone considers an expression or act false or offensive is not by itself sufficient to warrant sanction. Our ability to have important conversations presupposes that we have a space where people feel respected and encouraged to exchange ideas, and I will enforce standards of good faith argumentation and discussion to that end. Basically, value, respect, and support each other in our classroom speech. If you tend to use language like, “that’s a pretty dumb idea actually,” expect to be sanctioned by me. Better phrases like, “I see why that’s a tempting idea, but I think it doesn’t work because of this reason” are just superior when it comes to preserving our space for productive conversation. Respecting others doesn’t mean agreeing with them or deflecting with phrases like “I guess you’re entitled to your opinion,” but rather involves an obligation to politely challenge them on ideas that you see as problematic. All of this applies to me as well! In the words of Bill and Ted, let’s be excellent to each other.
