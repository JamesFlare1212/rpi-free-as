## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

Technological innovation is responsible for most of economic growth, and is crucial to deal with major societal concerns. This course examines the economics of innovation and new technologies. It shows how technologies are created and put to use, from basic science through invention and development to diffusion and practical use. Additional topics include R&D determinants, sources of innovation, product improvement, cost reduction, incentivizing and stimulating innovation, patents, evolution of innovation and technology, innovative competition in industry, and the national innovation environment.

**Writing for ECON-4110:**  
Writing is due weekly. You will upload a Word file weekly to our LMS website, by each Friday. (No writings are due during Spring Break or Thanksgiving break.) I may show and discuss writings in class.  
The writings describe content from the course and incorporate your reactions and ideas. Writings may be prose or poetry, science fiction or science. They may be highly mathematical or not at all. You might write in blog form, or as a summary of the scientific literature and your reactions to it. You choose a form for the writing for each class day, and a clear target audience such as policy makers, students, economists, or the general public. The form and target audience may change for each class day. The writing must satisfy these requirements:

1. Make a separate writing entry for each class day. Exclude the Introduction (first class) and Conclusion (last class). Over the semester, two other class days’ content may be skipped to allow for necessary absences.
2. Keep *all your writings in one file*. The one file contains all writings in the whole semester. Download the template file from LMS.
   - 2.1. On the cover page, include your name and (see below) COMM+D appointment dates.
   - 2.2. For each class day, indicate your choice of writing form, target audience, and title.
3. Each class day’s entry must:
   - 3.1. Communicate some or all key content—ideas, findings, and perhaps methods—from that day of the course. This does not need to be essay style; poems and fiction can communicate the same content.
   - 3.2. Include your own ideas or reflections for that day of the course. Include policy ideas for at least some class days.
4. Cite your sources and include a list of references.
   - 4.1. Citations are the brief mentions of sources in the main text, and may indicate specific pages or sections. References are information about articles, books, and other sources so that readers can find the original sources.
   - 4.2. As a default, use the Chicago author-date citation and reference style. See for example the readings from class topics 3 (Determinants of innovation: Supply push), 5 (Determinants of innovation: Firm size & concentration), 8 (Innovation incentives: The case against patents, real roles of patents), and 11 (Cost reduction & its exploitation). Or use footnotes or endnotes if they would better fit a writing style such as poetry or fiction.
   - 4.3. In citations, include specific page numbers if the content you use comes from a particular part of a book or paper.
   - 4.4. For reference lists, I encourage you to use bibliographic software such as Zotero, Mendeley, or EndNote, though it will take an hour or more to get used to the software.
5. Quote and cite.
   - 5.1. For text or figures copied from any source, quote the text and cite the source.
   - 5.2. For information used from a source, even if you do not use exact text or figures, cite the source.
   - 5.3. Text quoted from a source must be in quotation marks (or clearly indicated as quoted text by using an indented block), with a citation with specific page numbers. Use ellipses … for omitted text, and square brackets [] for your own clarifications inside quotations.
   - 5.4. If you copy a table or a figure, cite where *you* obtained the table or figure, with specific page numbers. (Students often want to cite where the table or figure originated, and it is fine to indicate the original source, but you must cite where *you* got it.)
   - 5.5. If you do not follow these standard practices, you could be accused of plagiarism, and get in trouble. This matters for all your classes, and outside of universities too!
6. Improve prior weeks’ work. Improvements may be in reaction to comments of the instructor (and TA if any), other students’ thoughts, and your own insights and editing. Part of the writing grade is based on how well you revise your prior work.
   - 6.1. Use Track Changes in Word to document improvements to prior weeks’ work. Please turn *off* Word’s Track Changes feature just before writing a new entry, and please turn *on* Word’s Track Changes feature when making changes to an entry you wrote previously. That way, I (and the TA if any) can see what you changed. (In Microsoft Word, the Review tab has a switch that shows whether Track Changes is on or off.) If you cannot use Word for some reason, please discuss with me (or the TA) to find a workable alternative.
7. Total at least 4,000 words by the end of the semester.
   - 7.1. This is a rule of communication-intensive courses at Rensselaer.
   - 7.2. However, please don’t make your writing wordy just to fill space, nor leave in content that deserves to be cut!
   - 7.3. Other than this, there is no specific length requirement.
8. Vary your genre, and your target audience, during the semester. For example, you may write a federal policy report to policy makers, a blog to the general public, advice to a company’s top management, fiction, poetry, a cartoon guide for beginners, etc. Use at least two or three different genres, and at least two different target audiences.

Submit your writing, including revisions, each Friday.  
The Comm+D Center at Rensselaer provides feedback to improve your writing. It is a requirement to:

9\. Submit, at least once by Feb. 1, some of your weekly writing to Comm+D to receive feedback on your writing. Use the Comm+D feedback to make improvements to your previous and future writing. At the bottom of the cover page of your cumulative writing document, list the date(s) when you met for Comm+D appointments.

Using Comm+D multiple times will generally improve your writing grade, because I will check the dates and because the feedback will help your writing.  
Feedback and grading: I expect to provide feedback on writing throughout the semester in a shared way, so everyone can benefit from comments and so we learn to write well together. Individualized feedback and grade advice may be given as feasible. The final writing grade will determined at the end of the semester. The grade will reflect how much of the writing you complete—write about all class days except the Introduction and Conclusion, except you may drop two days—and how well you do in the writing. For a good grade, I expect substantive writings that are on-topic, fun, and interesting. I expect you to fix problems discussed in class and in writing handouts, including in sections you wrote earlier. So when I tell the class to replace “in order to” with “to,” you should replace it in your past writings as well as your future writings. I expect to see many edits that improve your prior work.

**Leading Class Discussions:**  
Once during the semester, groups of 2, or if needed 3, students will lead a class discussion of about 20-30 minutes. I will randomly assign people to groups. Please help each other prepare in advance, to plan the discussion. Each member of the group must spend equal time directing the discussion. This is *not* a presentation. It is leading the discussion to help ensure everyone learns from our class reading.  
When leading the discussion:

- Do the reading a week in advance.
- Make a list of major points. The goal is to help ensure your fellow students (a) understand and remember key points, and (b) talk through important and potentially controversial issues.
- Think of ways to help ensure students understand and remember key points. You might create and distribute/show mnemonics (memory aids) to help remember the key points. You might give out a practice quiz with answers. You might give out an example and practice for any key mathematics, or for statistical interpretation.
- Think of ways to guide the discussion on important and controversial issues. What questions should be discussed by the class, and how can you ask people about them and still keep the discussion on-topic? Are some issues obvious and some not? What alternative views are there, besides the view in the reading? If no class members think of an important point, outlook, or controversy, how can you bring it up?
- Email me two quiz questions on your reading, with answers, at least 48 hours in advance. The questions should be fill-in-the-blank or multiple-choice or very-short-answer, and should test people’s skills on main content of the reading. Answers should be hard to guess for people who did not do the reading or participate actively, but reasonably easy to get right for people who did the reading and learned the relevant class content. Make your questions clear and unambiguous. I may edit or change quiz questions as needed.
- Discussion leaders’ grade for that class’s quiz questions will be based on the discussion-leading and quiz questions. Discussion leader group members may receive different grades. Please keep your discussion-leading time about equal; I will grade partly on your effort to keep the discussion-leading time about equal.
