## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

The objectives of this course are to expose students to aspects of *chemical crystallography* and to provide a working knowledge of a Rigaku Synergy-S 4-axis dual-source X-ray diffractometer for the purpose of *small-molecule* single-crystal structure determination by X-ray diffraction experiments. While the course was created by a synthetic inorganic/organometallic chemist and all examples will involve small molecules (as opposed to ionic solids or proteins), appreciation of travel symmetry, space group symmetry, atomic form factors, Bragg’s Law, Laue equations, reciprocal space, Miller Indices, structure factors, and methods of crystal growing, selection, mounting, centering and data collection translate to all applications of crystallography.

## Course Content

### Content Details:

Students completing the course will demonstrate the following knowledge or competence:

**Travel Symmetry** – Students will appreciate symmetry elements and point group of a molecule, as well as recognize that *translational symmetry* is the characteristic feature that defines crystalline solids. Students will learn about point symmetry operations, e.g., inversion centers, mirror planes, rotation and roto-inversion axes found in molecules and crystals, appreciating symmetry in both Hermann-Mauguin and Schoenflies classification systems. We will examine travel symmetry operations, e.g., glide planes and screw axes, and combine it with point symmetry to create three-dimensional *space group* symmetry. This understanding permits the student to appreciate the *Z*-value (or multiplicity of the general position), as well as the asymmetric unit (*Z’*).

**Diffraction theory** – Students will consider the elastic scattering of X-rays first by a single object/electron, and then by atoms. In the process, they will see how the *form factors* that describe scattering by atoms change with atomic number and scattering angle. We will derive *Bragg's Law*, which gives the angle at which the scattered X-rays constructively interfere if the X-rays are reflected from parallel planes in a crystal. and consider scattering in the forward direction (transmission) of an incoming beam of X-rays by one-, two-, and three-dimensional crystals through *Laue's equations*. This content is required to explain the underlying principles of single-crystal X-ray diffraction experiments.

**Real/Reciprocal Space** – Students will understand the relationship between the real space lattice that defines a crystal structure and *reciprocal space* lattice that gives rise to the observed diffraction pattern of the crystal. We describe how to assign *Miller Indices* to lattice planes and calculate the *d-spacing* between families of parallel planes in real space. This content is required to understand unit cell determination, as it is the location of spots/reflections, i.e., reciprocal space *peak positions*, that tell us about the real lattice upon which the molecular motif is overlaid.

**Atomic Form and Structure Factors** – We examine the factors that determine *peak intensities* of diffraction peaks, focusing mainly on the *structure factor*. Students appreciate the linear absorption coefficient and consider how to use this information when selecting specimens to screen in diffraction experiments. All of the information about where the atoms are located in the unit cell is contained in the *structure factor F(hkl)*, therefore we look at the origins of the structure factor and see how they are calculated.

**Operation of Rigaku Synergy-S Single-Crystal X-ray Diffractometer** – Students will learn the basic functions of operation of a Rigaku Synergy-S 4-axis dual-source X-ray diffractometer. The following sections will each have an associated *lab practicum*: (1) manipulating, shaping, and selecting proper crystals, (2) mounting and centering crystals, (3) screening crystals and collecting unit cell data with appropriate X-ray radiation and temperature, (4) searching the Cambridge Structural Database (CSD) for matching cells, (5) data collection strategies, (6) structure solution, (7) structure refinement and common problems.

### Additional Academic Integrity Course Policy and Penalty Information:

**Academic Integrity**

**You are expected to work independently and without collaboration** on homework and quizzes. One (1) instance of unacceptable collaboration or plagiarism will result in a reduction of the final course grade by one (1) letter grade (ex. B to C). Another instance of academic dishonesty will result in failure of the course. Plagiarism and “cheating” is taken very seriously. Engaging in either robs others of their work, has a permanent impact on your credibility as a scientist, and may severely affect your academic performance and future career.

The relationship between students and faculty is based upon trust and the continued maintenance of this trust is necessary for education to be successful. Students need to trust faculty to make appropriate judgments about the content and structure of the course. Faculty members need to trust that the work turned in by students represents their own effort. Violation of this trust undermines the educational process. In addition, as professionals, you may be designing products upon which lives depend, as a result, there is no tolerance for breach of academic integrity such as cheating or plagiarizing. The Rensselaer Student Handbook and the Dean of Students Office Webpage (http://doso.rpi.edu/update.do?artcenterkey=676) defines various forms of academic dishonesty. Please familiarize yourself with these and ask us if you have questions about what constitutes academic dishonesty.
