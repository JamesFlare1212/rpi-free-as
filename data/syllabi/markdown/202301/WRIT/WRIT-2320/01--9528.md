## Learning Outcomes

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes (CLOs):

- Students will demonstrate knowledge of creative nonfiction writing in general and the subgenres in particular by utilizing appropriate terminology to analyze and discuss published essays.
- Students will establish a consistent creative practice by regularly producing short creative writings.
- Students will demonstrate engagement in the writing process through observing, drafting, peer critique, and revision.
- Students will actively engage in a creative community of peers through participation in workshop and discussion.
- Students will synthesize learning outcomes by constructing a learning portfolio that showcases their skills and development in the field of creative nonfiction writing over the course of the semester.

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes Assessment Measures:

**Reading.** In this course, you will learn to read like a writer, which is substantially different than the reading you may be used to in English classes. The expectation is that as you engage with these texts you are “looking behind the curtain” to determine how the artistic effects were achieved and preparing to discuss your theories with others. Reading should be an active endeavor.

**Short Exercises (SE).** These are short creative writing prompts designed to give you practice in one particular skill. There is no minimum length so long as the prompt is thoughtfully addressed. Maximum length is one double-spaced page. Short exercises should be posted on the weekly SE board.

**Craft Discussion Boards.** These approximate the kinds of thoughtful, initial comments about a text you might make if we were all in the same room. The purpose is to help you organize your thoughts before class and to get discussions started in a remote environment (which can be awkward). You can think of these as brief educated reading reflections (as opposed to informal opinions or product reviews, for example) that set the tone for fruitful in-class discussions. These are not essays and no thesis is required. The length requirements here are flexible; I am simply interested in seeing you engage with the texts using course terminology and frameworks and hearing your questions and ideas. The writing style need not be formal, but it must engage with the reading in a way that opens up into larger discussions. All students will make an initial post in response to a prompt. **Please post by class start time (EST) on the date listed on the assignment schedule.** Students who do not attend a given class are required to make an initial post and reply to two peers on the board within 24 hours (barring emergencies). Students who struggle to participate orally in class may also decide to write replies to peers. Students should be prepared to elaborate on their post in class. Discussion boards will be assessed as Provocative (P), Substantial (S), Emergent (E), or Unsatisfactory (U). See rubric at the end of this syllabus. You will earn grades in this category based on the quality of the majority of posts: Majority P = A; Majority S = B; Majority E = C; Majority U = F.

**Workshop.** Instruction in this course follows the guidelines established by the Association of Writers and Writing Programs (AWP) for the teaching of creative writing, which include a “challenging writers’ workshop” as a hallmark. They define this as*…a seminar in which students critique one another’s work under the mentorship of an accomplished writer-teacher. The workshop is writing intensive, offering each student multiple opportunities for submission and revision of creative work.* This method of instruction is considered the gold standard for developing writers at all levels of expertise, and workshopping is a key learning tool. Workshopping teaches you to read and respond to written work from a variety of perspectives, and hearing critique of your own writing will help you understand how successfully your work achieves your goals.  
**End Comments.** Good feedback can be short or long, so I won't impose an arbitrary length on yours. Your stance should be that of a careful and interested reader. You're making suggestions or observations the writer can take into the next draft or into other pieces. In my feedback, I will assume your work is a draft in progress and gear my comments toward improvements you can make in future drafts, even though you may not actually choose to rewrite that particular piece. Feedback is most helpful if it makes specific points. It's least helpful when it deals in generalizations.

**Observation Journal.** This is simply a place for your to record your observations about the world around you, which includes your internal landscape (Note: this is not a diary though—stick to observations, such as “my eyes squinch whenever I hear my sister on the stairs”). I strongly suggest you write in your observation journal a minimum of three times a week. Schedule a time and place to write, preferably while enjoying some sunshine. Include snippets of dialogue, engagement with the five senses, descriptions of your emotional landscape, memories, and patterns you see in the world around you. We will regularly share our observations as a way to touch base. I may ask to see selected entries as part of in-class activities. Other than that, these are for your benefit. You will include your favorite observations in your final creative portfolio.

**Creative Portfolio**:Rather than producing a major new work at semester’s end, you will instead compile and revise a portfolio of work that you feel best represents your learning this semester, as reflected in the learning outcomes listed on this syllabus.

- ### Academic Integrity

  Student-teacher relationships are built on trust. For example, students must trust that teachers have made appropriate decisions about the structure and content of the courses they teach, and teachers must trust that the assignments that students turn in are their own. Acts that violate this trust undermine the educational process. The Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities and The Graduate Student Supplement define various forms of Academic Dishonesty and you should make yourself familiar with these. In this class, all assignments that are turned in for a grade must represent the student’s own work. In cases where help was received, or teamwork was allowed, a notation on the assignment should indicate your collaboration.

  Violations of academic integrity may also be reported to the appropriate Dean (Dean of Students for undergraduate students or the Dean of Graduate Education for graduate students, respectively).

  If you have any question concerning this policy before submitting an assignment, please ask for clarification. In addition, you can visit the following site for more information on our Academic Integrity Policy: [Students Rights, Responsibilities, and Judicial Affairs](https://info.rpi.edu/dean-students/student-rights-responsibilities-and-judicial-affairs).

### Additional Academic Integrity Course Policy and Penalty Information:

All assignments that are turned in for a grade must represent the student’s own work completed for this class in particular. If you have a compelling reason for wanting to use previous work of yours for the class, it must be substantially and fundamentally revised, and you must get approval from the instructor first.

Submission of any assignment that is in violation of this policy will result in a penalty of a zero for the assignment and the potential to fail the course. Second offenses will result in automatic failure of the course and the potential for disciplinary action by the institution.

If you have any question concerning this policy before submitting an assignment, please ask for clarification.
