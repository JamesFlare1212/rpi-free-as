## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

This course explores different genres of written communication employed in a variety of situations. Emphasis is placed on utilizing communication strategies that are tailored to both the audience and the context in which the communication takes place. The ethical implications of communication within a specific environment are also considered. This course develops a process approach to writing, which moves from planning through revision and final editing.

## Course Text(s)

## Course Goals

### Goals:

The primary goal of this course is to help you communicate in ways that actually make something happen through using narrative as a way to influence people’s actions, beliefs, and understanding. More specifically, the course is designed to improve your ability to:

- Communicate with a variety of *real audiences* in different written and spoken generic forms.
- Achieve a *variety of purposes* (provide perspective on an experience, analyze and review something, and persuasively argue your position on an issue).
- Incorporate visuals (graphs, visual images, page/screen design) effectively into your written and oral presentations.
- Practice creating and making effective oral presentations and being attentive and aware listeners.
- Assess and provide feedback on your own writing and the writing of others.
- Practice writing, speaking, and listening regularly and develop self-confidence in these skills as a life-long communicator.
- Become aware of the choices you’re making as a user of both Standard Academic English and English for everyday use.

## Learning Outcomes

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes (CLOs):

- • Analyze your audience. Identify specific knowledge, needs, emotions, values, and experiences that are characteristic of your audience, and how those experiences are both individual and social-cultural in origin.
- • Engage your audience. Show your audience how your topic matters to them, especially by showing how your topic relates to what the audience knows, cares about, wonders about, etc. (i.e. to consider things your audience takes as “givens”)
- • Explain your ideas in ways that reflect a thorough understanding of the subject and your audience’s needs, knowledge, values, etc. by anticipating and answering the audience’s likely questions and elaborating appropriately
- • Organize texts effectively, especially by using specific “moves” in most formal writing: getting directly to the point in answering audience’s likely questions, creating and fulfilling expectations, moving from given to new information.
- • Create an appropriate voice, not only in the words you choose but also in the kinds of questions you answer or avoid, and the kinds of information you choose to include and exclude.
- • Incorporate visual information effectively.
- • Practice and hone your speaking and listening communication skills in both one-on-one, small group, and large group settings.
- • Reflect on your communication practices and their growth/development over the course of the semester.
- • Begin to establish a toolkit of “practices” that you can take with you for your reading, writing, speaking, and listening practices.

## **Guided In-Class Writing Practices**

At the beginning of each class period, we’re going to engage in a writing exercise/practice that encourages us to engage with your inner audience, speaker, and listener. These in-class writings will serve as entry points/sparks/challenges to help stretch your creative process, and prepare you for and potentially help polish your formal assignments. **While I will not be assessing these writing practices on a scheduled basis (or even collecting them), *they* *WILL be a major part of your final reflection assignment.***

## **Informal Assignments** (5% of final grade)

Informal assignments will take various forms. They may include submitting a short piece of writing, bringing material that you will use in completing a formal assignment, completing an improvisational activity via email, or short in-class writing assignments. These informal writing assignments will be graded out of three points, depending on the thoughtfulness and quality of what you turn in. If you have room for improvement on one or two of these assignments, your final grade will not be hurt too much. **Please take note, though:** A string of 3/3s will significantly help your final grade, whereas a string of 1/3s may negatively impact your final grade. **You will be well-informed of the assignment format, mode, and due date for turning in each informal assignment via the Course Schedule.**

## **Formal Assignments**

**1) Telling a Story** (20% of final grade)

*No one else sees the world the way you do, so no one else can tell the stories that you have to tell. – Charles de Lint*

This assignment requires that you tell a story that you could submit to The Moth Radio Hour storytelling program [https://themoth.org]. It should be a story that gives your audience an *insider’s perspective* on an experience that has shaped who you are/how you understand the world/how you interact with others (or yourself). You’ll tell your story for an audience of people who could benefit from or learn from hearing your story. (In this case, your audience is our classroom community. You may also consider submitting this to the McKinney Writing Contest!) *You will share the “kernel” of your story with me and your peer writing group to receive feedback before you begin to write. In the end, though, you will be the one to decide what story you want to tell depending on your level of comfort, courage, and vulnerability.*

The assignment is also shaped on The Moth’s criteria and expectations. You can learn more about the organization/show on their website, and they offer storytelling tips [https://themoth.org/share-your-story/storytelling-tips-tricks] as well.

*One difference* is that while The Moth does *not* allow you to use notes or read from a paper, I will. However, you should still practice enough in order to just reference your text during your story telling. For this assignment, I’m asking that you first ***write*** the story (but write it as you want to **tell it aloud**) and *then* tell the story during a 2-day Listening Party in class. You will have ample opportunity to “practice” reading your work aloud during peer writing groups; it is also possible to practice your story with a tutor at Comm+D by making an appointment

**2) Cover Letter & Resume** (20% of final grade)

*When I go through hundreds of applications from people who all have very similar-sounding experience, cover letters are the only glimpse I have into a person’s personality. – Sophia Amoruso*

*Emphasize your strengths on your resume, in your cover letters and in your interviews. It may sound obvious, but you’d be surprise how many people simply list everything they’ve ever done. Convey your passion and link your strengths to measurable results. Employers and interviewers love concrete data. – Marcus Buckingham*

Applying for jobs, internships, research assistantships, and other professional endeavors is an important skill you’ll need to practice both during your time at RPI and afterward. For this project, you’ll be choosing an *actual position to apply* to. We’ll be learning how to use visual design concepts to create a clean, polished resume, and how to communicate your experience/research in ways that your future employer/director/supervisor will be excited to read! The long-term goal is to have a resume that you can return to and update as you need, and a cover letter template to revise as necessary for different positions/contexts/opportunities.

**3) Problem/Solution Project** (25% of final grade – 5% for project; 10% for presentation)

*Don’t raise your voice, improve your argument. – Desmond Tutu*

*Persuasion is often more effectual than force. – Aesop*

For this project, you’ll be proposing your own project that starts with asking questions about a topic you’re passionate about wanting to change either in school or “real” life. You’ll be choosing your audience for your project based on who will actually be able to make an effective change to answer the question/solve the problem/change the state of things.

We’ll be working through this project in phases in order to strengthen several skills you’ll use throughout your academic and professional endeavors: **researching** using databases, developing **personal archiving** practices, using **citation styles** to give credit to outside sources, **analyzing and evaluating** outside sources as they relate to your major **claims** (points, opinions, thesis), and **synthesizing** those sources. We’ll also be working on using **data** (evidence, facts, statistics, expert testimony, concrete details) to support **warrants** (reasons/answers) based on the **three basic methods** of persuasion—logos (logic), ethos (ethics), and pathos (emotion) to help others understand the value and importance of your project. *You will be required to use between **4-6 outside sources** (including at least ONE multimodal source–images, photographs, sound bites, tables, charts, etc.) for this project. You’ll be required to practice the citation style most used in your major.*

You will also be presenting your project to the class in 3-Minute Thesis style, including a *single, static PowerPoint slide*. The important part of this project is to be able to explain your project clearly to a non-specialized audience in 3 minutes. No more, no less. More information will be provided about this portion of the assignment later.

**4) Final Reflection** (20% of final grade)

For your final project, you will be curating a text presentation of how you have individually met the learning outcomes for the course. There will be more details forthcoming in an extended assignment sheet and rubric, but here is the gist:

You’ll choose 10 unique “entries” from your archives (in-class writing practices, informal writing assignments, peer feedback, etc.) to showcase how you, as an individual, have met each of the ten (10) learning outcomes and how you have progressed as a communicator over the course of fifteen weeks. You’ll take screenshots/photos of this “evidence” and include captions to explain how each entry meets each objective.

In addition to these entries, you’ll reflect on your individual experience in the course as a whole. You’ll have the opportunity to share how you’ve progressed/grown/evolved as a writer, reader, listener, and speaker (and learner in general!) through suggested prompts and questions.

## Grading Criteria

### Criteria Details:

To help you achieve the goals and outcomes for this course, I will introduce you to research and theory about narrative structures, metacognition, mindful attention/awareness, audience, persuasion, and visual rhetoric. Based on the understanding of the theoretical background that supports this course, you’ll learn strategies you can use to communicate effectively with your audience for any communication practice—oral, written, or visual—in any situation, both in classrooms and in your career. These strategies are not algorithms or recipes that can be followed blindly: They are actions and choices people routinely take and make—sometimes consciously, habitually, and/or intuitively—in communicating effectively. In your assignments, therefore, you’re expected to:

- Demonstrate generic conventions and audience-centered communication
- Organize communication coherently and cohesively
- Produce grammatically and syntactically appropriate written and oral communication (including using Standard Academic English when appropriate and warranted)
- Incorporate visual information effectively
- Appraise the communication of peers
- Revise texts as part of the writing process and in response to peer and instructor feedback
- Construct claims in a variety of communication formats
- Select appropriate arguments, evidence, formats, and language to support those claims
- Maintain academic honesty and citate your sources fairly and correctly
- Reflect on your own work in terms of the learning outcomes

In grading all of your work, I’ll ask four basic questions:

- How do you identify the audience? What tactics are you using to engage that audience?
- How do you address that audience clearly and specifically, and take into account the context in which you are writing?
- How does everything in your work (narrative, details, language, visuals) create a clear, cohesive piece given the nature of your subject, the audience, and the context of the communication?
- How is your work designed with both intention and impact in mind?

For each formal assignment, I’ll provide a detailed rubric for more specific criteria that will help you understand expectations and how to meet them.

At any point, you can request a breakdown of your grade, which I will respond to within **48 hours.** Rensselear uses an institute-wide grading scale that will be used in this course. ### Additional Course Information:

## **Center for Communication+Design (Comm+D)**

The Center for Global Communication+Design (Comm+D) is a FREE resource for all members of the Rensselaer community: students, faculty, and staff. You can make appointments or utilize their online resources by visiting the Comm+D website. Comm+D is staffed by experienced graduate and undergraduate tutors who will work with you, one-on-one to discuss:

- ANY type of communication project – written texts, oral presentations, websites, visual elements
- For ANY purpose – classwork, research publications, personal projects, etc.
- From ANY discipline
- At ANY stage in the process – from brainstorming and hatching plans, to revising after receiving feedback (or before submitting for feedback!), to asking questions about citation styles, to polishing your final product.
