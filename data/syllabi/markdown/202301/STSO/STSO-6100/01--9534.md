## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

This course is an advanced introduction to “the policy-relevant side of STS” (Hess 2012). We will examine three broad topics:

1. How states shape scientific research and the development of technologies and infrastructures.
2. How legislators and regulatory bureaucracies use scientific knowledge and expert opinion in decision-making processes.
3. How publics, particularly social movements, engage in political processes that are dominated by scientific and technological experts.

## Learning Outcomes

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes (CLOs):

- Critique and contribute to scholarly conversations in STS about politics and policy.
- Analyze science and technology policy issues.
- Make sense of research funding programs, as relevant to their own dissertation research and to science policy more broadly.

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes Assessment Measures:

**Readings and Discussion.** This is a discussion-centered course, and all students will be expected to actively lead and participate in discussions about the assigned readings. Each week, students will sign up to introduce the articles/chapters that are assigned. These introductions to the readings should be about 5 minutes long, and include the following:

1. Brief information about the author and the context in which they wrote the piece.
2. A quotation that sums up the key idea of the piece.
3. A question to initiate discussion. Please note that a discussion question is an open-ended question that is likely to prompt diverse responses from the participants in this course. Discussion questions should be grounded in specific passages from the readings (e.g., “on page 25, the author says…”) but should provoke students to think beyond the text (e.g. compare to another text, apply to a relevant example, evaluate from a different perspective, reflect on the broader themes connecting the assigned readings).

**Dissertation Logic of Inquiry Analysis.** Further details about this assignment will be provided in class. The first objective of this assignment is to read a book about science and technology policy that is based on a dissertation. A list is provided below, but you may suggest others - there are many good books based on dissertation research! Please note that many of these books have undergone extensive revision and include research done after the dissertation. You can usually figure that out by reading the acknowledgements.

The second objective of this assignment is to analyze, in a 600-1000 word essay, the author’s logic of inquiry (e.g. inductive vs. deductive approach, research design, process of analysis, etc.). I hope that this assignment will help you to think about your own dissertation projects and improve your capacity to offer methodological critiques of research in STS. You will briefly present this paper in class.

**Frame Analysis.** The first objective of this project is to familiarize yourself with a science and technology policy issue of your choice. The second objective is to learn how to conduct a frame analysis. You will first read a chapter called “Frames: Beyond Facts Versus Values” by Willem Halffman and “Framing Climate Change” by Mike Hulme. Then you will peruse the online magazine *Issues in Science and Technology* to find a topic that you wish to examine. Select at least three texts related to this topic (they could be articles in *Issues in S&T,* or other sources) to use as the basis for a frame analysis. Write an analysis, in 1200-1500 words, of the frames that are in play in relation to your chosen issue.

Bonus! If you would like to learn more about frame analysis, and about the construction of "problems" more broadly, the person to read is Carol Bacchi. Feel free to explore the following, which I will include in the readings folder:

- Bacchi, Carol, and Susan Goodwin. *Poststructural policy analysis: A guide to practice*. Springer, 2016.
- Bacchi, Carol. "Drug problematizations and politics: Deploying a poststructural analytic strategy." *Contemporary Drug Problems* 45.1 (2018): 3-14.

**Special Issue Commentary.** The first objective of this project is to read a policy-relevant “special issue” of one of the major STS journals (see list below). The second objective is to write a commentary about the articles in the journal issue, drawing on the knowledge that you have acquired in this course. This commentary should be in the range of 3000-5000 words and should be written as though it were to be published as the concluding chapter of the special issue. It should convey your unique perspective on the articles, their unifying themes, and their connections to the broader field of policy-relevant STS. You will give a short talk based on your commentary in class.

## Week 4 –US Science Funding - Meeting January 31

Go to Grants.gov and carry out the following investigation:

1. Examine the search options (check boxes). What are the different types of funding instruments? Who is eligible for different types of funding? Can you tell which agencies are supporting scientific research?
2. Examine the research funding opportunities (past and present) by funding agency (e.g. Department of Defense, Department of Agriculture, NASA, etc.). Use check boxes to narrow down the categories to focus on science and technology research. How do these funding agencies differ in their research funding priorities? Do their calls for proposals have any distinctive characteristics?
3. Use the search function to identify funding agencies and programs that may support research on a topic that interests you (e.g. “endangered species,” “online privacy,” “gender”). Read the funding opportunities carefully. What are the priorities? How do you think these calls for proposals were written? Do you observe any absences or silences in these calls for proposals? What opportunities do they offer for an STS scholar like you?

Now focus on the National Science Foundation, a key source of funding for STS research. Read about the historical context for the creation of NSF, its subsequent shifts, and the STS program there:

Kevles, Daniel J. "The National Science Foundation and the debate over postwar research policy, 1942-1945: A political interpretation of *Science--The endless frontier*." *Isis* 68, no. 1 (1977): 5-26.

Mirowski, P., and E-M. Sent. "The Commercialization of Science and the Response of STS." *Hackett, Edward J.; Amsterdamska, Olga; Lynch, Michael (ed.), Handbook of Science and Technology Studies* (2008): 635-689.

Hackett, Edward J. "Trends and Opportunities in Science and Technology Studies: A View from the National Science Foundation." In *Science, Technology, and Society*, pp. 277-291. Springer, Dordrecht, 2000.

Finally, go to https://beta.nsf.gov/funding/opportunities/science-technology-studies-sts and click the button to browse projects funded by the STS program at NSF. Read at least five project abstracts. How do these scholars explain the public value of their work? What is the “intellectual merit” and what are the “broader impacts” of each project?

### Additional Academic Integrity Course Policy and Penalty Information:

Any instances of plagiarism will result in a failing grade for the assignment. Repeated instances of academic dishonesty will be grounds for failing the course. Plagiarism includes purchasing term papers; copying or handing in the writing of another student (current or former); using sentences verbatim from a published source without appropriate referencing (when in doubt, cite the source); and presenting as one’s own the detailed argument of a published source. “Recycling” papers written in other courses is also forbidden.
