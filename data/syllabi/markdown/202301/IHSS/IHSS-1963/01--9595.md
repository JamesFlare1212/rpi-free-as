## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

**This course will provide a comprehensive introduction to the field of Cultural psychology- an interdisciplinary study of how culture reflects and shapes the mind and behavior of its members. It is an exploration of the human mind, and of one’s own mind, by thinking through the ideas and practices of other peoples and their cultures. Students examine evidence of cross-cultural similarities and differences in mind, self, emotion, and morality with special reference the way cultural traditions and social practices regulate, express, and transform the human psyche – the understanding of which is closely linked to understanding what is happening in the institutional systems in the United States.** **In the course we also examine evidence-based practices for improving the overall well-being of cross-cultural communities, as well as an analysis of policies or practices that impact social, cultural, environmental, and economic outcomes.**

**The four goals of psychology can also be effectively applied to study cultural psychology by describing, explaining, predicting, and controlling (influencing) behavior across cultures. Cultural psychology research informs several fields within psychology, including social psychology, developmental psychology, and cognitive psychology. Incorporating a cultural perspective in psychological research helps to ensure that the knowledge we learn is more accurate and descriptive of all people.**

## Course Goals

### Goals:

- Students will learn about human development and how it varies (and remains the same in some ways) across a variety of cultures.
- Students will Learn about interactions between culture and one's sense of self, identity, and self-esteem. In some cultures, such as the dominant culture in the USA, independence and individuality are emphasized. In many other cultures, and in many place across Asia, an interdependent sense of self is fostered, in which a person's identity is rooted in one's social roles, group obligations, and group memberships. Family, village, clan, and society are more important in interdependent cultures than are individual traits, preferences, or goals.
- Students will explore how culture affects perception, memory, math abilities, problem solving, creativity, dialectical thinking, consciousness, and intelligence.
- Students will examine how linguistic differences shape and result from cultural forces.
- Students will explore the challenges of understanding how culture influences the physical and psychological health of people across cultures, along with gaining insight into some culture-bound syndromes.
- Students also examine cultural differences within the United States in relation to the psychological disorders experienced by ethnic minorities, migrants, and refugees. It is clear that psychology is not equally accessible to all people across cultures, or even within the United States.

## Valuing Diversity

This course is intended to reach students from diverse backgrounds and perspectives - all are welcome here. The diversity students bring to class, across gender, sexuality, age, disability, socio-economic status, ethnicity, race, religion, and learning needs is valued as an important asset and strength in the learning process.

What to expect from this class:

I will not “teach” via lectures. This is a discussion-based class. In other words, instead of lectures, you will have an opportunity to share knowledge via small/large group discussion, role-playing, general comments, and class activities. To optimize students’ learning, I use several methods of instruction. We will discuss the assigned readings, talk about why researchers conducted the research we read about, consider what they could have done better and what they did right, and think about possible next steps that could be taken. We will also discuss implications, future directions, and possible contradictions among the findings and ideas. As we discuss the readings, please raise your own points and think about what the research you read about means. Keep in mind that you are expected to read the assigned chapters and journal articles before class. I will have posted several PPTs and videos every week. I expect that you will have watched these videos according to class schedule. If you have not done the required reading and watched the videos, your ability to contribute to the class discussion will be significantly curtailed.

Since race, class, and culture are rarely honestly discussed in our society, this class has the potential to be powerful. The course readings, discussions, presentations, and videos will likely stimulate thought and affect.

During this course almost all students will experience anger, anxiety, fear, surprise, and excitement at some point. In class we can discuss these reactions and explore how they may impact our interpretation of the readings. Admittedly, this may be a bit uncomfortable at times, but if we create a safe environment it should be okay. It is threatening to (re)examine values and beliefs; however, I believe this is part of the learning process.

Creating a “safe space”

I believe it is critical to develop a safe “space” in which the sensitive issues of race, and intersectionality can be addressed. Thus, it is important for us to explore our reactions (both cognitive and affective) to the readings and class discussions. Creating space involves providing a safe, nonthreatening environment. To accomplish this, I am encouraging you to respect one another’s contributions; this does not mean you have to agree with all positions or that you cannot voice opposition; it simply means letting others voice their opinions without interruptions or without fear of being attacked personally.

## Learning Outcomes

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes (CLOs):

- Define and conceptualize Cultural Psychology
- Formulate individual and developmental processes, adjustment and adaptation issues, and social systems and structures that different cultures
- Explain methodological issues in studying cultural psychology, and have an ability to locate, understand, and articulate research and literature topics related to cultural psychology
- Identify effective ways and promising strategies to address problems and to improve well-being for diverse communities

**Students’ rights and responsibilities** are explained in The Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities (https://rpi.app.box.com/s/3f1cl3w1lt1mq8gqla68v6uk3vh1c19f) and govern the conduct of both faculty and students. Rensselaer’s policies on academic integrity are explained on pp. 16-19 and include strict prohibitions against academic dishonesty. Please note in particular that the plagiarism is “the omission of acknowledgement or reference” of sources, whether or not intentional (so “I did not mean to plagiarize” is not an adequate excuse). The definitions and examples presented below are a sampling of types of academic dishonesty and are not to be construed as an exhaustive or exclusive list. The academic integrity policy applies to all students.

1. Academic Fraud is defined as the alteration of documentation relating to the grading process; for example, changing exam solutions to negotiate for a higher grade or tampering with an instructor's grade book.
2. b. Collaboration is defined as deliberately facilitating an act of academic dishonesty in any way or form; for example, allowing another student to observe an exam paper or allowing another student to "recycle" one's old term paper or using one another's work in a paper or lab report without citing it as another’s work.
3. Copying is defined as obtaining information pertaining to a graded exercise by deliberately observing the paper of another student; for example, noting which alternative a neighboring student has circled on a multiple-choice exam.
4. Cribbing is defined as the use or attempted use of prohibited materials, information, or study aids in an academic exercise. Examples include using an unauthorized formal sheet during an exam.
5. Fabrication is defined as the unauthorized falsification or invention of any information in an academic exercise. Examples include the use of "bought" or "ready-made" term papers, or falsifying lab records or reports.
6. Plagiarism is defined as representing the work or words of another as one's own through the omission of acknowledgment or reference. Examples include using sentences verbatim from a published source in a term paper without appropriate referencing or presenting as one's own the detailed argument of a published source or presenting as one's own electronically or digitally enhanced graphic representations from any form of media.
7. Sabotage is defined as the destruction of another student's work. Examples include destroying a model, lab experiment, computer program, or term paper developed by another student.
8. Substitution is defined as utilizing a proxy, or acting as a proxy, in any academic exercise. Examples include taking an exam for another student or having a homework assignment done by someone else.
