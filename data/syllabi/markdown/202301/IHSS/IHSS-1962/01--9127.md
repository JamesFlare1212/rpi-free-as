## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

Stories, Power, Medicine  
Can stories heal trauma? How do conceptions of mental illness change over time and vary drastically in  
a global context? What social and economic factors determine who gets to tell their illness narrative?  
This class explores the intersection of narrative and medicine. By paying particular attention to race,  
gender, sexuality, and class, this course examines the role of storytelling in articulating, maintaining,  
and subverting power structures when it comes to definitions of health, illness, and (dis)ability.  
Materials include a variety of genres, such as journalism, fiction, clinical case studies, memoir,  
philosophy, and scholarly essays.

## Learning Outcomes

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes (CLOs):

- Upon successful completion of the course, students will: • recognize and appreciate how the humanities can inform, enrich, and contextualize supposedly neutral, fact-based, and universal conceptions of medicine
- hone their communication skills through exploration and discussion of complex social and cultural issues in their historical contexts
- learn to approach writing as a form of inquiry and a social practice
- rhetorically analyze and assess various communication situations regarding tone, ethos, pathos, logos, and others
- appreciate textual production as a collaborative process via feedback mechanisms and team-based writing tasks and presentations
- gain experience in expressing ideas effectively for a variety of purposes, to different audiences (scholarly, public, personal), and in different media (including textual and multi-media formats)
- develop competence in several genres, such as rhetorical analysis, discussion moderation, collaborative oral presentation, project proposal, argumentative paper, personal narrative (traditional or multi-media format)
- participate in a learning community through feedback, encouragement, and accountability

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes Assessment Measures:

**Assignments**

**Participation (worth 20%)**

Be on time, well prepared, and present in mind throughout the duration of our class meetings. Contribute consistently, actively and meaningfully to our discussion. Often, we will do short writing exercises and group work in class. The spirit of our meeting is collaborative rather than competitive.

- It is your responsibility to have the assigned reading materials with you in class (either paper copy or digital copy on a device other than a cellphone).
- Avoid showing up late or leaving early. Whenever you come to class after I take the roll, it is your responsibility to make sure your presence is recorded by me at the end of the class session.
- Using your cellphone during class, even if only to ‘check the time,’ is prohibited and will impact your participation grade.

Grading: At the end of each class session, I will make a note (either check, check +, or check -) in terms of each student’s contribution to the class. Full credit will receive a student who regularly attends on time, brings the assigned reading to class, is always well-prepared, focused and engaged in our conversation and our writing tasks, and who participates actively, positively, and respectfully.

**Personal Narrative (worth 10%)**

At the end of our section on memoir and life writing, students will be asked to write a narrative about a personal experience related to health or illness in a broad sense. In this creative assignment, they will reflect on the ways in which a past event or experience helps them to better understand themselves and their worlds.

**Moderating the Class Discussion (worth 10%)**

Each student will lead our class discussion once during the semester. They will adopt the text portion for the day, close-read 2-3 short passages from the assigned text(s), which includes:

- taking us to these relevant quotes, reading them out loud, and commenting on word use as well as on underlying assumptions and implications
- determining the quote’s significance in the context of the work as a whole
- possibly drawing connections to other course texts
- and preparing three to four productive, thought-generating discussion questions

The emphasis of this assignment is on analyzing the quotes as well as on setting up issues from the text for our class discussion. The presentation should begin with a very brief contextualization: a few words on the author(s), the genre, and the historical context (if applicable).

Students will prepare a few engaging slides to help visualize their ideas. They will upload their presentation in .pdf format to LMS *before* our meeting at the latest. The speaking time should be roughly **10** **minutes** ***excluding*** the ensuing class discussion. If additional sources are consulted (books, websites, etc.), students must include a list of works cited.

There will be a sign-up sheet in class. First come/first served.

**Analytical paper (worth 20%)**

Students will write a rhetorical analysis of one or several course texts. They will examine what and how others have written about a particular health-related topic and place their personal inquiry in the context of a broader academic debate. To that end, this essay requires reviewing the assigned course readings, making observations, thinking analytically about these texts, and commenting on them.

Requires first and final draft.

**Argumentative paper (worth 25%)**

Students will write an argumentative paper on an important health-related issue they identify in one or several of our course texts. They will use both course texts and outside sources in order to formulate an argument about that issue. The assignment involves a research component.

Requires proposal, first draft, and final draft.

**R****evision Process**

Both the analytical and the argumentative paper involve the submission of first drafts and feedback from the instructor and/or peers. Specific prompts will be posted and due dates appear on the course schedule. All first drafts must be substantially revised based on the student’s evolving engagement with the course as well as their peers’ and the instructor’s feedback. Each piece will be evaluated based on *how well* it executes the assignment requirements as well as the extent to which the writer has demonstrated a commitment to the revision process.

Do not:

- submit a first draft of a writing assignment as your final draft
- only make minimal changes
- disregard the comments your reviewers make

**Collaborative Presentation (worth 15%)**

Students participate in an ‘expert panel,’ a shared-interest group of 3 to 4 students. Each student prepares an individual presentation based on their **A****rgumentative** **P****aper** that connects to the group’s more general topic. As a group, they will identify and discuss connections and tensions between their individual paper projects.

Oral component (panel discussion in class):

1\) After a brief introduction of the speakers, each student presents their short paper on a distinct ‘problem’ connected to the group’s overall topic.

2\) Then the group brings in conversation overlapping issues, shared questions, and possible tensions between their papers. Like a panel of experts, the speakers will engage in an exchange of ideas before a general Q & A with the audience can begin.

Written component:

1\) the group’s collective visual presentation with slides on each talk as well as slides on the connections and issues between individual talks (.pdf only)

Evaluation:

Individual grade (50%): individual student’s oral presentation and corresponding written section

Collective grade (50%): group’s discussion/exchange/Q&A with the audience and their collective written presentation (slides)

**Submission of Assignments**

All written assignments must follow MLA formatting (double-spaced, 1-inch margin on all sides, header, page numbers, 12pt. Times New Roman or comparable font, no extraneous spacing, etc.).

MLA sample paper: [https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/research_and_citation/mla_style/mla_formatting_and_style_guide/mla_sample_paper.html](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/research_and_citation/mla_style/mla_formatting_and_style_guide/mla_sample_paper.html).

All written assignments will be submitted via Blackboard LMS. Do not email me your assignment, unless we’ve made such arrangements. Submissions are only acceptable as a .pdf. Never submit .pages.

Please polish and proofread all typed work before you submit. **You are responsible for ensuring your work is turned in appropriately and legibly. Illegible or blank assignments turned in** **won’t** **count as submitted, so check your submissions.**

**Late Policy**

Deadlines ensure that your work and my work remains manageable. Try to always submit assignments on time. Moderating the Class Discussion, the Collaborative Presentation, and the final draft of the Argumentative Paper must be submitted by the due date in order to receive credit. For these assignments, late submissions won’t be considered. For the other drafts of papers (first and final drafts) a grace period of 24 hours applies. Beyond the grace period, I will only accept your late draft if you requested and were granted an extension in advance, that is, before the due date or within the grace period. Once again, I cannot grant an extension on the final draft of our third paper.

### Additional Academic Integrity Course Policy and Penalty Information:

**A****cademic Integrity**  
Student-instructor relationships are built on trust. For example, students must trust that instructors have made appropriate decisions about the structure and content of the courses they teach, and teachers must trust that the assignments that students turn in are their own. Acts that violate this trust undermine the educational process. The Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities and The Graduate Student Supplement define various forms of Academic Dishonesty and you are responsible for becoming familiar with these. Some examples of plagiarism include copying, paraphrasing, or summarizing without acknowledgment, submission of another student's work as one's own, the purchase/use of prepared research or completed papers or projects, and the unacknowledged use of research sources gathered by someone else. Failure to indicate accurately the extent and precise nature of one's reliance on other sources is also a form of plagiarism. Students are responsible for understanding legitimate use of sources, the appropriate ways of acknowledging academic, scholarly, or creative indebtedness.

All assignments that are turned in for a grade must represent the student’s own work completed for this class in particular. In cases where help was received or teamwork was allowed, a notation on the assignment should indicate your collaboration. If you have a compelling reason for wanting to use previous work of yours for the class, it must be substantially and fundamentally revised, and you must get approval from the instructor first.  
Submission of any assignment that is in violation of this policy will result in a penalty of a zero for the assignment and the potential to fail the course. Second offenses will result in automatic failure of the course and the potential for disciplinary action by the institution as the student will be reported to the Dean of Students as appropriate. If you have any question concerning this policy before submitting an assignment, please ask for clarification.

For more information, see Student Rights, Responsibilities, and Judicial Affairs, [https://info.rpi.edu/dean-students/student-rights-responsibilities-and-judicial-affairs](https://info.rpi.edu/dean-students/student-rights-responsibilities-and-judicial-affairs), and consult *The Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities*.
