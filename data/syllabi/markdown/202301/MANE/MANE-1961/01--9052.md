### Prerequisites or Other Requirements:

This course is a pilot section to test the hypothesis that 4 ENGR-1100 Introduction to Engineering Analysis plus 4 ENGR-2530 Strength of Materials can be better taught with better student outcomes and better integration of modern computational tools as 3 Mechanics I + 2 Introduction to Engineering Computation + 3 Mechanics II.  
Prerequisites: High-school level trigonometry, algebra, and analytic geometry; high-school level physics is recommended.

### Additional Information for Course Information Section:

This first year lecture+studio course introduces you to some of the most fundamental computational techniques in engineering: matrix algebra for analyzing linear systems. It does so in the context of engineering modeling and design exercises combined with computer-assisted computation to leverage the power of human analysis and design. Most of the learning takes place in the classroom through lecture content and collaborative studio exercises with some additional out-of-class reflection exercises. The course begins with the study of matrices, their mathematical properties, and methods for using matrices to solve engineering problems. It continues with matrix applications of particular importance for analyzing engineering mechanics problems, and ends with a deeper study of matrix structural analysis.

### Additional Academic Integrity Course Policy and Penalty Information:

Academic Integrity  
Student-teacher relationships are built on trust. For example, students must trust that teachers have made appropriate decisions about the structure and content of the courses they teach, and teachers must trust that the assignments that students turn in are their own. Acts that violate this trust undermine the educational process. The Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities and The Graduate Student Supplement define various forms of Academic Dishonesty and you should make yourself familiar with these as appropriate.  
In this class, all in-class exercises and homework assignments (that are turned in for a grade) must be significantly representative of your own work unless it is specifically stated that multiple authors are permitted. As an individual you will submit your own work with your own substantial and significant individual content and style.  
In cases where teamwork is allowed, a notation on the assignment should clearly identify all collaborators and their individual contributions.  
That said, I strongly encourage you to collaborate in studying for this course and working on the exercises and homework assignments to maximize your learning and to broaden your experiences in how one might apply what you learn to solving challenging engineering problems. Nonetheless, at the end of the collaborative process you must have your own copy of your deliverable in which you identify your collaboration (e.g., in comments and/or references clearly indicated in the assignment). Prior to submission you should review and revise your deliverable to match your unique style and approach to organizing and communicating your information.  
Specific to this course, academic dishonesty can take the following forms.  
1\) Taking an exam with any assistance from another person, regardless of who they are, unless the instructor specifically states that it is a group activity (e.g., a group oral exam).  
2\) Using any materials (such as a crib sheet or a calculator) that you did not bring for you and you alone to use on an exam, unless given explicit permission by the proctor (e.g., typically for extraordinary circumstances such as the failure of your calculator during an exam, etc., though such assistance should not be expected or relied on).  
3\) Using any materials (such as crib sheets or extra crib sheets, any electronic device – wireless or otherwise – such as a laptop, cell phone, calculator, etc.) that are not explicitly permitted during an exam.  
4\) Interaction with a wireless device (e.g., a cell phone or tablet) during an exam (except when explicitly allowed by the proctor) will be considered an illicit data exchange and will result in a zero for the entire exam.  
5\) Exiting the exam room without permission, or not returning in a prompt and timely manner when given permission. “Prompt and timely” is at the discretion of the instructional team.  
6\) Submission of a deliverable that, except for short phrases or properly referenced material, consists of any content not created by and original to you.  
7\) Indicating someone as “present” (e.g., on an attendance sheet or submitting an in-class exercise) who is not present in class at the time the indication is made.  
8\) Collaboration is encouraged. However, assisting someone else with work where they intend to turn in the work as if it was done on their own will itself be considered a serious violation of academic honesty. Give credit for any assistance received, and have sufficient content of your own making according to the expectations of the deliverable.  
The penalty for submission of an exam that is in violation of this policy is failure of the course and referral to the Dean of Students with a recommendation for expulsion.  
Submission of a deliverable that is in violation of this policy will result in a grade of zero on the assignment for the first offense. If there is a subsequent infraction the student fails the course and is referred to the Dean of Students.  
If you have any questions concerning this policy before submitting a deliverable please ask for clarification.  
If you are ever concerned about passing the course please talk to me as soon as possible. I will work with you as I would anyone and everyone in class to help you help yourself. A student’s dishonesty in this course might be motivated by greed or an inflated sense of entitlement, but it should never be motivated by fear.
