## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

This course explores ethical issues posed by the proliferation of artificial intelligence technologies. In order to do so, we will first discuss the philosophical issues with ethical theories, and how we can be justified in making ethical judgments in particular cases. We will then survey a number of issues, some of which are as follows. How should we approach thinking about ethical obligation generally? How should we think about designing an ethical algorithm? As autonomous cars are most likely the robots to be integrated into our lives at scale, what challenges do they pose for human-robot interaction design, psychology, law, and socio-political systems? Which ethical approach or theory should an automated process be programmed to follow when there’s no legal guidance? How ought we make sense of obligation and responsibility in the context of the decisions of an autonomous AI (designer, user, AI itself)? Trust is an essential component of social human life. But how will integrating AI into our lives require us to understand the novel philosophical and legal issues related to interpersonal trust between people and AI? How can we know if AI has a moral status, and deserves the respect a person deserves? Is it permissible for companies to “teach” their products using interactions with the general public? If artificial general intelligence is achieved, how likely is it that an artificial agent’s interests will align with ours? What should we make of the concerns about job displacement due to AI technologies? How worried should we be about AI? Are we too worried? Not worried enough? What early indicators should influence our attitude about how to answer that question?

## Learning Outcomes

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes (CLOs):

- Develop an explicit rather than tacit understanding of the ethical challenges posed by AI.
- Develop the ability to identify and interrogate our assumptions (and develop some views) about how we ought to handle these issues. While some of these problems are more hypothetical, others present (almost daily) dilemmas and so students ought to leave the course better prepared to think effectively about these problems.
- Develop the ability to argue clearly and effectively, in both oral and written form, as well as to effectively listen and share in dialogue.
- Distinguish and evaluate different philosophical arguments.
- Be able to plan and work with other people on abstract and difficult projects.

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes Assessment Measures:

Take-home exams, argumentative essay, group presentation project.

## Policies

### Academic Policies:

- ### Academic Integrity

  Student-teacher relationships are built on trust. For example, students must trust that teachers have made appropriate decisions about the structure and content of the courses they teach, and teachers must trust that the assignments that students turn in are their own. Acts that violate this trust undermine the educational process. The Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities and The Graduate Student Supplement define various forms of Academic Dishonesty and you should make yourself familiar with these. In this class, all assignments that are turned in for a grade must represent the student’s own work. In cases where help was received, or teamwork was allowed, a notation on the assignment should indicate your collaboration.

  Violations of academic integrity may also be reported to the appropriate Dean (Dean of Students for undergraduate students or the Dean of Graduate Education for graduate students, respectively).

  If you have any question concerning this policy before submitting an assignment, please ask for clarification. In addition, you can visit the following site for more information on our Academic Integrity Policy: [Students Rights, Responsibilities, and Judicial Affairs](https://info.rpi.edu/dean-students/student-rights-responsibilities-and-judicial-affairs).
