## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

This course engages students in a critical examination of television and its historical and present status as a dominant popular cultural phenomenon. Television in this context is broadly understood as an evolving medium for motion pictures in the age of technological convergence. We’ll consider it as a medium for narrative, for entertainment, for dispersing information, and for other uses. Using a variety of analytical and theoretical approaches, we’ll consider the significant influence television has on individual, social, and cultural experiences—addressing questions of representation, identity, economics, and ideology. The class uses a collaborative learning process, allowing students to influence the content and emphasis of the class and reflecting the democratic nature of popular culture media.

## Course Goals

### Goals:

1\) You will develop a faculty for critical media engagement with television, learning how to apply aesthetic, historical, and critical frameworks as tools towards deepening your understanding of the medium. Watching numerous genres and modes of television, you’ll apply those critical lenses to their close study. Of particular importance is learning how to make connections between conceptual issues and ‘television’ as a cultural phenomenon and practice (of viewing, critiquing, sharing, and creating). Although this class focuses on television, its lessons can be applied to and contextualized in the larger mediascape; you should gain a deeper understanding of how media shapes us as individuals, as groups and communities, and as cultures.

2\) As a complement to these critical tools, you will enhance your reflexive media awareness—your processual and dialogic understanding of your relationship to television—so that you can more fully articulate your experience, contextualize it, and empathize with others’ perspectives. You will develop your communicative/generative skills by articulating and refining your thoughts in oral, written, and presentational forms, and (if you are so inclined) by creating media works in various formats as a way of engaging with concepts we encounter in this class.

3\) Finally, this class uses a collaborative learning process; the course will be shaped by your interests and prior knowledge as a community, as well as by my disciplinary, personal and pedagogical expertise. Through the use of non-traditional educational strategies, you’ll engage in meta-examinations of the learning process, considering how the course functions as a ‘medium’ for learning.

## Learning Outcomes

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes (CLOs):

- CLO 1 Identify, explain, and evaluate conceptual and theoretical strategies involved in media studies as relates to television.
- CLO 2 Apply critical lenses to media texts, and debate the value and function of those critical strategies.

- CLO 4 Work collaboratively with a group of peers to compose a multi-faceted critique of a media text.

## Grading Criteria

### Criteria Details:

ASSESSMENT: ‘CRITIC POINTS’-BASED ‘GAMEFUL’ GRADING

Introduction:

Grading for this class will unfold a little differently than you may be used to. Using an ‘additive’ and ‘gameful’ approach, your performance in the class is based on the points that you earn, much like in some game systems (a video game, a table-top role-playing game, or what have you). Everyone begins with 0 points. At the start of the semester, there are 470,000+ points to be earned through various tasks. As you accomplish tasks in the class, you build up points towards your final grade. No assignment or task is actually ‘required;’ instead, there are a number of points required for ranks (grades), and you are able to plot your course through the class based on your interests and your schedule. You will be given a worksheet at the beginning of the semester that you can use to help you to plan out your goals and keep track of your points; make sure to refer to the assignments list and the calendar at the end of the syllabus to plan your own personal schedule of assignments.

Assignments will be assessed using a ‘medal’ system. For most daily and small assignments, that ‘medal’ will reflect the following categories. Gold 100% Solid, weighty, polished, addressing all required aspects of assignment

Silver 90% A response with clarity and strength, but maybe a little tarnished

- ### Academic Integrity

  Student-teacher relationships are built on trust. For example, students must trust that teachers have made appropriate decisions about the structure and content of the courses they teach, and teachers must trust that the assignments that students turn in are their own. Acts that violate this trust undermine the educational process. The Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities and The Graduate Student Supplement define various forms of Academic Dishonesty and you should make yourself familiar with these.
