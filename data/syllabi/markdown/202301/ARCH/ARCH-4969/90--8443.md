## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

**Description:** Places—landscapes and cities—are as much a mental construct as natural or physically built. This course aims to explore the diverse interconnected “narratives” that shape and define our perception and idea of places. In its first edition of the course, as part of the 2023 Latin America Program, it will use as source the unique experience of traveling and living abroad. Travel narratives have been a source of Western fascination for thousands of years: from the early “vague tales of silent silk merchants,” Marco Polo’s description of his travels in China, Cristóbal Colón *Diaries* of the Conquest of what would become Latin America, to today’s narratives in times of mass tourism, travel and food guides proliferation and distributed and personal means of communication. As students use their own direct experience to reflect on the character and nature of Latin America, this class will serve as travel log and archive of the 2023 RPI Latin America Program.

“Kublai Khan does not necessarily believe everything Marco Polo says when he describes the cities visited on his expeditions, but the emperor of the Tartars does continue listening to the young Venetian with greater attention and curiosity than he shows any other messenger or explorer of his. In the lives of emperors there is a moment which follows pride in the boundless extension of the territories we have conquered, and the melancholy and relief of knowing we shall soon give up any thought of knowing and understanding them” (Italo Calvino, *Invisible Cities* 5).

According to the back-cover, Calvino’s book is a dream-like story-telling at the twilight of an empire. In the garden sit the aged Kublai Khan and the young Marco Polo—Tartar emperor and Venetian traveler. The emperor has sensed the end of his empire coming soon. Marco Polo diverts the emperor with tales of the cities he has seen in his travels around the empire: cities and memory, cities and desire, cities and designs. Soon it becomes clear that each of these fantastic places is really the same place.

What is that place? Where is it? Is it in our heads? Is it in our hearts? How would we describe it to ourselves? And to others? What does that description say about us? And about the way in what we see? We will be the story-tellers of the places and cities we visit and live in. Experiences, excitement, disappointment, cultural shock, homesickness, points of view, memory making, desires and designs, all of it will be the material for this course. We will read some theoretical work on travel narratives, the tourist gaze, literary works, but mostly and over all we will write. Everyday. Even if it just is three or four words that summed up that day’s experience. In our meetings we’ll share our writings and discuss them; and edit them.

**Topics:** travel literature, creative writing, content production, online publishing and social networking.

**Course Structure:** In this edition, the course is organized in 4 sections according the schedule of the 2023 Latin America program: 1) Boot Camp-Vaquerías—Córdoba mountains; 2) Travels; 3) Córdoba; and 4) Final Recap. It will include content production (textual and visual) and research, development, editing, and implementation of a publishing strategy through different mediums, such as online, printed media and exhibition.

**Course Requirements:** Students will work in a think-do-tank environment, not only studying but mainly producing and disseminating their own narratives. Students will regularly produce both visual and written critical content. Class meets in one or two (TBD) weekly discussion/workshop sessions where students will share their writings for discussion and editing. Intertwined with the narratives we’ll be discussing issues emerging in presentations, readings and viewings, and experiences that inform the travel log narratives. Students are required to complete the assigned material in advance and come to class prepared to participate actively. Students must provide weekly brief reports/questions of the assignments and travel log progress. As core assignment students will work in the development of a final narrative/photographic essay on a topic of their choice (upon faculty approval).

## Learning Outcomes

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes (CLOs):

- 1.The student will have a hands-in knowledge of topics, issues in discussion, and recent developments in contemporary Latin America and will be familiarized with its canonical and popular cultures.
- 2\. The student will perfect her/his/their narrative skills (both visually and in writing).
- 3\. The student will demonstrate the ability to critically think, analyze, and contextualize disparate sources of information from different fields concerning Latin America.
- 4\. The student will apply effective oral, written, and visual communication skills to present a coherent and sustained argument in specific topics.
- 5\. The student will be able to articulate her/his/their insights about the readings/viewings and the writing narratives in progress.
- 6\. The student will respectfully receive and respond to other’s comments.
- 7\. The student will reflect through the account of her/his/their personal experience on the notions of the traveler and foreignness.
- 8\. The student will participate and developed a shared (as a class) body of content that accounts for her/his experience in this abroad program.

### Additional Academic Integrity Course Policy and Penalty Information:

The definitions and examples presented below are samples of types of academic dishonesty and are not to be construed as an exhaustive or exclusive list. The academic dishonesty policy applies to all students, undergraduate and graduate, and to scholarly pursuits and research. Additionally, attempts to commit academic dishonesty or to assist in the commission or attempt of such an act are also violations of this policy.

- Academic Fraud: The alteration of documentation relating to the grading process. For example, changing exam solutions to negotiate for a higher grade or tampering with an instructor’s grade book.
- Collaboration: Deliberate facilitation of academic dishonesty in any form. For example, allowing another student to observe an exam paper or allowing another student to “recycle” one’s old term paper or using one another’s work in a paper or lab report without citing it as another’s work.
- Copying: Obtaining information pertaining to a graded exercise by deliberately observing the paper of another student. For example, noting which alternative a neighboring student has circled on a multiple-choice exam.
- Cribbing: Use or attempted use of prohibited materials, information, or study aids in an academic exercise. For example, using an unauthorized formal sheet during an exam.
- Fabrication: Unauthorized falsification or invention of any information in an academic exercise. For example, use of “bought” or “ready-made” term papers, or falsifying lab records or reports.

Plagiarism: Representing the work or words of another as one’s own through the omission of acknowledgment or reference. For example, using sentences verbatim from a published source in a term paper without appropriate referencing, or presenting as one’s own the detailed argument of a published

- source, or presenting as one’s own electronically or digitally enhanced graphic representations from any form of media.
- Sabotage: Destruction of another student’s work. For example, destroying a model, lab experiment, computer program, or term paper developed by another student.
- Substitution: Utilizing a proxy, or acting as a proxy, in any academic exercise. For example, taking an exam for another student or having a homework assignment done by someone else.
