## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

As an architect and as an entrepreneur, your job is to bring ideas to life by solving real problems for real people. To do that well, you need to deeply understand the needs of your users and then tailor your skills and work to those needs. This experiential course will introduce you to design thinking (DT). DT is a structured process for designing innovative solutions to ambiguous problems in ways that really meet people's needs. Design thinking can be broken down into four stages that are meant to be part of a rapid cycle of learning and experimenting:

1. Empathize with the user
2. Define the problem
3. Ideate possible solutions
4. Prototype solutions with quick experiments

As an entrepreneur, you need to not only make sure your solution solves a real problem for real people, but also that it has a viable business model (aka makes money). People need to care enough about the problem that they are willing to pay for your solution, and your solution needs cost you less than they are willing to pay. Entrepreneurs use a business model canvas to strategize about the ways they create and deliver value to their users. The design thinking process can be applied to solve problems relating to each part of the business model canvas.

You'll work with a small "founder" team to apply design thinking and entrepreneurial skills to design innovative solutions to real problems. Each week your team will work on one of the stages of design thinking to make progress toward your solution. As real entrepreneurs you'll have to think critically about what you are learning and whether you are ready for the next step or need to reassess your assumptions and repeat an earlier step.

## Course Goals

### Goals:

Students will . . .

1. Apply the design thinking process to approach an ambiguous problem or opportunity.
2. Utilize tools and frameworks to empathize with users and identify customer needs in a market to discover "customer insights."
3. Deploy the basic tools of primary research including user interviews questions, surveys and ethnographic studies.
4. Concisely define a user persona and problem statement.
5. Lead and participate in ideation exercises to generate a wide set of options with a team.
6. Design and implement prototypes to test key risks and assumptions for an entrepreneurial venture.
7. Apply the practice of iteration within each step of the design thinking process and across the steps.
8. Use the Lean Business Canvas to analyze business ideas/opportunities to identify key assumptions within a proposed business and articulate the path to verifying assumptions.
9. Effectively present your entrepreneurial process and pitch your venture concept in a compelling way.
10. Know the basic meaning of Feasibility, Viability and Desirability for Entrepreneurs and the relationship among these three things.

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes Assessment Measures:

We developed a game mechanism to mimic the real-world, non-linear process entrepreneurs go through to find product market fit. Each week the student teams have to tackle a different "phase" of the entrepreneurial process. They reflect on how it went and what they learned and then decide whether they need to circle back to iterate on a previous phase, or progress forward to the next phase. They don't have to go in a particular sequence and they can repeat a phase as many times as they need to, but they must have moved through all phases at some point by the end of the course.  
Each week, students demonstrate hands-on application of key principles of design thinking and entrepreneurship. They collaborate in small teams of "founders" to work through the stages of the entrepreneurship journey. Each week they have to identify key assumptions in their business and then conduct a variety of activities to prove or disprove those assumptions: user interviews, surveys, reading discussion forums, user observations, ideation sessions, prototyping, testing, and more. They organize artifacts from these experiments into a weekly team presentation to share their hypothesis, their tests, what they learned, and what they need to test next. In addition to faculty feedback, students think critically about the work of their classmates and provide constructive feedback to help further the application of key concepts. Guest lectures give students the opportunity to see themselves in the shows of a real life entrepreneur and ask questions about how they dealt with challenges similar to those the students are experiencing with their group projects. In addition to their specific projects, students have reflected on their key learnings about the entrepreneurship process itself and shared those with their classmates.

## Grading Criteria

### Criteria Details:

55% is from your weekly reviewers, with each review weighted based on the average review score of the reviewing team. Each member of the reviewing team will give your team a score of 1-10 in the following areas:

1. Hypotheses: Clear hypotheses that address the most important risks and assumptions of the venture.
2. Experiments: Experiments are thoughtful designed, address the hypotheses, and demonstrate creativity and effort.
3. Insights: The team extracted interesting learnings from their experiments that drive their presentation forward.
4. Presentation: The presentation was clear, interesting, and engaging.

25% is for your final team presentation

Sharing your journey: What steps did you follow?

1. Clarity of your story (1-5)
2. Insights (1-5)
3. Creativity (1-5)
4. Adherence to 8 min goal (1-5)

Startup pitch

Problem. The problem you've discovered and how you've proven a market cares enough to want a solution

1. Who (1-5)
2. Why (1-5)
3. When (1-5)
4. Where (1-5)
5. How much does it cost (1-5)

Solution. Your proposed solution to the problem and how you've proven it could satisfy the target market

1. Are the users and other stakeholders aware of the problem? (1-5)
2. Do they care? (1-5)
3. Are they actively seeking a solution? (1-5)

Traction. What traction you've had already and what that shows about future growth. Think product-market fit, not necessarily revenue.

1. How compelling is the evidence that the solution solves the problem? (1-5)
2. What is the TAM? (1-5)
3. How will the market be accessed? (1-5)
4. What is the expected effectiveness? (1-5)
5. Any special experience in the market or special access to it? (y/n)

Competition. What unfair advantages / defensibility your startup can boast over competitors.

1. How focused is the startup solution on a particular niche market? (1-5)
2. How hard would it be for someone else to enter the market and compete or for existing powers in the market to adopt this approach? (1-5)
3. How quantifiable is this evidence? (1-5)

Ask. What are you asking for?

1. Valuation and its derivation? (1-5)
2. Use of capital? (1-5)

Few words and good visuals on slides? (y/n)

Good representation from across the team? (y/n)

10% is for adhering to our rules

10% is your individual participation

1. Did you speak up in class?
2. Did you submit reviews of another team every week?
3. Did you complete your teammate ratings at the end of the course?

10% teammate ratings

At the end of the course and at least once in the middle of the course, each team member will rate each teammate using this question: "This teammate did their fair share to help our team be successful." ### Additional Academic Integrity Course Policy and Penalty Information:

# ACADEMIC INTEGRITY

Intellectual integrity and credibility are the foundation of all academic work. A violation of Academic Integrity policy is, by definition, considered a flagrant offense to the educational process. It is taken seriously by students, faculty, and Rensselaer and will be addressed in an effective manner.

If found responsible for committing academic dishonesty, a student may be subject to one or both types of penalties: an academic (grade) penalty administered by the professor and/or disciplinary action through the Rensselaer judicial process described in this handbook.

Academic dishonesty is a violation of the Grounds for Disciplinary Action as described in this handbook. A student may be subject to any of the following types of disciplinary action should disciplinary action be pursued by the instructor: disciplinary warning; disciplinary probation; disciplinary suspension, expulsion and/or alternative actions as agreed on by the student and hearing officer. It should be noted that no student who allegedly commits academic dishonesty will be able to drop or change the grade option for the course in question and is not eligible

to request an F examination for the course.

The definitions and examples presented below are a sampling of types of academic dishonesty and are not to be construed as an exhaustive or exclusive list. The academic integrity policy applies to all students, undergraduate and graduate, and to scholarly pursuits and research. Additionally, attempts to commit academic dishonesty or to assist in the commission or attempt of such an act are also violations of this policy.

Academic Fraud

Academic Fraud is defined as the alteration of documentation relating to the grading process; for example, changing exam solutions to negotiate for a higher grade or tampering with an instructor's grade book.

Collaboration

Collaboration is defined as deliberately facilitating an act of academic dishonesty in any way or form; for example, allowing another student to observe an exam paper or allowing another student to "recycle" one's old term paper or using one another's work in a paper or lab report without citing it as another's work.

Copying

Copying is defined as obtaining information pertaining to a graded exercise by deliberately observing the paper of another student; for example, noting which alternative a neighboring student has circled on a multiple-choice exam.

Cribbing

Cribbing is defined as the use or attempted use of prohibited materials, information, or study aids in an academic exercise. Examples include using an unauthorized formal sheet during an exam.

Fabrication

Fabrication is defined as the unauthorized falsification or invention of any information in an academic exercise. Examples include the use of "bought" or "ready-made" term papers, or falsifying lab records or reports.

Plagiarism

Plagiarism is defined as representing the work or words of another as one's own through the omission of acknowledgment or reference. Examples include using sentences verbatim from a published source in a term paper without appropriate referencing, or presenting as one's own the detailed argument of a published source,

or presenting as one's own electronically or digitally enhanced graphic representations from any form of media.

Sabotage

Sabotage is defined as the destruction of another student's work. Examples include destroying a model, lab experiment, computer program, or term paper developed by another student.

Substitution

Substitution is defined as utilizing a proxy, or acting as a proxy, in any academic exercise. Examples include taking an exam for another student or having a homework assignment done by someone else.
