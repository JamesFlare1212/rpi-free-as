## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

Architectural Design 4 tackles the problem of designing housing in downtown Troy, or more specifically *multi-unit collective* housing, in which some aspect of the domestic realm is shared among multiple domestic spheres. While many aspects of our personal and professional lives are increasingly communicated into collective spheres, a majority of our domestic spaces are structured by the prevailing American ethic of individual property, individual building. This ethic has manifested itself predominately in suburbs developed since World War II, and has now become such a fixture of a particular type of consumer-driven lifestyle that it is being exported to other countries, other cultures.

Clearly this is not a sustainable future as the world becomes increasingly urbanized and more extreme pressures are placed on the Earth’s resources. So as a typology, multi-unit collective housing must be understood as a global cultural necessity due its relative efficiency in terms of quantity of land, building materials, and energy required to construct and operate each housing unit. The semester-long project in this studio will be to design a mixed-use, multi-unit housing project on an urban site in downtown Troy, NY at the northwest corner of Congress and Third Streets. The site is currently occupied by a small fast food franchise building, which for the purposes of this studio will be removed, leaving the entire parcel bound by Third St. to the east, Congress St. to the south, Franklin Alley to the west, and existing buildings to the north, free for a new building.

In 2018 The City of Troy released a Comprehensive Plan for ongoing development in the city: https://www.troyny.gov/wp-content/uploads/2019/09/RealizeTroyComprehensivePlanMay2018.pdf

This plan describes the historic downtown zone, which includes the studio site at Congress and Third Streets, as “an architecturally rich, walkable district with many boutiques and restaurants, and a successful Farmer’s Market.” It notes that adding housing units in the historic downtown zone “will enhance safety, support activity beyond the workday and would better position the city to attract continued investment, job and population growth. Over time, and through population growth, the downtown could attract new neighborhood-serving amenities such as a small, well-stocked grocery store.” As a response to the City of Troy Comprehensive Plan, this studio will design a building containing 30 housing units and street level retail in the urban context of downtown Troy.

The studio will address housing design at range of scales from the small, intimate scale of quotidian ritual to the scale of the building, its various components, and its site. There will be structured moments in the semester when work will jump from one scale to another to effectively develop the project at the scale of the entire building and its site, and at the scale of details within an individual housing unit. Working in teams of two will ensure that projects are thoroughly developed at a range of scales.

Designing housing is an opportunity to materialize ethics of exchange and communication. It is an opportunity to mold and shape the form and materiality of the city into experiences of daily life. Ultimately, an architect’s attitude toward designing housing is a socio-political act. Projects designed in AD4 must successfully navigate pragmatics of generating and organizing an architectural program into a coherent building that sensitively responds to and shapes its user’s needs, while also maintaining high levels of formal, material, and aesthetic experimentation that push the discipline forward.

## Course Content

### Content Details:

**COURSE COMPONENTS, SEQUENCE AND DELIVERABLES**

1. **Precedent Documentation and Analysis**

Each team of two students will be assigned two precedent projects to analyze. The precedents will be chosen from a list of built housing projects on urban sites. Each team will acquire comprehensive knowledge of the complexities of these two projects and make a presentation to the entire studio about them. The goal of precedent analysis is to expose every student in the studio to a range of design strategies for urban multi-unit housing.

Before making their analyses, student teams will produce a series of drawings that comprehensively document the precedent projects and can subsequently be used as the basis for analytical diagrams. These drawings will include plans, sections, elevations, and any other drawings required to fully describe the project. They must be constructed from scratch in two dimensions. Images of drawings scanned from books and collected online may be used as underlays for these drawings, but the drawings must not be tracings of images. Rationalized dimensions, organizational principles, and architectural alignments must be implemented in a consistent and precise manner to CONSTRUCT the drawings rather than TRACE them. Develop clear, consistent, legible graphics for all drawings. Use the Rhino layer template provided to organize drawings. **These documentary drawings must be output directly from Rhino, do not use Illustrator.** Scale and format of drawings to be determined with section critic’s guidance. Include north arrow and graphic scale.

From completed documentary drawings students will develop a series of analytical diagrams that describe the functioning, organization, and relation of the project to its surrounding site. Analytical diagrams must show the following:

- \- parti diagram
- \- overall organizational strategies in plan and section
- \- orientation to north and solar exposure
- \- relationships between private and public programs
- \- horizontal and vertical circulation in relation to each other
- \- structural system
- \- site circulation and relations
- \- repetition and variation in unit types
- \- overall building dimensions (in feet and inches)
- \- unit, corridor, and stair dimensions (in feet and inches)
- \- gross square footage
- \- net square footage

Scale and format of diagrams to be determined with section critic’s guidance.

*Deliverables - Precedent Documentation (Due 1/23):*

A set of constructed two-dimensional drawings comprehensively documenting two housing precedent projects.

*Deliverables - Precedent Analysis (Due 1/23):*

A series of notated analytical diagrams addressing issues above. Scale and format of diagrams to be determined with section critic’s guidance.

2. **Site Analysis**

In order to understand the context in which the project will be located, students will make an analysis of the surrounding site. Like many small cities in the United States, Troy has undergone several transformations in its history from colonial beginnings to rapid industrial development spurred by rail and water transportation infrastructure, to a loss of manufacturing and disinvestment, to a current period of some redevelopment. New architectural projects are part of the changing nature of the city, and they must be designed with an awareness of context. New buildings do not need to be visually similar to existing buildings around it, but they must be designed in response to other aspects of context. Each studio section must assemble a site analysis incorporating the following elements, some of these elements will be completed by each two-person team as directed by the section leader:

- \- Photographic documentation of building elevations on Third between State & Ferry, and Congress between Second and Church
- \- Horizontal and vertical datums governing the architecture along Third and Congress
- \- Access points to buildings along Third and Congress
- \- Distribution of public and private programs along Third and Congress
- \- A map of transportation infrastructure in the city of Troy and surrounding region.
- \- A map of public programs in a three-block radius of the site.
- \- A zoning analysis of the site.
- \- A three-dimensional model of a three-block radius around the site.
- \- Solar exposure analysis of the site.
- \- FEMA flood map analysis of site.

*Deliverables – Site Analysis (Due 2/6):*

Student teams will be responsible for delivering one or more of the items listed above according to how work is distributed across the section.

3. **Generative Modeling and Drawing**

An abstract design exercise will generate formal and conceptual languages for the project. Generative work must consist of physical modeling and drawing. Physical models must be spatial, multi-material assemblies i.e., they must consist of at least three parts that fit together, are made of different materials, and define interior and exterior spaces. Generative models must be developed and discussed in terms of their potential to define a parti diagram for the project.

Beyond these general requirements, specifics of this generative exercise will be determined by each section critic individually.

*Deliverables – Generative Modeling and Drawing (Due 2/6):*

A set of speculative drawings/models that form the basis of a formal/conceptual design language, and a parti diagram for the project. Scale and format of drawings to be determined with section critic’s guidance.

4. **Programming and Massing**

The primary goal of this semester is to design a mixed-use building containing housing, street-level retail, and parking at the northwest corner of Congress St. and Third St. in Troy, NY. The project must be resolved in terms of planning, egress, structure, and envelope design. It must respond effectively to all aspects of the site analysis performed earlier in the semester. The project must incorporate the following primary program elements:

- \- 10 studio apartments
- \- 10 one-bedroom apartments
- \- 10 two-bedroom apartments
- \- Private and/or public outdoor space
- \- Ground level retail
- \- 15 off-street parking spaces
- \- Underground storage, mechanical, and/or parking spaces

Students are required to develop an annotated graphic document that specifies square foot areas of all program elements and demonstrates how these areas are configured on site in accordance with zoning regulations, horizontal and vertical circulation, and egress requirements. This document must specify:

- \- configuration of horizontal and vertical circulation
- \- egress routes from housing units and public spaces
- \- type, size, and distribution of housing units
- \- size and distribution any ancillary spaces deemed necessary and/or desirable for the project (lobby, mail room, gym, etc.)
- \- location(s) of outdoor space
- \- overall project square footage
- \- ratio of built area to open space
- \- how the project meets zoning regulations

Develop a physical massing model at 1/16” scale that translates the program study into building massing on site in relation to context.

*Deliverables – Programming and Massing (Due at Midterm Review – 2/27 or 3/2):*

An annotated graphic document specifying all aspects of program described above. Physical massing model at 1/16” scale.

5. **Floor Plans, Sections, and Elevations**

Building on skills developed last semester in AD3, two-dimensional orthographic projections (plans, sections, and elevations) will continue to be used instrumentally in design. Similar to last semester, these drawings MUST:

- \- be built up from scratch – a three-dimensional model may be used as a reference underlay to help produce the drawing, however it must not be included in the drawing
- \- be drawn at 1:1 scale
- \- make use of the layer structure template provided
- \- be used to explore and evaluate design decisions
- \- bring together aspects of the precedent analysis and generative drawings with design decisions made through explorations of massing, programming, and context
- \- be used to show an accurately designed parking lot
- \- be used to show realistic design and configuration of different types of housing units including how units aggregate, relate to structural and plumbing systems, light and air requirements, circulation, and egress
- \- describe all components of architecture at accurate scale
- \- be used to describe accurate floor, wall, and roof thicknesses
- \- be used to describe simple floor, wall, and roof assemblies
- \- be graphically clear through effective use of line weight and line type

Refer to Architectural Graphic Standards as well as references file provided to the studio for information regarding architectural drawing standards.

*Deliverables – Building Floorplans, Sections, and Elevations (First iteration due at Midterm Review 2/27 or 3/2, final iteration due at Final Review 4/12) :*

Four floor plans minimum, two sections minimum, two elevations (along Congress St. and Third St.) at 3/16” scale. **Output drawings directly from Rhino rather than using Illustrator to assign graphics.**

6. **Unit Design and Detail Chunk Model**

Architecture always operates across a wide range of scales, from the global scale of the project and its site to the local scale of how individual parts of a building are put together, and everything in between. In this phase of the project, shift design efforts from large-scale organization and figuration of the building to a smaller-scale design of individual housing units. Consider the following in designing housing units:

- \- entry sequence
- \- apertures in exterior walls/roof/floors of the building
- \- apertures in interior walls/roof/floors of the building
- \- how light and air enters the unit
- \- relationship of private and public spaces within the unit
- \- sectional qualities of the unit
- \- how units aggregate with each other
- \- window access for bedrooms according to fire code

Building on skills developed in building the tectonic detail last semester in AD3, explore a portion of the building in a large-scale detail chunk model. While chunk models in AD3 were at 1/4” scale, AD4 models will be built at 1/2” scale and will thus incorporate greater detail. This model must explore and show the following:

- \- detailed design of residential and public interior space in relation to exterior of building
- \- detailed exterior envelope development
- \- relationship of structure and envelope
- \- accurate floor, wall, and roof thicknesses
- \- well-developed floor, wall, and roof assemblies

This model does not need to show the full height of the building, it may be a chunk extracted from 2-3 floors of the building at any selected height.

*Deliverables – Unit Design and Chunk Model (Due at Final review 4/12):*

Plans and sections of 2-3 adjacent units at 1/2” scale.

Physical model extracting a chunk of the building and site. Model materials to be determined with section leader’s guidance.

7. **Three-Dimensional Imagery**

Three rendered views of the project are required: one aerial view, one eye level view of building exterior, and interior view within the new building. Test and save multiple camera options for each view. Once views have been selected, add detail to the three-dimensional model in areas visible in the views. Research rendered images and find a reference example(s) that references an appropriate aesthetic for the project. Develop a material and texture palette, a technique for handling context, and an overall aesthetic that complements and reinforces conceptual ideas of the project. Consider how an image conveys a vision of realism rather than being realistic, and how it is hyperreal rather than photoreal.

Images must be rendered in Rhino VRay and post-processed in Photoshop rather than output in highly deterministic visualization software such as Lumion, Sketchup, etc. Consider what aspects of image creation will be handled in Rhino vs. Photoshop. Refer to lecture given on digital imagery in AD3, refer to image making techniques and process introduced in Digital Constructs 3 and 4.

*Deliverables – Three-Dimensional Imagery (Due at Final Review 4/12):*

Three rendered views of the new addition in relation to existing building and site. Size and format of images to be determined with section leader’s guidance

8. **Project Manifesto**

A manifesto is a concise, incisive declaration of intent; it is an argument for a position taken. Write a manifesto for the project describing the following:

- \- how the project addresses lessons learned in precedent analysis
- \- how the housing units provide a unique residential experience
- \- how housing and public programs respond to context and site analysis
- \- the aesthetic approach of the project
- \- any other aspects of the project deemed most important

*Deliverables – Project manifesto (Due at Final Review 4/12):*

300-word text graphically incorporated into final presentation boards.

## Learning Outcomes

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes (CLOs):

- 1\. Architectural Documentation Skills – Students will demonstrate ability to accurately construct a series of graphically refined two-dimensional drawings of an existing building from reference drawings.
- 2\. Architectural Analysis Skills – Students will demonstrate ability to communicate an analytical reading of an existing building in a series of clear graphically annotated diagrams.
- 3\. Site Analysis Skills – Students will demonstrate ability to understand and document site context in terms of the architectural qualities, configuration, and use of surrounding buildings; transportation infrastructures; zoning regulations; and solar orientation.
- 4\. Generative Modeling and Drawing – Students will demonstrate ability to think abstractly and imaginatively in two and three dimensions and will demonstrate how abstraction informs an architectural parti.
- 5\. Programming and Massing – Students will demonstrate ability to understand and organize an architectural program and show how it informs building massing situated on site.
- 6\. Zoning – Students will demonstrate how their design meets City of Troy zoning requirements.
- 7\. Solar Orientation – Students will demonstrate how their design is oriented to solar exposure and how the design responds to direct sunlight.
- 8\. Multi-Unit Housing Design – Students will demonstrate ability to design different types of housing units in terms of their interior configurations and in terms of how units relate to the building as a whole. Students must show how individual units aggregate, relate to structural and plumbing systems, light and air requirements, circulation, and egress.
- 9\. Architectural Assemblies – Through detailed drawings and models, students will demonstrate ability to understand and design realistic structural thicknesses and assemblies of wall, floor, and roof components.
- 10\. Two-dimensional Orthographic Drawing – Students will demonstrate ability to clearly represent architecture and accurately design a building at a range of scales from site to architectural assembly through the use of two-dimensional plans, section and elevations.
- 11\. Large-scale Physical Modeling – Students will demonstrate ability to construct a large-scale physical model that represents a portion of a building in detail. Students must demonstrate ability to bring together multiple materials and ability to skillfully craft a physical model.
- 12\. Three-dimensional Imagery – Students must demonstrate ability to compellingly communicate conceptual and aesthetic aspects of an architectural project.
- 13\. Writing – Students must demonstrate ability to communicate an architectural agenda clearly and concisely through using writing as a complement to graphic and physical production.

**Pin-ups**

Periodically students will be required to present their work publicly in a formal review process. These presentations are a critical part of the architectural education. It is an opportunity for the student and the faculty to judge the project and critically evaluate the process of design. It may be appropriate at points in the semester to evaluate only selected projects in order to convey information relevant to the entire class. In this situation it is important for each student to witness and participate in the review process. Even if your project is not being reviewed, the criticism and discussion should inform the evaluation of your own progress and design.

**Desk Critiques**

The design studio environment involves two-way communication. The role of the studio critic is to evolve and strengthen the student’s design process. Every time the student meets with the faculty, he or she must have new iterations of work based on previous conversations. If the student has not produced new work, the faculty reserves the right to skip the critique and continue with other students who have produced new material.

### Additional Academic Integrity Course Policy and Penalty Information:

**Academic Integrity**

The Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities defines various forms of Academic Dishonesty and procedures for responding to them. All forms are violations of the trust between students and teachers. Student-teacher relationships are built on trust. For example, students must trust that teachers have made appropriate decisions about the structure and content of the courses they teach, and teachers must trust that the assignments that students turn in are their own performance. Acts that violate this trust undermine the educational process.

**Policy on collaboration and cheating/plagiarism**

Interdisciplinary collaboration is a critical component of this course. All assignments that are turned in for a grade must accurately represent the student’s own work, and properly cite authorship of work that was not solely completed by the student. Any miss-representation of work shall be evaluated and could result in a failing grade for the assignment. A subsequent infraction by the student will result in a failing grade for the course.
