## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

**“Introduction to Artificial Intelligence”** is a practical, critical, and hands-on introduction to modern AI systems and their impact on society. The course is designed for students who want to understand not only how AI systems work, but also how they shape knowledge, decision-making, labor, institutions, and everyday life.

Students will explore major approaches to artificial intelligence, including symbolic AI, machine learning, deep learning, large language models, semantic search, retrieval-augmented generation, knowledge graphs, and AI agents. A central theme of the course is **semantics**: how AI systems represent, transform, retrieve, generate, and sometimes distort meaning.

Throughout the course, students will build small AI prototypes, analyze real-world AI systems, evaluate model behavior, and critique the social and ethical implications of AI deployment. Topics include data and labels, embeddings, prompting, hallucination, retrieval, evaluation, bias, privacy, explainability, automation, and responsible AI design.

The course culminates in a team-based final project in which students design, build, and critically evaluate a small AI system or conduct a structured AI audit. Projects will require both technical implementation and reflection on semantic design, failure modes, risks, and societal impact.

Final projects will normally be completed in teams of two. If enrollment numbers require it, one team of three may be formed. Teams will be provisional during the first week and finalized by the end of Week 2 to allow for enrollment changes. If a student adds or drops the course after teams are formed, the instructor may reassign students, merge teams, allow a student to continue individually, or adjust project scope as needed.

## Learning Outcomes

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes (CLOs):

- \- Explain major AI paradigms Describe key approaches to artificial intelligence, including symbolic AI, machine learning, deep learning, generative AI, retrieval systems, and AI agents.
- \- Analyze AI as a system of representation and meaning Explain how AI systems represent meaning through symbols, labels, categories, embeddings, prompts, metadata, ontologies, knowledge graphs, and documents.
- \- Build practical AI prototypes Develop small AI applications or experiments using methods such as classification, semantic search, prompting, retrieval-augmented generation, or structured knowledge representation.
- \- Evaluate AI system behavior Assess AI systems using criteria such as accuracy, reliability, hallucination, evidence quality, robustness, explainability, and user trust.
- \- Identify limitations and failure modes Analyze where AI systems break down, including errors caused by poor data, ambiguous categories, weak semantic representation, biased assumptions, or inappropriate deployment contexts.
- \- Critically examine the social impact of AI Discuss how AI systems affect fairness, privacy, labor, education, institutions, access, surveillance, accountability, and power.
- \- Apply responsible AI principles Design safeguards, human oversight mechanisms, refusal boundaries, documentation, and responsible use recommendations for AI systems.
- \- Communicate AI concepts clearly Present technical and critical analyses of AI systems to an audience, including system design, semantic choices, evaluation results, risks, and limitations.
- \- Collaborate on an AI project Work effectively in a small team to design, build, evaluate, and present an AI prototype or structured AI audit.
- \- Reflect on AI, semantics, and society Articulate how questions of meaning, data, models, and social context shape the design and consequences of AI systems.

- ### Academic Integrity

  Student-teacher relationships are built on trust. For example, students must trust that teachers have made appropriate decisions about the structure and content of the courses they teach, and teachers must trust that the assignments that students turn in are their own. Acts that violate this trust undermine the educational process. The Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities and The Graduate Student Supplement define various forms of Academic Dishonesty and you should make yourself familiar with these. In this class, all assignments that are turned in for a grade must represent the student’s own work. In cases where help was received, or teamwork was allowed, a notation on the assignment should indicate your collaboration.

  Violations of academic integrity may also be reported to the appropriate Dean (Dean of Students for undergraduate students or the Dean of Graduate Education for graduate students, respectively).

  If you have any question concerning this policy before submitting an assignment, please ask for clarification.
