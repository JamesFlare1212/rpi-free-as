## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

This course will introduce students to literary works, both classic and contemporary, and give students the opportunity to develop and practice skills in the art of creative writing, including attention to image, energy, tension, and pattern. A writer’s relationship to their work deepens and changes when reading and writing become a regular habit. Therefore, the course format will be based on a number of short readings and exercises weekly, both in class and for homework, in addition to three completed drafts of creative writing (narrative, poetry, drama). A primary aspect of the course is engaging in a writing community: discussion, peer review, informal critique, and workshop will be key components. Student participation is the heart of this course.

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes Assessment Measures:

**Creative Project Portfolios (3):** The project portfolio consists of 1) A draft of a creative writing project 2) A non-evaluative response to peer project(s) 3) A brief project reflection.

**Craft Discussion Boards:** This is an asynchronous class activity in which students participate in a discussion of creative writing craft via LMS. This assignment consists of responding, via short essay, to a prompt on the weekly craft discussion board and responding to two peer posts. Your initial post and responses will be evaluated for depth of understanding, specificity, appropriate use of textual references, and creativity.  
**End Comments.** These are written responses to peer workshop submissions, to be crafted and submitted before workshop. Good feedback can be short or long, so I won't impose an arbitrary length on yours. Your stance should be that of a careful and interested reader. You're making suggestions or observations the writer can take into the next draft or into other pieces. Your response should indicate 1) what you felt the objective of the piece was 2) which aspects of the piece with which you connected 3) which aspects of the piece felt disengaging, confusing, or otherwise less effective.

**Writer’s Notebook.** Throughout the session, you will be keeping a notebook as a “catch-all” for your creativity. I will regularly assign notebook activities that will help you prepare for class, and I will invite students to share their works. During class, we will also use the writer’s notebook to reflect on our time together. Furthermore, students are encouraged to use this notebook more often than assigned, to capture thoughts, ideas, observations, sensations—anything that will keep the creativity flowing. The writer’s notebook will be turned in on Week Three and again on Week Six at the end of the session. Completion references assigned entries and any additional entries you choose to make. It will be evaluated as follows: Complete = 100%; Majority Complete = 70%; Majority Incomplete = 0%-10%.

**Mission Statement & Final Reflection.** In weeks one and six you will be writing a letter to yourself or to a person who inspires you creatively. In week one, this letter will focus on what you hope to explore in the course. In week six, you will respond to your week one letter, reflecting on how your goals (potentially) shifted and how you showed up for your creative self. The grading criteria are: timeliness, formatting/proofreading, and authenticity.

This is a small seminar class, the success of which is entirely dependent on active student participation. Therefore, I expect all students to attend every class and participate to the extent of their abilities. ### Additional Academic Integrity Course Policy and Penalty Information:

Student-instructor relationships are built on trust. For example, students must trust that instructors have made appropriate decisions about the structure and content of the courses they teach, and must trust that the assignments that students turn in are their own. Acts that violate this trust undermine the educational process. The Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities and The Graduate Student Supplement define various forms of Academic Dishonesty and you are responsible for becoming familiar with these. Some examples of plagiarism include copying, paraphrasing, or summarizing without acknowledgment, submission of another student's work as one's own, the purchase/use of prepared research or completed papers or projects, and the unacknowledged use of research sources gathered by someone else. Failure to indicate accurately the extent and precise nature of one's reliance on other sources is also a form of plagiarism. Students are responsible for understanding legitimate use of sources, the appropriate ways of acknowledging academic, scholarly, or creative indebtedness. All assignments that are turned in for a grade must represent the student’s own work completed for this class in particular. In cases where help was received, or teamwork was allowed, a notation on the assignment should indicate your collaboration. If you have a compelling reason for wanting to use previous work of yours for the class, it must be substantially and fundamentally revised, and you must get approval from the instructor first.

Submission of any assignment that is in violation of this policy will result in a penalty of a zero for the assignment and the potential to fail the course. Second offenses will result in automatic failure of the course and the potential for disciplinary action by the institution.

If you have any question concerning this policy before submitting an assignment, please ask for clarification.
