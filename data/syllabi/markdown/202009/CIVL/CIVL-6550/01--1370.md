## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

The application of geotechnical engineering to the environmental area. Deals with waste disposal, waste containment systems, waste stabilization, and landfills. Emphasis on design of such facilities. Includes related topics necessary for design, e.g., geosynthetics, groundwater, contaminant transport, and slurry walls. Some field trips are possible. (Students cannot receive credit for both this CIVL 4140 and CIVL 6550).

Topics covered:

- Brief review of background material related to geotechnical engineering, chemistry (inorganic and organic), containment transport, groundwater hydrology, etc.
- Regulations governing waste disposal
- Introduction to geosynthetics
- Landfill siting
- Conceptual design
- Liner design
- Slope stability and settlement
- Leachate generation and collection
- Final cover
- Evaluation after construction

Lectures are the same for 4140 and 6550. 6550 students will receive additional assignments, extra questions on exams, and will be a group leader for the term project. CIVL 6550 students may be required to read additional professional papers and publications, and do additional laboratory experiments.

## Course Goals

### Goals:

The field of Geoenvironmental Engineering covers a wide range of topics and engineering skills. This is reflected in the suggested textbook and the wide range of reference texts on this topic. For example, in Part I of the Sharma & Reddy textbook, which includes background material, each chapter could easily represent one or more courses of study if one were to have a proper background in those areas. Examples: geotechnical engineering, chemistry (inorganic and organic), contaminant transport, groundwater hydrology, etc. Students cannot be expected to have proper backgrounds in all those areas, regardless of their field of engineering or science, and it is not possible to cover all the topic areas in great detail. Thus, the course can be considered an introduction to the field of geoenvironmental engineering, and a survey of the various background areas needed to practice geoenvironmental engineering. As a result, the course requires significant reading and independent exploration, with much less emphasis on rigorous analytical and mathematical techniques (as opposed to basic techniques, which must be mastered).

### Additional Academic Integrity Course Policy and Penalty Information:

Some assessments in this course, like exams, have a clearly marked place on the front page that requires a signature confirming academic integrity. If you forget to sign the document before submitting it, I will contact you directly to ask you to sign before grading your exam. I expect that you will complete exams without speaking to other students via another communication channel. **You cannot interact with Chegg.com or any other online/internet assignment sharing system for any exams or term projects.** Interaction with it results in an automatic failure for the entire course. A result of the violation using online resources to assist in graded assignments will result in a formal report to the Dean of Students. There are ways that I can detect your use of these online resources; please do not compromise academic integrity.

***Engineering is an important and learned profession. As members of this profession, engineers are expected to exhibit the highest standards of honesty and integrity. Engineering has a direct and vital impact on the quality of life for all people. Accordingly, the services provided by engineers require honesty, impartiality, fairness, and equity, and must be dedicated to the protection of the public health, safety, and welfare. Engineers must perform under a standard of professional behavior that requires adherence to the highest principles of ethical conduct. In addition to the consequences outlined in the Student Handbook, I ask you to consider how Academic Dishonesty devalues a Rensselaer degree. Indeed, employers and students, past and present, place great value in the rigor of an RPI degree.***

Intellectual integrity and credibility are the foundation of all academic work. A violation of Academic Integrity policy is, by definition, considered a flagrant offense to the educational process. It is taken seriously by students, faculty, and Rensselaer and will be addressed in an effective manner. If found responsible for committing academic dishonesty, a student may be subject to one or both types of penalties: an academic (grade) penalty administered by the professor and/or disciplinary action through the Rensselaer judicial process described in this handbook. Three relevant academic integrity violations to emphasize include:

**Collaboration:** Collaboration is defined as deliberately facilitating an act of academic dishonesty in any way or form; for example, allowing another student to observe an exam paper or allowing another student to "recycle" one's old term paper or using one another's work in a paper or lab report without citing it as another's work.

**Copying:** Copying is defined as obtaining information pertaining to a graded exercise by deliberately observing the paper of another student; for example, noting which alternative a neighboring student has circled on a multiple-choice exam.

**Plagiarism:** Plagiarism is defined as representing the work or words of another as one's own through the omission of acknowledgment or reference. Examples include using sentences verbatim from a published source in a term paper without appropriate referencing, or presenting as one's own the detailed argument of a published source, or presenting as one's own electronically or digitally enhanced graphic representations from any form of media.

*Homework:* Students are permitted and encouraged to work together on homework, as described above. Seeking help from other sources is also encouraged. However, the work you submit should be that of you and your group, developed collaboratively (not independently). Evidence of your merely transcribing another person’s homework will result in a zero grade for the homework assignment in question, **for all parties involved**. A second offense will result in a zero grade for homework for the entire semester **for all parties involved**, with a report made to the Dean of Students Office.

*Exams:* Collaboration during an exam (exchanging verbal or written information or copying) is, of course, prohibited. Also prohibited is the use of unauthorized materials during an examination. In addition, the use of email, text messaging, or the internet during exams for any purpose is strictly prohibited. All parties involved will receive an academic penalty, which will range from a numerical grade of zero for the exam to a failing grade in the course, deemed by the instructor to be appropriate to the infraction. Submission of an exam for re-grading after having changed an answer will result in a similar academic penalty. All incidents will be reported to the Dean of Students.

*Projects and Other:* Outrageous offenses such as arranging for a substitute to take an examination, attempting to obtain examination questions or solutions in advance, plagiarizing any material for the term project, or attempting to change a grade record will result in a failing grade for the course and a recommendation to the Dean of Students that the student(s) in question be suspended.
