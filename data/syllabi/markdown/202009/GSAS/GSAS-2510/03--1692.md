## Course Goals

### Goals:

Games are user experiences, and game designers are those who craft, develop, and (de)construct those experiences. This course will help refine your game design ability by: 1.) broadening the range of activities you consider to be “game design,” 2.) providing you with analytic lenses to deconstruct and understand the affordances, constraints, successes, failures, and social dimensions of existing games 3.) incorporating themes, mechanics, and abstractions from “non-game” media and texts into game design, 4.) training you in effective reading, writing, and communication skills in order to better collaborate with interdisciplinary teams, work with players, and pitch game ideas, and 5.) teaching you how to listen to--and not just hear--your players, users, teammates, clients, and self.

## Course Content

### Content Details:

The best way to learn how to design games is to design games. The major mechanic of the course will be the use of game design processes to rapidly iterate and playtest game prototypes. As such, this course will be an intensive marathon in game design, where students will work both as individuals and in teams to develop 6 polished early-stage concepts or prototypes (and their accompanying documentation) by the end of the semester. Students will also reflect upon the philosophical and social dimensions of games, and how to strategize for those dimensions in game design.

Some of the core principles that will be practiced in each assignment are:

**Brainstorming and mindmapping** – tools and methods for generating lots of ideas, identifying promising ideas and combinations of ideas, being comfortable with generating more bad ideas than good, combating early stage lock-in and brute force design.

**“Paper” Prototyping and Playtesting** – your ideas become real by building and testing them. This means that you can’t plan out all of the best parts of your game--or prevent the bugs in your game--in your head. Paper prototyping allows you to cheaply and quickly test different mechanics, narratives, and aesthetics for your game with different audiences. The more playtesting you do, and the more different kinds of audiences you playtest with, the more you will learn about your *own game*, and the more you can push it to be better. Remember, the point of playtesting is to see where and how your game succeeds and fails, so embrace all the failures and problems your game will inevitably have.

**Holographic Essential Experiences** – All games have essential experiences: feelings, memories, callbacks, and ludonarratives that players come to know while playing the game. Some of the strongest game designs have a clear vision of their game’s essential experiences, and develop them *holographically* throughout the game. To design holographically means to infuse your game’s essential experience throughout every element of the game, so that if the game were “shattered,” players could still feel the essential experience in each shard. For example, if an essential experience of a game were “sadness,” players could experience sadness by reading the narrative, viewing the art, and manipulating the game’s mechanics. The essential experience of sadness is heightened and made more meaningful through the combinations of these experience-inflected parts of the design.

**Your Identity as a Designer** – No matter your role on later design teams (programmers, artists, writers, musicians, etc.), you are in part responsible for creating the essential experience of the game for your players—doubly so when following the principles of holographic design. This means always foregrounding the players’ experiences in your work, developing your own voice as a designer, and learning to listen to players and other designers of your game. It also means that you have a stake in the overall design of your game. In this class, well will work against the trend towards modularization in game development—the idea that everyone has a specific role to play, and a specific part to build, that are then assembled to make a game. Instead, every member of the team is part of the development of the game’s design and playtesting. Even when group members inevitably work separately, they are always working clearly towards the essential experiences of their games.

Overall, this is a labor-intensive course that requires a sustained effort throughout the semester. Students will need to start on their projects early, reach out to playtesters and their group members efficiently and empathetically, and dedicate adequate time to polish, iterate, and hone their game design concepts throughout the course. In general, it is expected that you will spend between 8 to 10 hours every week outside of class time on your reading, prototyping, playtesting, and writing. Always be thinking about your portfolio.

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes Assessment Measures:

**Game Design Challenges**

There will be two kinds of game design challenges in this class: individual “concept” sprint assignments and larger group “build” assignments.

Concept challenges are generally short, 1-week sprints that require a short, polished design document, but do not require a full build of a game. While we require that you source feedback (via the class Discord page), you do not necessarily have to run a playtest of your concept (though we always encourage you to do so!). Please post in-progress sketches and ideas to our class Discord. All students must post and respond at least once per week, but the class is small enough that we hopefully will be able to have more sustained conversations throughout the week.

For every Concept challenge, the student must submit a single PDF that includes:

- ○ A title page that gives the name of the game and an “abstract” that describes the game in three or four sentences
- ○ A set of complete game rules, written in accessible language (gender-neutral language, and must be written in a way that allows players to “pick-up-and-play”)
- ○ A description (250-500 words) that outlines the prelusory goals and extrinsic/intrinsic motivations for players
- ○ A detailed (1000 words) description of the game goals, the player means, the game rules, states of play, and actions that trigger or change states of play, and how those elements are contributing two prelusory goals and player motivation
- ○ Mock up “Beauty shots” of the game pitch. These can include elements like character concept art, background art, rough level designs, interface designs, etc., but also **must include** mock-ups of “screenshots” from the game, including screens that illustrate beginning-state play, major potential actions or game-state changes, and end-state triggers. For live-action games, users should take pictures of expected role-play, and document actions and changing play states in those images.

Build challenges are longer group assignments that generally unfold over the course of three weeks. Builds require multiple, iterative prototypes and playtests of your concept, thorough playtesting and prototyping documentation, and revisions that both increase in polish and respond to player and designer playtesting feedback. Student groups will typically have three iterative deadlines for each Build project, designed to get both instructor and fellow student feedback, as well as to check in on external playtesting progress.

For every Build challenge, the student group will turn in (one per group to LMS):

- The actual presentation prototype game materials (dropped off or as a .ZIP folder, depending on prototype medium)
- A single PDF that includes:
  - ○ A title page that gives the name of the game, the names of each group member, and an “abstract” that describes the game in three or four sentences
  - ○ A set of complete game rules, written in accessible language (gender-neutral language, and must be written in a way that allows players to “pick-up-and-play”)
  - ○ Photos of the prototyping process, with image captions and descriptions under each image
  - ○ “Beauty shots” of the “final” game assets (physical and/or digital), as well as the final game being played, with image captions and descriptions under each image
  - ○ A design process statement – a 1500 word statement that describes the group’s design process, brainstorming/mind-mapping/initial design direction, the feedback from playtesting sessions, and design iterations

**Individual “Concept” Challenges:**

**One Button Design Challenge (Due Date Friday, September 11)**

Students will conceptualize a digital one-button game. The game should take place in a single screen environment without scrolling, and game input is limited to a single button (i.e., no D-pad or button modifiers [like holding down a shoulder button]). Students should design a game that they feel will offer challenge and fun, but that uses the single button in interesting and even unexpected ways. As you begin thinking about your project, make sure to start by brainstorming different platform configurations that could reasonably be called “one-button.” We will do some demonstrations of this kind of brainstorming work in class.

- Focus on a significant use of the limited input.
- Interpret the subject in a clear and interesting way and present a concept that could be implementable under normal work conditions.
- Develop an unambiguous ruleset for the game, a list of behavior of any component of the game, the goal, the winning and losing condition, and the evolution of the play sessions over time.

**Affordance Challenge (Due Date Friday, October 9)**

We recognize the importance of affordances when the design of an item is missing them, or when affordances and constraints operate unexpectedly. This is as true for physical environments as it is for games. On RPI’s campus (or your local environment), take pictures of affordance challenges that you encounter, analyze them, and how to fix or interact with them in a playful way. As you seek affordances to play with, remember that affordances are relationship-based, not property based. That is, what may be a useful affordance for some users may be invisible to or even a hindrance for other users. Also remember that there are several overlapping dimensions of affordances/constraints, and not all are physical. As a reminder, the affordances/constraints we will discuss in class include:

1. **Physical:** affordances and constraints that primarily work in materials ways. A key may only fit in one kind of slot, a Tetris piece can be rotated to fit into several different kinds of places, or to build new slots.
2. **Semantic:** affordances and constraints that operate due to social rules and commonly held interface beliefs. A “red light” at a crosswalk does not physically prevent a car from moving, but it is remarkably effective at creating a “Stop” action.
3. **Cultural:** affordances and constraints that have no predetermined rules, but work because of broader cultural and social conventions. Typically, students know not to sit at the “teacher station” when they enter a classroom, even though that rule has never explicitly been communicated to them. It is considered rude or weird to enter an elevator and stare at another person or at the wall, rather than turning around and facing towards the doors.
4. **Logical:** affordances and constraints that operate by implying a logical relationship. The most common example are dials for oven burners; we expect that the dials will be arranged such that we don’t need a chart to tell us which dial turns on which burner. When they are not arranged in such a way, users often “slip” and light the wrong burner.

**Fix-A-Game (Due Date Friday, November 13)**

Students will be assigned a “broken” game—a game of chance where the mechanics themselves are not meaningful—and will prototype a “more meaningful” type of gameplay for that game. Students should:

- Develop a ruleset for the broken game
- Consider what may be meaningful for the broken game (for example, roulette is both broken and very meaningful)
- Develop an iteration of the game that creates more meaningful play (we’ll describe this more in class)
- Not stray so far from the original themes and meaning of the game that the iteration becomes unrecognizable

“Meaningful Play” in this design challenge should be more than just attaching a progressive game mechanic to your prior game. For example, you could, in theory, make Roulette more meaningful by turning it into a board game and using the spinner as the random-number generator to determine moves on the board, similar to the “Game of Life.” But that change also doesn’t take into account some of the already more meaningful elements of roulette (the betting, the weighing of percentages, the opportunity for the gambler’s fallacy, etc.), and “fixes” the game by essentially stapling another game to it. Think carefully about interesting ways you can make the game itself more or differently meaningful, gripping, emotional, etc. that don’t involve completely changing the essential experiences of the game.

**Group “Build” Challenges:**

**Asynchronous Game (Group Size 3, Due Date Tuesday, September 29 )**

One of the challenges of this semester is that we’re going to be in a hybrid environment, both in space and time. As such, your first two group Build challenges will have you engage with these constraints as play possibilities.

The first challenge explored play modalities across time. Students will design a multiplayer game that can be played asynchronously--that is, the players do not have to be playing at the same time in order for the game to continue. While, in theory, every turn-based game could be considered asynchronous (a player in online asynchronous Civ, for example, could theoretically take days to make a move), your game should be designed so that the asynchronicity is a key meaningful gameplay element. While the majority of the early playtesting should be done on paper/in a physical space, students are allowed to think about the game as taking place in a primarily digital space. However, students should be sure to not rely on “digital magic” in their playtesting to paper over gameplay, essential experience, or player interaction concerns.

As you start working with your group, you should thinking about different examples of successful asynchronous games and play--there are lots of different ways to create meaningful play dispersed in time, and not all of them involve digital technology. Some we’ve come up with to get you started:

- Words with Friends (digital mobile game, two-player but multiple play sessions at once)
- Geocaching (hybrid digital-physical game, one player setting a GPS cache, with one or more players navigating to it)
- Chess-by-Mail (analog board game, two players, mixture of gameplay and socialization)
- Paint-by-Numbers Community Mural (a designed mural with labeled spaces for community members to fill in according to the appropriate number. Sometimes done synchronously in a party, sometimes over time. Following numbers is generally encouraged, but not mandated, leading to some fun transgressive play)

Some questions to help guide your group as you begin thinking about your design:

- What do you do to design/research an enjoyable asynchronous, to encourage your players to come back?
- Do you build in time limits?
- Do you allow open time?
- Is it closed-loop with players (e.g., a private game of “Words with Friends” or “skribbl”) or open, where different players can enter and leave play?

First Concept must outline basic hybrid gameplay, the number of players, and those players’ roles, means, rules, and goals. Concept should also demonstrate thought put into the platform/delivery mechanisms of the game, and why the game will be fun or meaningful to play even with potential long breaks in play.

Rough Prototype must demonstrate basic gameplay elements, rules, and goals of the game in a playtestable state. While no advanced material prototyping is required at this stage, students must have a general plan of what/how many assets need to be constructed, and the materials and time required to do so (this, of course, may change after playtesting). Students should also have a plan for testing physical elements of the game digitally, or digital elements of the game physically, depending on the needs of the game and our situation regarding COVID quarantining.

Detailed Prototype must demonstrate design iterations post playtesting, proofs-of-concept for the game assets, early concept art, and advanced aesthetic intention for the game. It must also demonstrate changes and iterations based upon Discord feedback, faculty/student feedback, and playtester feedback.

Final concept and report must demonstrate further playtesting refinement and pivots, polished final assets, and mockups of finalized concept.

**Hybrid Game Design (Group Size 3, Due Date Friday, November 3)**

The second challenge, “Hybrid Game Design,” will focus on mixed reality or hybridized digital and physical space. Game must be played by at least two players, one operating primarily in a digital modality, and one operating primarily in physical modality. Players are allowed to use the “opposite” modality to communicate with one another (e.g. physical players could use a smartphone or laptop, digital players could yell out the window), but otherwise must engage in play primarily in their modality.

When developing the game, think about games that are already successful in doing this. One great example is the VR game “Keep Talking and Nobody Explodes,” but you can also draw inspiration from pre-digitaal “hybrid” games like “Marco-Polo” or “Pictionary,” or hybrid game-adjacent events, like a physical/virtual marathon.

First Concept must outline basic hybrid gameplay, the number of players, and those players’ roles, means, rules, and goals. Concept should also demonstrate thought put into the platform/delivery mechanisms of the game, and how the affordances of the digital platform and presumed physical spaces will interact. Be sure to think clearly about things like physical accessibility and personal safety.

Rough Prototype must demonstrate basic gameplay elements, rules, and goals of the game in a playtestable state. While no advanced material prototyping is required at this stage, students must have a general plan of what/how many assets need to be constructed, and the materials and time required to do so (this, of course, may change after playtesting). Students should also have a plan for testing physical elements of the game digitally, or digital elements of the game physically, depending on the needs of the game and our situation regarding COVID quarantining.

Detailed Prototype must demonstrate design iterations post playtesting, proofs-of-concept for the game assets, early concept art, and advanced aesthetic intention for the game. It must also demonstrate changes and iterations based upon Discord feedback, faculty/student feedback, and playtester feedback.

Final concept and report must demonstrate further playtesting refinement and pivots, polished final assets, and mockups of finalized digital interface/playscreen and its interaction with physical play.

**Grow-A-Game (Group Size 3, Due Date Friday December 11)**

In our final project, we will explore both how games can contribute to better understandings of social phenomena, as well as how games always already have social, cultural, and political values embedded within them. As designers, it is our job to be acutely aware of the societal implications of our game narratives, mechanics, and aesthetics (see, for example, the recent Ubisoft pro-facist/anti-BLM mobile game debacle, which we will explore more in class).

In the Grow-A-Game Challenge, student groups will produce a game that embodies a game designer’s social responsibilities as an artist, designer, and person within society. The game redesign and social responsibility will be highly constrained by the instructor through the Grow-A-Game system. Students must then:

- Research the social issue assigned
- Research other games that have done this work
- Design a game that incorporates the social issue—importantly, this design must be holographic; the social issue must permeate all aspects of the redesigned game
- Must incorporate polished design components

Note that the game should showcase a well-researched perspective on the social issue, and should serve to **\*make an argument\*,** rather than attempting to portray a neutral of “both-sides”-ism approach to an issue. The game should also not simply be a “simulation” of the social issue but rather an exploration of it that highlights aspects the designers feel important, and uses the medium of games to leverage emotional and interactive experiences that might not be possible through static text.

Some useful examples of these kinds of socially-engaged games are:

- *Consentacle* (consensual sexual exploration, kink, queerness)
- *Spirit Island* (colonialism, neoliberalism, scientism)
- *Sign* (linguistics, deaf culture, the relationship between bodies, language, and play)
- *The Train Game* (fascism, information criticality and literacy, mundanity in genocide)

While replayability should always be a consideration, it will be up to the designers to balance issues like replayability and game balance/fun with argumentation and emotional impact. Of the four above games, for example, *Spirit Island* weighs heavily toward replayability, whereas *The Train Game* is designed to be played only once.

First Concept must demonstrate sufficient peer-reviewed research into the social issue, a collection of games that have explored the issue before, a tracing of the strengths and weaknesses of those explorations, and evidence of multiple rough concepts, pivots, and plusses that take into account the group’s research.

Rough Prototype must demonstrate basic gameplay elements, rules, and goals of the game, and must be in a playtestable state. While no advanced material prototyping is required at this stage, players must have a general plan of what/how many assets need to be constructed, and the materials and time required to do so (this, of course, may change after playtesting).

Detailed Prototype must demonstrate design iterations post playtesting, proofs-of-concept for the game pieces, early concept art, and advanced aesthetic intention for the game.

Final concept and report must demonstrate further playtesting refinement and pivots, polished final assets, and evidence of the holographic deployment of the social issue.

In addition to the normal components of the submitted PDF, students should provide a section that details their research about the social issue, how they inflected that research throughout their game design, and how they were able to test the effectiveness of that issue with diverse play groups.

**Game Lenses Presentation (Due Date: Upload to YouTube and submit link to LMS sometime after Thanksgiving, but before December 15)**

Each student will be randomly assigned a game design lens from Art of Game Design, a Book of Lenses. Students will then give a 8-minute presentation describing that lens, and interpreting the mechanics, aesthetics, and narrative of a game of their choice through that lens. This presentation should be recorded and uploaded to YouTube, with a link submitted to LMS. The best presentations will make the growing list of the “Greatest GSAS Lens Archive.” Feel free to be creative and use visual and aesthetic elements in your video! Powerpoints are a minimum requirement, but think of other elements that can help get your interpretation across in interesting ways. Cosplay? Sock puppets?
