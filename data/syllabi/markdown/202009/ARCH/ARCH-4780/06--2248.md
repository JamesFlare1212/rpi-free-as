## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

**SUBVERSIVE INFRASTRUCTURE Introduction[in progress]**

This studio proposes a multitrack approach for the investigaton a new architecture in tropical forrest environments.

The tropical forest presents itself as a paradigmatic - and often mythical - image in the collective imagination. A place of wonder and fear, survival and profit. It is also a context of ephemerality, where the architectural desire for permanence is in continuous under threath. All buildings seems to enter immediate decadence. Not strangely, the tropics have been noted as the place where ‘modernism came to die”.

In a context of buidings with non-thermal bridges (!), initial shelter needs appears very much essential: well-ventilated spaces, that protect life from sun and rain (and living threadts), anchored firmly but lightly to a sometimes unstable soft ground. This initial premise may however unravel a basic question -- what and how to build? -- that may appear simplistic, but are the core issues of the design practice politics in the tropics.

What would be the the territitorial strategies and architecture languages -- and the manner of building them -- that could signal new paths for tropical urban enclaves. What programs could corporate and harness the preservation of the natural conditions, while at the same time promote and equal access to shared benefits and progress of the common good? How local conditions and needs are balanced against global pressures? After all further economic development in the region does threaten the global ecological equilibrium, however prevalent notions of ecological preservation may condemns the region inhabitants to poverty and general stagnation. Are there other options than the ‘indigenous’ alternative retreats for the globetrotting eco-touristic tribes -- more than often explotative of local culture (just think some recent lecturesat our school by John Hardy and Roth) with little peripheral benefit for the local communities,

Ours studio aims for non-patronizing, even un-romantically and brutal, approach in the quest to rethink these paradigms. Population will increase, therefore industries, farming, commerce, transportation will (and should) happen. How could both local development and global ecological equilibrium be balanced (and even accelerated)?

## Course Content

### Content Details:

The semester’s work will be organized in 3 parallel tracks:

- Track 1> TECTONIC REHEARSALS: hand-on physical explorations looking for a radical update of local appropriate (low, craft) technologies. Through a soft constructivism approach, students will develop novel explorations of three-dimensional structures combining finite linear elements and planar and/or textile materials. The emphasis will equally placed in their formal and spatial ambition, as well in their procedural building logic and economical contextual pertinence. Individual work

- Track 2 > SITE RECITE: reading and discussion seminars, presentations of critical topics (some assigned by the faculty, others identified and proposed by the students) aiming to unravel the complex matrix of issues and opposed and contradictorical interests converging in these types of contexts. The final objective is the development of a comprehensive series of synthetic diagrams that present the student political thesis. Cluster or team work.

- Track 3 > SUBVERSIVE INFRASTRUCTURE: students will develop two projects -- of different sites, scales and (even) design speeds:
  - 3.1 & 3.3 SLOW TRACK: students will articulate architectural argumentations at an urban scale that may emulate, predict, and inform patterns for the development of incremental urbanism projects and tactical urban theory. Different cities along the Amazon River will be assigned to clusters of students, who may choose to work individually or as teams -- correspondingly the faculty will adjust the scale, scope and deliverables.
  - 3.2 FAST TRACK (competition): in the middle (or end) of the semester, students will engage for three weeks in a real-life competition for the proposal of a sustainable compound for a coastal site in Costa Rica. This competition was proposed and will be funded by Shane Gavitt (class 2015). More details will be provided as the semester progresses.

## Learning Outcomes

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes (CLOs):

- Students will have develop and improved their “haptic thinking” skills; understood the importance of craft in relation to architecture; and have a practical notion of high and low technological ways of thinking and making.
- Students will have explored and developed structures that “touch the Earth lightly” (Murcutt); as well articulated and developed complex tectonics proposals, such as the concept of “textile tectonics”, in particular in their application to architecture.
- Students will have explored in an applied manner theoretical notions such as informal, “mestizaje”, “antropofagia”, hybridity, etc. that have informed the development of “other modernities”, not just in the tropics but other regions of the “global south.
- Students will have identified and interpreted social, economical, environmental and political constraints and opportunities, and exploit them as conceptual grounding in their design proposals; claim the urban and infrastructural as design scales pertinent to the field of architectural design; and develop design proposals that interweave structure, systems and program in comprehensive works of architecture.

### Additional Academic Integrity Course Policy and Penalty Information:

The following statement regarding academic dishonesty is taken verbatim from The Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities Intellectual integrity and credibility are the foundation of all academic work. Academic dishonesty is, by definition, considered a flagrant offense to the educational process. It is taken seriously by students, faculty, and Rensselaer and will be addressed in an effective manner.

If found in violation of academic dishonesty policy, students may be subject to two types of penalties: the instructor administers an academic (grade) penalty, and the student may be subject to the procedures and penalties of the student judicial system outlined in this handbook.

Since academic dishonesty is a violation of the Grounds for Disciplinary Action, the student may be subject to any of the following szanctions: disciplinary warning; disciplinary probation; disciplinary suspension, expulsion and/or alternative actions as agreed on by the student and hearing officer. It should be noted that no student who allegedly commits academic dishonesty will be able to drop or change the grade option for the course in question.

The definitions and examples presented below are samples of types of academic dishonesty and are not to be construed as an exhaustive or exclusive list. The academic dishonesty policy applies to all students, undergraduate and graduate, and to scholarly pursuits and research. Additionally, attempts to commit academic dishonesty or to assist in the commission or attempt of such an act are also violations of this policy.

Academic Fraud: The alteration of documentation relating to the grading process. For example, changing exam solutions to negotiate for a higher grade or tampering with an instructor’s grade book.

Collaboration: Deliberate facilitation of academic dishonesty in any form. For example, allowing another student to observe an exam paper or allowing another student to “recycle” one’s old term paper or using oneanother’s work in a paper or lab report without citing it as another’s work.

Copying: Obtaining information pertaining to a graded exercise by deliberately observing the paper of another student. For example, noting which alternative a neighboring student has circled on a multiple-choice exam.

Cribbing: Use or attempted use of prohibited materials, information, or study aids in an academic exercise. For example, using an unauthorized formal sheet during an exam.

Fabrication: Unauthorized falsification or invention of any information in an academic exercise. For example, use of “bought” or “ready-made” term papers, or falsifying lab records or reports.

Plagiarism: Representing the work or words of another as one’s own through the omission of acknowledgment or reference. For example, using sentences verbatim from a published source in a term paper without appropriate referencing, or presenting as one’s own the detailed argument of a published source, or presenting as one’s own electronically or digitally enhanced graphic representations from any form of media.

Sabotage: Destruction of another student’s work. For example, destroying a model, lab experiment, computer program, or term paper developed by another student.

Substitution: Utilizing a proxy, or acting as a proxy, in any academic exercise. For example, taking an exam for another student or having a homework assignment done by someone else.

SUBMISSION OF ANY ASSIGNMENT THAT IS IN VIOLATION OF THIS POLICY WILL RESULT IN A PENALTY OF “NO CREDITS” IN THE FIRST CASE (AND THE ASSIGNMENT, PROJECT, OR EXAM CONCERNED) AND AN AUTOMATIC “F” (FAIL) FOR THE ENTIRE COURSE IF THE ISSUE REPEATS A SECOND TIME.
