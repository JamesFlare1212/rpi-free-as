## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

The Integrated Design Schematic Studio is placed at a critical juncture in the curriculum. A most  
important element is indicated by the term ‘Integrated’ in the title of the course. In addition, as  
will become clear below, the course requires the projects undertaken to address a broad range of  
design criteria required by National Architectural Accrediting Board because they are essential to  
the successful proposition of real building projects. Buildings require the synthesis of user  
requirements, compliance with building and zoning codes, accessibility requirements, structural  
necessities, and energy standards as proscribed by regulating bodies and authorities, in addition  
to program, site, and context issues. This list may seem both daunting and burdensome. But consider  
that all built projects have met, and in many cases exceeded, these requirements. Many are driven forward by their consideration.  
As one develops skill and confidence as a designer is is often by developing knowledge and  
experience  
in managing constraints and requirements in a way that facilitates innovation. This should be  
obvious from the quotations above. Many other examples can be drawn from literature, music, and  
other creative fields. But just as it’s critical to understand that constraints and creativity have  
no opposition, it is equally important to realize that the constraints do not produce the  
creativity. The designer does that. It is done by constantly probing and pushing in order to  
understand what is possible and to be able to realize the properties of the constraints, to realize  
how the various requirements operate together, and ho sometimes one constraint can be used together  
with other ones to produce the uncommon or even the new.  
This semester is the schematic phase of a two semester studio sequence that will finish with the  
Integrated Design Development Studio in a subsequent semester. In order to be effective in IDD,  
projects in IDS must achieve a level of clarity and integration that precludes any substantial  
redesign in a later semester. Therefore the primary goal of the semester is to develop a schematic  
design that resolves code and zoning issues, has a clear structural proposition, environmental  
strategy, and lucid program and circulation distributions. This is only accomplished by having a  
strong architectural concept in terms of which decisions regarding a myriad of issues can be made.  
The results of these decisions should be a mutually supporting set of decisions that operate across  
technical, environmental, and social parameters.  
These must be clearly set forth in the documents produced by the end of the semester.  
**PROJECT OVERVIEW**  
**The Post-COVID Business Center**  
The COVID pandemic revealed many things that would have taken much longer to discover in other  
circumstances. One of these was the way certain kinds of businesses transitioned from traditional office type  
workplaces to on-line work. This transition has been predicted and experimented with since the  
mid-1990s, but wide-spread experience with distributed work has, of course, never been attempted  
before. In many circumstances, it went very well. Not only was work accomplished, but it was often  
undertaken in much better balance with the rest of the worker’s life. Job satisfaction and productivity was  
often quite high. One would expect (and we will see if) this impromptu large-scale social experiment will  
result in lasting changes to some kinds of work. There are obvious ecological benefits from a reduction in  
the daily commute. Similarly, time spent in transportation can be eliminated and applied to other activities  
with family or friends. Perhaps homes will be redesigned to accommodate the residential workplace.  
Certainly, we’ll need to upgrade our internet service.  
But not everything shakes out in favor of telecommuting. There are organizations who work in a  
distributed fashion now and some of these have found the need to meet in person on a regular basis.  
Once a month, every-other-week, or at other intervals that make sense for them there is a need for a  
temporary ‘headquarters’ or ‘main office’ to have face-to-face meetings of various types. This summer, as  
part of a much larger larger post-covid discussion, I had a conversation with a building owner who had  
been hosting these kinds of meetings for an otherwise distributed networked organization for the past  
several years. Their needs were for presentations of various kinds, for discussion sessions, but also  
significantly, for receptions and meals and the attendant interpersonal conversations. Organizations need  
not only the pragmatics of accomplishing tasks but social cohesion that is engendered by social  
interaction. These organizations are able to pay a significant daily rent and catering fees, because they  
have no other real estate expenses. This suggests that there may be a business model that could  
provide these services to organizations. The project this semester is to consider the kind of facility that  
this could be, and importantly, how to provide a temporary corporate headquarters on a rental basis for  
organizations that do not have a scale which could support a flagship structure by adopting a service  
provider model.  
The design of office spaces is traditionally bifurcated into roughly two classes. The spec office building is  
generally built in order to lease the space to any of a variety of possible tenants, therefore it is generic in  
nature and often built of economical materials. It is seen as an engine of monetary profit for the  
developer or owner of the building. One can envision any number of generic modernist boxes along the  
mid-town avenues in New York City or adjacent to many cloverleafs in New Jersey. The other tradition is  
the building built as an expression of organizational aspiration, a headquarters that bears the name or  
imprint of the owner. It is often designed with a significant architectural presence that might speak about  
the identity or aspiration of the organization. They are often built with better materials and more careful  
design as a permanent home for the organization. Here one might envision SOMs Lever House in NY,  
Saarinen’s buildings for John Deere in Moline, or Apple’s new ring in Cupertino by Fosters. And, one  
could recall spectacular interior spaces such as Roche-Dinkeloo’s Ford Foundation Building in NY or  
Wright’s Johnsons Wax in Racine.  
In contrast to many of these projects projects of both types, we will not propose an expansion into  
suburban space, but rather build in the city, in Albany. This office building is similar to both of the  
preceding types and also different from both. The offices are rented to external organizations on a short term,  
probably daily, basis but constitute the permanent facility for the owner. As such, the owning  
organization will have a vested interest in projecting an appropriate design quality, building with durable  
materials and details, and keeping long-term energy costs in check.  
This notion of a rentable headquarters for many organizations constitutes a new building type, although it  
has much in common with spec office buildings, convention centers, corporate headquarters, performing  
art centers and research facilities. Precedent studies and continued architectural research will be  
required in order to develop an appropriate response to this client’s brief. A more detailed program will be  
issued later specifying facilities to be provided and activities accommodated, but this facility is to be the  
intermittent headquarters of the renting organization.The spaces will be rented for a day or days at a  
frequency determined by the organization. The facilities available will accommodate varying needs for  
meeting size and type. Some might be considered technically equipped conference rooms, with an an  
adjacent space that might transform in its function from reception, to cafe, to informal conversation, to a  
space for cocktails, to setup space for a catered dinner. These spaces need to be private and  
autonomous and so have their own coat rooms, toilets and service entries. An organization would rent  
and staff the suite and hire catering to suit its needs. These kinds of meetings might have different sizes  
and so rooms of different scales will be required. Interpersonal interaction changes with the scale of the  
group, smaller groups engender conversation, larger ones tend to presenter and audience  
configurations. Theaters for presentations and panel discussions will be required. Rensselaer SoA  
innovations like the Collaborative Augmented Immersive Research Environment (CRAIVE Lab) or the  
Cognitive Immersive Room (CIR) from CISL at EMPAC explore the intersection between embodied and  
virtual attendance and would find ample opportunities in a facility such as this. The anticipated size of the  
complex will be approximately 60,000 SF.  
The project will be sited in Albany, NY in a Mixed-Use Zoning District,1 part of the Downtown Business  
Improvement District (BID) in the Albany 2030 Comprehensive Plan. For the BID there are goals to strengthen  
the Small Business Development Community, particularly well-suited for the deal estate service bureau  
envisioned here. Each project will be required to make a proposal for additional program consistent with  
the goals of the project based on their research that will comprise 20% of the project.

## Learning Outcomes

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes (CLOs):

- Health, Safety, and Welfare in the Built Environment [NAAB SC.1] — students will understand the impact of the built environment on human health, safety, and welfare at multiple scales, from buildings to cities.
- Professional Practice [NAAB SC.2] — students will understand professional ethics, the regulatory requirements, the fundamental business processes relevant to architecture practice in the United States, and the forces influencing change in these subjects. [partial fulfillment]
- Regulatory Context [NAAB SC.3] — students will understand the fundamental principles of life safety, land use, and current laws and regulations that apply to buildings and sites in the United States, and the evaluative process architects use to comply with those laws and regulations as part of a project. [partial fulfillment]
- Technical Knowledge [NAAB SC.4] — students will understand the established and emerging systems, technologies, and assemblies of building construction, and the methods and criteria architects use to assess those technologies against the design, economics, and performance objectives of projects. [partial fulfillment]
- Design Synthesis [NAAB SC.5] — students will develop the ability to make design decisions within architectural projects while demonstrating synthesis of user requirements, regulatory requirements, site conditions, and accessible design, and consideration of the measurable environmental impacts of their design decisions.
- Building Integration [NAAB SC.6] —students will develop the ability to make design decisions within architectural projects while demonstrating integration of building envelope systems and assemblies, structural systems, environmental control systems, life safety systems, and the measurable outcomes of building performance. [partial fulfillment]

## Grading Criteria

### Criteria Details:

Grades will be established according to the following policy:  
**A (EXCELLENT)** The design work is exceptional in every way. It has been undertaken with  
uncommon insight and energy and the ideas have been pursued with criticality and creativity.  
The project shown a strong ability to integrate systems and exceed the functional  
requirements. The results demonstrate the ability to innovate.  
**B (GOOD)** Project is a creative and energetic response to the program, the work is timely,  
complete and well presented. Performance above the norm; accurate and complete; beyond the  
minimum requirements of the course; work demonstrates marked progress and initiative.  
**C (AVERAGE)** Satisfactory work that meets minimum requirements and demonstrates  
satisfactory comprehension, communication skills, and effort; demonstrates little initiative to  
investigate the problem without substantial input from the instructor; work shows little  
improvement. Building systems are treated in a default manner and are not significantly  
transformed with respect to other systems.  
**D (INFERIOR)** Unsatisfactorily meets minimum requirements; demonstrates minimum  
comprehension, communication skills, and effort at an inferior level; initiative lacking;  
improvement not noticeable. The work is not produced and submitted on time, or is  
incomplete. The various building systems show minimal integration.  
**F (FAIL)** Does not meet minimum requirements; fails to adequately demonstrate comprehension,  
communication skills, and effort. major project presentations may be missed or have  
inadequate or missing components or the work does not demonstrate the care and  
thoroughness befitting a professional education. Systems show no integration.

Dishonesty and procedures for responding to them. All forms are violations of the trust between  
students and teachers. Student-teacher relationships are built on trust. For example, students must  
trust that teachers have made appropriate decisions about the structure and content of the courses  
they teach, and teachers must trust that the assignments that students turn in are their own  
performance. Acts that violate this trust undermine the educational process.  
Policy on collaboration and cheating/plagiarism  
Interdisciplinary collaboration is a critical component of this course. All assignments that are  
turned in for a grade must accurately represent the student’s own work, and properly cite authorship  
ofwork that wasnot solelycompleted by the student.Anymis-representation ofworkshallbe evaluated and

Academic Integrity Statement  
(2014-2016 Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities, August 2014)  
Intellectual integrity and credibility are the foundation of all academic work. A violation of  
Academic Integrity policy is, by definition, considered a flagrant offense to the educational  
process. It is taken seriously by students, faculty, and Rensselaer and will be addressed in an  
effective manner.  
If found responsible for committing academic dishonesty, a student may be subject to one or both  
types of penalties: an academic (grade) penalty administered by the professor and/or disciplinary  
action through the Rensselaer judicial process described in the Rensselaer Handbook of Student  
Rights and Responsibilities.  
Academic dishonesty is a violation of the Grounds for Disciplinary Action. A student may be subject  
to any of the following types of disciplinary action should disciplinary action be pursued by the  
professor: disciplinary warning, disciplinary probation, disciplinary suspension, expulsion and/or  
alternative actions as agreed on by the student and hearing officer. It should be noted that no  
student who allegedly commits academic dishonesty will be able to drop or change the grade option  
for the course in question. A record of disciplinary action is permanently maintained by the  
Institute as noted below:  
RECORD OF DISCIPLINARY ACTION (2014-2016 Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights & Responsibilities,  
August 2014, p.15)  
Any disciplinary action can be disclosed to federal, state or local government entity, law  
enforcement, licensing or certification board, or corporate entity upon request of said agency if  
and only if: (a) by subpoena  
or (b) a student signs a confidentiality waiver for said agency or government entity.
