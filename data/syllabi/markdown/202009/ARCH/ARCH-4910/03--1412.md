SECTION TOPIC DESCRIPTION

Our Thesis section engages architectures of apparent contrasting natures: architectures of flow and contagion, architectures of isolation and quarantine. The studio inquires upon forms and typologies that can simultaneously produce containment and connection, as these concepts intersect with new concerns and problematics of BIOSECURITY and their behavior in TIME.

We will design new form that can emerge from the integration of 2 types of architectural diagrams: the diagrams of connectivity and contagion - that foster from single to collective gatherings to very large flows of people, and which have visibility and cultural, as well as social political and economic, ambitions - and diagrams of containment and isolation- which intersect diagrams of spread and contagion typical of bottom-up social and material interactions and cultures, and which they try to prescribe, regulate or organize in some form.

The studio will develop designs for different typological spaces - existing or new ones - that include spatial tactics which integrate new problems of filtering, monitoring, and flow - asking from the discipline a novel way to materially define spaces that engage virtual, often invisible, exchanges of biological, social and cultural natures.

Typologies students will inquire upon and design for, include programs typically associated with:

- travel hubs: airports, stations
- hospitals
- commercial spaces of different scales
- street scapes
- collective work spaces
- collective home spaces
- home-office
- daycare spaces
- educational spaces
- social and family living units

This FP section will be exposed to inquiries and work done in previous FP sections that explored specifically this topic, or explored topics that are inspirational to possible nascent typologies for the current topic, including:

"BIODROME: Terminal Architecture. Death and Life of/in Well-Tempered Airports.” (2014-15) Exploration of airport typologies for an Age of Epidemics.

“CLOUD ARCHITECTURE” (series 2010-2013) Inquiry on how to design very small components that can scale up orders of magnitude. Exploration of Architectures of Assembly, Disassembly and Reassembly.

“PLANETOLOGY: Foundation Landscapes for MASSIVE OBLIVION”. (2015-16) Inquiry on how to design architectural experience for inaccessible Landscapes and Infrastructures and Spaces. Engaging Nuclear Events and Nuclear Materials Storage Spaces, Toxic-in-Remediation Landscapes, and other invisible histories in physical landscapes.

“PERCEPTION: CONTROL ROOM” (2016-17) Inquiry on the design of technologically enabled centralizing collaborative control rooms, drawing from the intersection of increasingly intersecting sensing networks over physical and cultural landscapes and the respective data extracted, mined and operated upon.

“PROTO-ROOM” (2017-18) Exploration of the design and specification of Black Box Smart Spaces for the creation of Augmented Architectural Typologies.

“(IM)PERMANENCE” (2017-18) Inquiring on ways to use timelines and temporal dimensions to engage different scale domains and programs in architectural design.

“INCEPTION” (various years, option studios) Design of Virtual Architecture with characteristics not found in physical space but typical of networked digital environments.

“PET Architecture” (option studio): Hyper-Domesticity. Design of Domestic Environments intersecting with Artificial Intelligence.

The goal is that students learn from previous tropes in projects that engaged directly and indirectly strategies and tactics of creation or application of connection, disconnection, and temporality in architectural designs and structures - availing themselves of a broadening palette and understanding of contemporary materials and processes as part of the architectural body.

**CONNECTIVITY and CONTAINMENT**

The threat of EBOLA epidemics in 2014 and the current 2020 CORONA virus massive spread and evidence the dangers that ‘hubs’ bring in cases of infectious diseases and their often invisible diagrams, in a very globalized world.

The current COVID Pandemic made visible the threats forecasted for years, announced as enabled by a globalized world yet disconnected in its political organization.

The concept of contagion is usually applied to biological systems but one can easily find how this concept could as well be applied to cultural constructs.

The scenario of epidemics control is one more example where architecture becomes soft to lean into the small scale of socio-cultural exchanges.

There is nothing more elusive to architecture form than social relations - and yet, throughout its history, the discipline has been concerned with the creation of form that accompanies human cultures in their exercising of actions, protocols, symbols and overall experience of space as representative or vehicle for social and cultural life.

The invisibilities of social exchange manifest in the way that certain protocols have meaning which is culturally specific and therefore often undecipherable across cultures. The invisible ‘contagion’ of socio-cultural constructs is revealed as vocabularies are shared and therefore [mis]understood across cultures, in the ways behaviors populate an expanding set of spaces.

Often, there are less visible linkages, hubs and flows that allow connection and development of forms to establish and maintain themselves. The real invisibilities of social exchange lie perhaps, so far, beyond human knowledge and comprehension in the realm of material informational exchange that transpire across what seems to be a simple social protocol. There is also much to be learned in micro-biomes and genetic structures as they influence the development of forms for social and cultural protocol across ecological structures.

The invisibility of ‘contagion’ as it pertains illnesses is for us a much more obvious reality.

Structures of isolation for infectious disease use expanded garment design and industrial design techniques to make exchanges visible, controlled, filtered, or interrupted.

This visibility usually concerns protocol, meaning, a known gesture of dressing or undressing of a protective garment is a reminder, as well as an actual barrier, to a vehicle for propagation.

These are ready-made architectures that can be easily deployed within spaces, other

architectures, regions, unregulated landscapes. They are effective because of their capacity to redeploy through training protocols, while keeping the invisibility regarding the infection location or modus operandi.

The development of models that track ways in which a disease spreads show the unique role that fast transportation and international travel have in the emergence of new epidemics.

Historical maps analyzing the spread of the bubonic plague, for example, almost look like temperature maps - their chronological lines softly quasi- topographical, advance through the landscape topologically unchanged.

A number of models for disease spread of bacterial or viral origin, use tactics such as cellphone tracking and simulations with shortest path distance to enter the speed at which diseases propagate in a ultra-connected world. These models show a much more complex state of affairs regarding our situation in the face of an epidemic.

As well, one could easily imagine augmented material and media conditions being developed and becoming equally or more efficient at rendering visible that which is too small or subtle to be seen with the naked human eye - because too small, too slow, or too fast - to manage flows at greater ranges of scales. And ask the hard questions about efficacy of flow control and filtering of populations.

**QUARANTINE: Prisons, Panopticons, Filters**

The Architectures we look into in the studio asks questions that are familiar to the discipline, and its extended multi-disciplinary attitude.

Architectural practice has been known to be exceedingly good at creating barriers between worlds via cultural specifics: insides within outsides, and outsides within insides. However, as the 21st Century started to dissolve firmly established objects and subjectivities, architecture has become deeply aware of the permeability and performance of its material barriers, the complex ecosystems it operates in, and those it creates: interiors and exteriors have become much more blurred conditions to contend with.

The act of quarantining looks archaic, and it is: the isolation of a body from others to watch the potential symptoms of a contagious condition to other bodies. Quarantining stands in substitution of what would otherwise be the act of understanding or just seeing better a condition in a body - it is the way by which the body shows in extreme ways that which lies invisibly within itself.

The straightforward aspect of this problematic is the efficiency of architecture to effectively isolate bodies and materials from each other. The more ambiguous aspect of it is the way in which filtering works, as cultural and scientific practices are prone to error and misguided direction. The condition of ‘alien’ body staged in popular culture and art forms is a great metaphor for the dangers of a overzealous or not-efficient-enough filtering system as it relates to the locality of populations or their sense of belonging to a space or a body.

And in reality, this problematic tends to play out itself not only in apparently straightforward cases of virus epidemics and the need to control fast propagation to large populations, or in the isolation of radioactive materials - but as well in discussions on immigration, political asylums and the spread and mix of cultural beliefs and practices.

It was not too long ago that the fall of the Berlin Wall reminded us of how barriers, of different natures and materials, can create touching, while isolating, completely territories, can effect real impacts with their one-way cultural valves, and help produce distinct bodies with their insidious protocols of passage and maintenance.

The current pandemic speed signature cut across all realms of material, social, cultural, economical and political structures, helping reveal the inadequacies of present structures to cohesively deal with the challenges of a global, planetary 21st century. The emerging production of design theory literature on design and pandemic is remarkable about problems of effective isolation, being that evolving digital social networks, for more than a decade, had specialized on building up the connections between already highly isolated and segregated individuals, aggregates and cultures, keeping them in neat bubbles of appeased complacency.

Quarantining structures, protocols and gestures were experimented with, alternatively in space and time dimensions as knowledge concretized and factions emerged. Previously suspicious apparatus of surveillance and control gained notoriety for their revealed potential to structure ways out of the diverse crisis - health as well as economic - brought by a common, while misdirected, enemy. The discipline of planning, distributing and structuring space out of concepts of subdivision and connection, realized through materials - physical and virtual, was nearly too slow for the pace of developments: as much as the prolific production of PPE and plexiglass enclosures kept and keeps fabrication labs going, it was perhaps LTL’s Manual of Physical Distancing and its acknowledgment of the discipline’s power in projection and representation wizardry, that started to propose all the scales design can engage, to contribute in the current context. Moreover, thinkers such as Benjamin Bratton and Lydia Kallipoliti, look at discourses of post-pandemic urbanism in its broader palette of large and urban scale time-based protocol strategy, emerging material innovation, and digital network spatial and material impacts. Total planning, at the scale of landscapes, might just become cool again.

**Speed & Centuries**

We spent the 20th Century excited and then worried, about the power of speed - and the speed of change - over individual and collective will.

Following the explosion of urban population in the 19th century, architects and planners of the beginning of the 20th century - nascent disciplines in themselves as they are understood now - attempted very large design proposals for humans to be organized in these new machines that were cities. While thinkers like John Ruskin foresaw the long term problems (ex. pollution) humanity would have to deal with from the growth type it was embarking on (“The Storm Clouds of the 19th Century”), architects like Le Corbusier with “Radiant City” and Frank Lloyd Wright “Broadacre City”, showcased an enthusiastic, if not positivistic, view of new forms of totalizing structures for human co-habitation in denser environments. These drawings were structuring diagrams of disposition for movement or behavior - dividing the world of architecture history/theory into 2 sides: those who saw those drawings as organizations without meaning (room to play inside the structure), and those who saw the structure as the meaning (the structure is the play).

The post-war period, and 60’s and 70’s, highly informed by war technologies - radars, travel, information and communication, networks - was a period of reevaluation and of a split in that optimism. As engineering environments were ramping up the digital and computer revolutions - philosophers and cultural thinkers were reframing the power of speed in itself to create structures - or to do away with them.

Paul Virilio’s ‘Dromology’ (“Speed and Politics”) alerts us for the spirals of destruction that can outcome from self-feeding financial systems and the economical, social and political systems/structures that enhance or allow them to develop.

Italo Calvino “Quickness” (“Six Memos for the Next Millennium”), discusses the power of different types of thinking and intelligence and the potential and role of the human mind in the coming accelerated world.

For some of these thinkers, speed of evolution of things and context is claimed to be stronger than humans capacity to organize with each other.

Some of the architectural design iconic references we have of this period are related with this entrancing with change - they are still totalizing drawings boards to a degree - whereas they find new structural layers that integrate time and change into the chore of the organization typology.

Constant’s New Babylon envisioning of an utopian structure for free flow and play is based on a society where change is the key feature to accommodate. Here, a structure merely distributes and signals zones to be defined and redefined by users and their actions in their continuous creative and leisurely play, in a society no longer ruled by work.

Cedric Price’s Fun Palace creates a building size creative city structure, where nearly total possible reconfiguration of spaces attempts to engage the ability of the human mind to invent, collaborate and communicate.

Bernard Tschumi’s La Villette scheme and a continuing thread of theorizing on Event Spaces/City, situates human action and mobilization center stage to create, define and characterize space through program - where architectural design becomes an alternating structuring 'grid’ of either provocation or mapping to reveal hidden aspects of organization, impact and form.

We see traces of this goal and strategy in authors such as Andrea Branzi and his ‘weak urbanism’ theory of spaces with open ends to local engagement and time-based design and transformation, as well as in several other recent mappings, theories and proposals of informal urbanism and architectural design - where emergent form and socio-cultural self-organization are seen as bottom-up structures with higher capacity for human desirable inhabitable environments such as equality, social justice, identity celebration, sustainability to name a few. While these examples have different agendas, interests and even ideologies, their particular engagement with change has provided for some similar tools for mapping, space structuring and activation. We are left to ask what are the scales and mandates of a structure for change, and what kinds of change are possible to accommodate?

**New Structures and Tropes of Permanence and Impermanence**

And we do marvel at the machinic acceleration and the loss of control that speed brings.

Italo Calvino’s “Invisible Cities” seem as much an exercise in the free poetic imaginary on enchanted cities, as they can be understood as an exercise of speeding up: each city is characterized by one particular virtue/aspect which strongly overcame others, defining a rhythm, a structure, a character by which that city can be recognized. The "Invisible Cities" are great models to think about utopian structures and their power to define environments and their correlate mindsets.

As we are well into the 21st Century, cities have become relatively small in the grand scheme of things, as ecological systems and landscape scale thinking expanded the scale by which we understood these nodes as central. Our ever enlarged spatial and time-based observation point is able to uncover new creatures which are effectively measurable for their impact in societies and - similarly to the nuclear scare of the mid-20th century - are able to mobilize discussion and action because of their new found visibility. Mesmerized by their scale and presence, we try to name its new creatures - hoping that vocabulary brings them closer to us having power over them.

Timothy Morton’s “Hyperobject” and “Dark Ecology” claims for a fundamental paradigm shift in understanding concepts of Nature and human’s relationship with it and with Ecology. By being able to name large structures of impactful behavior such as "Climate Change”, Timothy Morton proposes there is a partnership to be struck between the two realms of the so-called ‘natural’ and ‘human actions’ that is structural for the 21st century - that explodes those subdivisions and instead there is an opportunity to reframe questions from the perspective of that collaboration, rather than a conservationist or static view of past equilibriums.

Lastly, the hopeful view by Buckminster Fuller, of humans united by media and light structures and footprint, has been highjacked by freeing technologies that render far more walls and panopticons than matter could ever build. All the structures mentioned can be seen again through the lens of ‘infrastructure’ - as structures that enable other structures, constructions, behaviors.

Similarly to the fears of megastructures, light communication infrastructures - transformed with current economic models of communication technologies become too easily transfigured into exploiting industries similar to the those of the 19th century - surveillance and data mining - extracting and exchanging value from mined movement, cultural exchange, behavior, desires, and dreams.

In several works by Liam Young and the Unknown Fields Division, technologies of mapping and networking become the new walls by which old architecture typologies can find new ways to restrain, open, connect, measure spaces and their inhabitants. “In the Drone Skies” shows us the possibilities of hidden typologies in cities (Prisons) as cities exchange previous construction methods based on 'brick and mortar’ by new equally - or more so - isolating structures made of digital invisible materials.

**GLOBAL VIEWPOINTS, MAPPINGS and STRUCTURES**

As much as we are aware of the power of temporary events with global and near permanent effects, our contemporary viewpoints also create a unique perception of powerlessness. We ask what is to be done in the face of rapid change and models failure: are there structures of higher permanence worth and possible to propose to the world as groundbreaking and foundational, or do we relegate efforts towards needlepoint local and/or temporary impacts that, in a patchwork fashion, build up to catch up with change?

While the natural and built world does not deal with binaries in a pure form, we tend to use them as extremes of gradients that organize living experience, models of living and aspects and typologies of the architectural design discipline. As we have prominently associated architectural design - buildings - with structural models of permanence, the technological acceleration across the last century not only expanded the lexicon of architectural design tools but also put pressure on its core methods, typologies, materials and models to respond to rapidly shifting situations.

As global perception points enable connectedness, there is greater awareness of shared problems, and a stronger presence of their magnitude and scale. Information and communication technologies created far reaching nets of connection between cultures, political and social dimensions and, as well, allowed for a better understanding of common global threats, problems, divergences. We are also more aware of different types of knowledge, skillsets, ideologies and ambitions of different cultures.

The maps we have been creating to represent what we know get deeper and thicker.

Maps of objects and entities have always referred back to their continuities in time. As we develop new disciplines such as genetics, we use a new augmented observation to understand new dimensions for species classifications, and verify new contiguities or continuums between things. This knowledge provides the formulation of new types of structure concepts, and new potentials for what change, transformation and iteration may mean across spectrums of different disciplines - as biology, physics, chemistry, genetics and mathematics grow new branches and evidence new ways by which they are linked.

Perhaps one of the most striking aspects of the 21st century is the capacity of change and transformation to scale up very quickly. It is present in the very word ‘viral’ which relates to main areas of development taking center stage now and probably in the near future: epidemics/pandemics, but also artificial intelligence, social networks, biotech.

In 2020, we have been re-acquainted with the ‘non-digital media’ impact of the word ‘viral’ - that our physical and digital networks have helped produce with magnificent speed and with, this time, duration and uncertainty.

### Additional Course Description :

**Introduction**

The professional degree *Final Project* thesis sequence is designed to enable fifth-year undergraduate students in the Bachelor of Architecture (BArch) program and third-year graduate students in the Master of Architecture (MArch) program to develop a semi-independent design research project over the course of two consecutive semesters. Led by individual section instructors, each thesis section in *Final Project* provides a specific area of design research through which students develop their work. In the fall semester of *Final Project* students concentrate principally on topic and material/formal research, while in the spring semester the focus shifts to design, representation, and production, with the expectation that the fall semester’s research will be utilized as a platform for the production of highly resolved architectural and/or urban proposals in the spring semester.

This particular approach to the development of an architectural thesis was adopted by Rensselaer’s School of Architecture beginning in the fall of 2010 in response to what might be considered a general transformation of the architectural thesis within architectural education over the last two decades.

Since its formation in the nineteenth century, the architectural thesis has represented an opportunity for students to produce fully independent architectural projects, whether “project” was defined as the demonstration of architectural knowledge in the solution to a specific design problem, as was the case with the Ecoles des Beaux-Arts, or a form of political, cultural, and/or disciplinary critique against such traditional forms of problem solving, as was the case with the post-modern thesis of the 1960’s and 70’s. “Design research”, as it has come to be known within contemporary schools of architecture, is perhaps the most recent iteration of this evolving curricular form, and not unlike its antecedents, can be seen as characteristic of the interests and ambitions of its own particular moment in time.

First, *design research* may be viewed as an indication of a general interest within contemporary architectural discourse to integrate aspects of both the profession and the discipline of architecture. If the problem solving implications of “design” are typically associated with the former, “research” as a mode of knowledge production, whether in the form of intellectual critique or creative innovation, is generally characteristic of the latter. Thus, *design research* is conceived as an integration of the two, thereby providing a space for students and faculty alike to engage in open-ended forms of inquiry and experimentation particular to the advancement of disciplinary knowledge, while simultaneously addressing the “real” issues, needs, and challenges facing the profession. As such, the research and design work produced in a *design research* sequence may be seen as both discursive and projective, that is, responsive to the internal discourses of architecture as an autonomous discipline while simultaneously engaging its exterior, i.e. the technological, social, economic, political, and environmental challenges of the built environment.

Second, *design research* positions the architectural studio as a space of collaboration between faculty and students for the exploration and production of new forms of knowledge and design innovation; hence the term “laboratory” often used by contemporary schools of architecture to describe this particular type of studio. As a means of pursuing a set of interests particular to the work characteristic of their research and design practices outside of academia, the design research studio, or laboratory, provides faculty with the opportunity to develop a set of questions and problems related to a particular area of *design research* through which their respective students engage and advance those particular questions and problems. In this particular configuration, then, the architectural studio is not simply a course for disseminating existing forms of knowledge and design expertise as part of a standard architectural education. Rather, this “laboratory” for *design research*, conducted through close collaboration between faculty and students alike, carries with it the larger ambition of producing entirely new forms of knowledge as a general means of advancing the discipline and by extension the profession of architecture.

**Phasing Structure**

The *Final Project* course sequence is unique in that it provides students and faculty the opportunity to engage in advanced design research in a way not possible given the time constraints of a typical semester-long studio. The expectation, then, is that by the end of the spring term, each student will have a highly developed design proposal represented by a substantial amount of presentation material. Thus, in addition to a typical set of drawings (plans, axos, sections, diagrams, etc) each student will be asked to produce large high-res renderings and a series of well-crafted physical models and / or prototypes.

Both the fall semester seminar and the spring semester studio are comprised of three phases of work, the structure of which helps to ensure that each student reaches his or her full potential over the course of the two-semester sequence. While sequential in nature, this phasing structure also produces overlaps at particular points in each semester. The intention here is to encourage productive lines of feedback between the various phases of work, while at the same time providing a clear structure and set of deadlines for each semester.

In the fall semester seminar, the three phases of work include *introductory readings and analysis*, *topic*/*formal research*, and *site/program research*. While the first phase of work serves as a general introduction to the larger discursive themes, questions, and provocations as determined by each of the four FP section leaders, the second phase of work combines research related to a particular discursive theme, i.e. *topic,* with a series of generative studies particular to questions of physical matter or *form*. The intention in this second phase of work is to see analytical and creative work not as separate and discrete endeavors but rather, endemic to one another in the larger context of the creative process. In this respect, thinking and making are viewed as mutually inclusive activities, thereby expanding the process of producing “research” to include acts of creative invention and production in addition to historical and theoretical analysis. This *formal research*, whether it consists of material or digital experiments, or perhaps both, also serves to provide a platform for transitioning into the schematic design phase at the beginning of the spring semester studio. As such, each student is armed with a body of formal research with which to address various issues and challenges related to site and program. Thus, the third and final phase of work in the fall semester, *site/program research*, establishes general conditions of location, scale, and use as a means of expediting this transition.

It is expected that each student complete the fall semester seminar with a clearly articulated and highly developed body of topic/formal research as well as site/program research as this ensures that the first phase of schematic design work in the spring semester studio is devoted exclusively to the development of a design proposal, in turn providing ample time in the second half of the spring semester for the production of large high-res renderings and well-crafted physical models and / or prototypes. However, the deliverables for the fall semester seminar are restricted to two-dimensional representations such as diagrams, drawings, and renders (whether digitally displayed or printed), i.e. three-dimensional physical models such as scale models should be excluded from the fall semester deliverables, as they are too time-intensive for a 3-credit seminar course.

And while each section leader maintains full autonomy in the framing of their respective seminar/studio section, including the selection of a particular *topic / formal research* theme, specific methodologies of making as it relates to that particular phase of work, as well as the determination of site if he or she chooses not to leave that to their students, they will be asked to follow the phasing structure in both the fall and spring semesters. This helps to ensure that each FP section is at a similar level of development over the course of the year and as a result can be expected to produce a certain quality as well as quantity of work by the end of the spring semester.

Additionally, section leaders will be asked to ensure that students in their respective seminar sections are organized in teams of two or three students for the *introductory readings and analysis* phase, the *topic*/*formal research* phase, and the *site/program research* phase. Team-oriented work for the first three phases will help to maximize the amount of topic/formal analysis, and the site and program research that students will be able to produce in the context of a 3-credit seminar course, as well as allow section leaders more time to provide adequate feedback to five teams instead of 15 individual students at this stage. Section leaders should plan to have students transition from team-oriented to individual work after the 3/4 review, and ask students to use the two weeks between the 3/4 review and Thanksgiving to begin developing individual design concepts/proposals from the collaborative material. By the end of the fall semester seminar, each students should have their own distinct design concept/proposal to use as a point of departure for the fall semester design studio.

## Course Goals

### Goals:

Over the course of the fall and spring semesters, students will be introduced to a particular set of architectural issues, problems, and themes as determined by their designated FP section leader and will be asked to explore those issues, problems, and themes through a series of research and design assignments. These assignments will challenge students to work in a number of different ways, engaging them in information gathering, textual and visual analysis, drawing and modeling, as well as design. The intention here is to produce a diversified body of work that incorporates aspects of research and design in a way that is thoughtful and critical as well as speculative and creative.

The fall semester’s work will culminate in the production of a concise (300-500 words) thesis statement for submission to each student’s respective FP section leader in PDF format. The spring semester’s work will culminate in the production of a bound book for submission to the library at the School of Architecture, which in addition to the fall semester’s topic/formal research, will also include the documentation of a fully developed design proposal.

Although each FP section’s research and design work may, at the discretion of the section leader, be formatted differently for the purposes of reviews during the semester, every student's FP book must be formatted according to the following speciﬁcations:

*Page size*: 8.5” square per template provided.

*Book length*: main body must be min. 34 and max. 40 pages (page count does not include cover, abstract, bibliography, and image credits).

*Structure*: the book must comprise the following sections:

- **Abstract** (not to exceed 250 words) – Each FP book will begin with an Abstract, i.e. a one-paragraph summary of the thesis. The purpose of the abstract is to help the reader quickly ascertain the thesis’ topic of inquiry (what is it about?) and design agenda (what is the project?).
- **Thesis Statement** (not to exceed 500-600 words) – Each FP book will contain a Thesis Statement, i.e. a concise statement that identifies the general topic of architectural inquiry for the design proposal as a set of questions, problems, and themes related to that topic as well as the student’s particular interests and opinions in relation to that topic. The Thesis Statement should also outline the general ambitions of the design proposal and demonstrate the ways in which the questions, problems, and themes identified in the general topic of architectural inquiry have been addressed by the student’s design work, ultimately contributing to a larger discussion of that topic.
- **History/Precedent** (intro paragraph on first page of section not to exceed 250 words. Additional text may be integrated on the following pages. Images to be placed per template’s instructions) – this section should adequately document the analytical research conducted during the first portion of the fall semester and may include collaborative as well as individual work. The section should provide both quantitative and qualitative information pertaining to that research, i.e. concepts, forms of historical and/or theoretical knowledge, and specific information related to architectural precedent studies. Furthermore, the research documentation should consist of both textual and visual material and include drawings, diagrams, photographs, and other forms of visual representation.
- **Design Research and Working Methodology** (intro paragraph on first page of section not to exceed 250 words. Additional text may be integrated on the following pages. Images to be placed per template’s instructions) – this section should adequately document how the student interpreted the analytical research in the context of developing his or her own thesis’ design agenda in the second half of the fall semester, and may include collaborative as well as individual work. The section should provide both quantitative and qualitative information pertaining to the development of a design technique or methodology, i.e. design research in the form of abstract experiments produced through drawings, renderings, and diagrams. Furthermore, the research documentation should consist of both textual and visual material and include drawings, diagrams, photographs, and other forms of visual representation.
- **Project Development** (intro paragraph on first page of section not to exceed 250 words. Additional text may be integrated on the following pages. Images to be placed per template’s instructions) – this section should adequately document the spring semester’s design work as conducted in the studio portion of the yearlong course sequence and provide both quantitative and qualitative information pertaining to that work, i.e. aesthetic and conceptual qualities, historical and theoretical influences, formal and spatial characteristics, programmatic and environmental performance, technologies and materials, etc. Furthermore, the documentation of design work should consist of both textual and visual material and include drawings, diagrams, photographs, models, and other forms of visual representation.
- **Conclusion** (text not to exceed 300 words. Images to be placed per template’s instructions) – each FP book will end with a short statement explaining the student’s own assessment of the thesis’ pedagogical value (what did you learn?) as well as its relevance in the broader context of architectural discourse (what is the thesis’ contribution to the field?).
- **Bibliography** – all written sources must be properly cited in the bibliography. Students must use the formatting standards per the InDesign template provided. For further guidance, students should refer to the Chicago Manual of Style. Failure to properly reference bibliographic sources constitutes a serious breach of the Institute’s Academic Integrity Policy.
- **Image Citations** – all visual sources must be properly cited in the image citations section. Use formatting templates provided in the InDesign document. For further guidance, students should refer to the Chicago Manual of Style. Failure to properly reference sources constitutes a serious breach of the Institute’s Academic Integrity Policy.

*Text Formatting*: each student’s book must adhere to the formatting standards per the InDesign template provided for body text, footnotes, bibliography, and image credits. For further guidance, refer to the Chicago Manual of Style.

*Images*: all images must be high quality (i.e. not pixelated or blurred). A 300 dpi resolution is highly recommended for all images.

*Additional instructions*: more information about layout and formatting can be found under the “instructions” layer in the InDesign template provided. The “instructions” layer must be turned off before exporting the book to PDF.

*Printing*: printing and binding are handled through Mixam. In order to use Mixam, students should first create an account, then select “paperback books” under “all products.” Then students should select the 8.5” square size, 40 sides (unless pages are added to the InDesign template, in which case the number of pages will have to be updated accordingly), color printing (throughout), 70 lb. paper weight (satin finish), and 100 lb. cover paper (satin finish).

After uploading a PDF of the book to Mixam, each student should ask a Mixam representative to create a spine for their book using part of the back cover so the horizontal colored band runs across the covers and spine.

## Course Content

### Content Details:

The organizational structure of Final Project differs substantially from both the seminar and design courses of the preceding four years of undergraduate studies and two years of graduate studies. In particular, Final Project is unique in the following three ways:

*Directed Research*

As a point of departure in the fall semester, each FP section leader will introduce a specific *design research* topic that serves to define the focus of their respective FP section for both the fall and spring semesters. Additionally, each FP section leader will have the option of providing specific guidelines for a particular design methodology to be utilized in their fall semester seminar, which may include the use of specified modeling software and/or production techniques. The intention here is to establish a model of *directed research*, i.e., while students may have more creative autonomy than in a conventional design studio, they are nonetheless working within a specific pedagogical and/or methodological framework as determined by their FP section leader and thus “directed” by their FP section leader as they engage in their independent research and design work.

*Final Project Assessment Committee*

In addition to their respective FP section leader, students will have access to an additional faculty member for critical and creative feedback over the course of the year. Each FP section will be appointed one assessment committee member who will be present on all reviews. This committee member will also participate in the evaluation of FP books at the end of each semester. As such, the FP section leader and the FP assessment committee member share responsibility for the academic assessment of students.

Additionally, the four studio FP section leaders will participate in each other’s reviews over the course of the year, providing informal feedback to one another. The intention here is to structure Final Project in such a way as to foster an environment of open feedback and exchange between the four section leaders and their designated assessment committee members, as well as the students in each FP section. In this way, the structure of Final Project endeavors to produce a climate of communal discourse unique to an undergraduate architecture education.

Further, the fall semester course (ARCH 4910) will be comprised of four separate 3cr seminars, each conducted by a different Final Project section leader.

## Learning Outcomes

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes (CLOs):

- The student will be able to demonstrate the ability to independently propose, refine, and develop a comprehensive design project.
- The student will be able to demonstrate knowledge and mastery of the theoretical and applied research methodologies and practices used during the design process.
- The student will be able to demonstrate the capacity to engage in both research and design work in such a way that each are beneficial to the other.
- The student will be able to demonstrate knowledge and mastery of the various issues, problems, and themes that inform the design project.
- The student will be able to demonstrate analytic ability through writing, diagramming, and drawing.
- The student will be able to demonstrate creative ability in the design and development of the project as well as a capacity to document and present their work in a clear and effective way.
- The student will be able to demonstrate an ability to communicate arguments in lucid written prose.
- The student will be able to demonstrate a working knowledge of the use of citations and attributions in scholarly writing and presentations.
- The student will be able to demonstrate effective research skills, including the ability to explore sources of knowledge external to the discipline of architecture.

## Grading Criteria

### Criteria Details:

For ARCH 4910 ARCH 4920, and ARCH 6750 each respective FP section leader will assign letter grades and modifiers with input from their respective FP assessment committee member.

For ARCH 6980, each respective FP section leader will assign a grade of U (unsatisfactory), S (satisfactory), or I (incomplete) with input from their respective FP assessment committee member.

FP section leaders along with their assigned assessment committee members will be expected to provide feedback at each review. Verbal assessment and input from the external critics will also be provided at reviews.

The fall semester 3cr seminar section of ARCH 4910 and ARCH 6750 will account for 100% of the final grade for the fall semester and the evaluation criteria is as follows:

Seminar assignments: 20%

Reviews: 40%

Thesis Statement: 25%

Participation: 15%

Total: 100%

The spring semester BArch studio (ARCH 4920) will account for 100% of the final grade for the spring semester and the evaluation criteria is as follows:

Studio assignments: 20%

Final review: 40%

FP book: 25%

Participation: 15%

Total: 100%
