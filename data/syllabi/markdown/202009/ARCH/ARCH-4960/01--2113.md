## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

Sailing back from one of his voyages Christopher Columbus (Cristobal Colon, in Spanish) wrote in his diary that he was returning from Paradise, such was the impact the newfound lands have made on him. From its “discovery” until today, such a dreamlike image, more desire than reality, has been a constant driven force through the history of Latin America. *El Dorado* myth, an ideal golden city somewhere deep inland, took many shapes (and names: *Trapalanda*, *La Ciudad Dorada*, the City of the Caesars) in the mind of the early Spanish conquistadores that madly searched for it –as so well it is portrayed in Herzog’s “Aguirre, the Wrath of God”--. The persistence of this utopian, and in many ways violent vision could be traced today in many contemporary practices, as in the cultural appropriationism proposed by the avant-garde Antropofagia movement -- later continued by the *Tropicalistas*--.This idea continue to be foundational on the 1960’s *Hombre Nuevo* (The New Man) carry out by the leftist revolutionaries, and even in the bold nationalism that drove the building of Brasilia.

Colon’s mention is not casual. It is a starting point for a definition to this part of The Americas, the foundational act that will link it to the European cultural traditions that name it in the first place. A Greco / Roman / Catholic / Mediterrean (first Spanish/Portuguese and later Italian) linage that will conflict and integrate with the (still very much alive) indigenous cultures, the forced African “migration”, and the successive waves of newcomers. A mestizo identity often defined in opposition to the Anglo-Saxon / Protestant / English-speaking part above the Rio Grande, a narrow oppositional definition used at both sides of the border that migration fluxes have placed under question.

History has shaped Latin America not as a single cultural territory but one where many temporalities are simultaneously operating with many different threats connecting the symbolic practices. Any attempt to frame this constant shifting field under one fixed perspective would result partial. It demands an open conceptual approach, an analytical grid as flexible (and arbitrary) as its reality is fundamental, in order to grasp the issues at play.

What are the conditions and practices emerging in this scenario? This course aims to identify and research recent architectural and urban practices in Latin America. We will study the production under an analytical framework driven by the notions such as hybridity, coloniality, and *mestizaje*.

In this edition, the seminar will focus their individual research in a selected group of authors from the Latin American canon: Lina Bo Bardi, Rogelio Salmona, Oscar Niemeyer, Vilanova Artigas, Amancio Williams, Eladio Dieste, and/or Luis Barragan. These architects will be analyzed and discussed in relation to work from other symbolic practices (mainly visual and literary arts), as well to its social, political and environmental context. Particular emphasis will be placed to locate these works and practitioners within the Latin American architectural tradition/practice and global contemporary architectural discourses.

**Course Requirements:** One weekly discussion seminar where students discuss issues emerging in presentations and readings, and present updates of their work. Students must provide weekly brief reports / questions of the assigned readings and may asked to lead class discussions at least twice per semester. As core assignment students will work in the development of a one-issue academic journal on Latin American Architecture, and each student will be required to contribute a 6-pages illustrated essay, where they reflect and develop their own critical views on a canonical author or topic of their choice (upon faculty approval).
