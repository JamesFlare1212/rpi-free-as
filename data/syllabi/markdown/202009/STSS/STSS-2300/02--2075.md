## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

The field of environmental sociology takes a holistic approach to environment and society, recognizing the environment as part of the process of social reproduction, and social phenomena as part of the maintenance or degradation of environmental health. This class offers an advanced introduction to key concepts and debates within this field, and examines how these ideas might inform solutions to the pressing environmental issues of today. Students will use concepts from environmental sociology to pursue a semester-long research project that examines environmental issues from a sociological perspective.

## Learning Outcomes

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes (CLOs):

- Describe environmental issues from a sociological perspective.
- Define key concepts and debates in environmental sociology.
- Use concepts from environmental sociology to explore contemporary ecological issues.
- Communicate a sociological perspective on environmental issues to a broader audience.

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes Assessment Measures:

- Supportive participation (10%): One of your primary roles in this class is helping to make this a space where people can work together and learn. This can involve participating in classroom conversations, reporting on group work, posting questions and comments in shared spaces, uplifting your classmates’ ideas, making space for people to speak, or working to create a sense of community in the classroom.
- *In-class assignments (20%): Throughout the semester, there will be a variety of short in-class assignments that will ask you to write, do arts and crafts, find articles, etc. Grades will be assigned based on thoughtfulness and creativity.* *Sustainability events (10%): You will virtually or physically attend at least two sustainability-related events hosted by RPI or other institutions, and submit 100-word reflections. These will be due October 9 and December 10.* Midterm (10%): There will be an in-class midterm that will ask you to write about ecological issues using concepts and theories covered in class.
- Research Zines (50%): In groups of 2-4, you will design a research project that examines “environment” and “society” as mutually co-constitutive, inextricable fields of play and power. This project should pick an environmental issue that interests you, and investigate it using original research. The grade for the term project will be based on the following assignments:
  - Project proposal (5%): In class, you will develop a preliminary idea for your research project. (October 5).
  - Research roundtable (5%): In class, you will share research updates with classmates, who will offer their reflections and alternative interpretations. (October 22)
  - Knowledge gaps (5%): In class, you will identify things you do not yet know about your field of interest, and investigate the reasons for this non-knowledge. (November 12)
  - 2 Research memos (10% each): Each group member will engage in organized research activities that will help you to learn more about your topic. This can be an interview, participant observation, a survey, discourse analysis, or a deep dive into the literature. Your memo should describe the data you collected, how you collected it, and what you learned from it. Each group member will submit two such memos. (October 22 and November 12)
  - Zine (10%): Your group will produce a zine that takes your important findings from the project and communicates them with a broader audience. You will be able to edit and resubmit your zine in response to comments. ()
  - Zine responses (5%): Each individual will provide brief written comments in response to other zines. ()
- Extra credit (up to 5%): You may arrange to give a 5-10 minute presentation on a specific environmental issue that you have encountered in your life. The topic of this presentation must be agreed on beforehand with your instructor.

## Policies

### Attendance Policy:

Attendance: If you are unable to attend a class session, you may make up for it without penalty by completing the steps outlined under “asynchronous participation” (listening to class recordings and posting on the Discord before the following class period). If you do not make up for a missed class session, this will count as an absence. Absences count as excused if you have an official note, or if we talk about it and it seems like a good reason. You may accrue two excused absences without having to make up for missed participation; beyond that we’ll need to talk about how to make up for missed content. You may also accrue two unexcused absences without having to make up for missed participation. Beyond that, each unexcused absence will result in a 3% reduction in your overall grade. Please talk to me if anything is making it difficult to participate!

With both excused and unexcused absences, you will still be responsible for making up in-class group activities.

Asynchronous participation: Synchronous participation is strongly recommended; it helps to create space for connection, communication, and mutual understanding. In general, the more perspectives in a classroom, the more interesting the discussion will be. However, I recognize that many students are attending from a different time zone, in which case synchronous participation will be difficult.

Asynchronous participation is possible if necessary. Participating in a class session asynchronously includes: (1) listening to recorded lectures and discussions, and (2) posting questions and comments to the Discord--either before class, in response to readings; or after class, in response to the lecture and discussion. If you do both of these things before the following class session, you will not be counted as absent. Any missed in-class activities will also be due before the following class session.

If you are going to miss a few classes, participating asynchronously can allow you to make up for missed material. Beyond three missed classes, please let me know what is going on. If you will never (or rarely) be able to attend class sessions—due to time zones or other reasons—that is totally fine, but please let me know at the start of the semester. You will need to find a group of students with whom you work on group activities outside class time.

### Academic Policies:

- ### Academic Integrity

  Student-teacher relationships are built on trust. For example, students must trust that teachers have made appropriate decisions about the structure and content of the courses they teach, and teachers must trust that the assignments that students turn in are their own. Acts that violate this trust undermine the educational process. The Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities and The Graduate Student Supplement define various forms of Academic Dishonesty and you should make yourself familiar with these. In this class, all assignments that are turned in for a grade must represent the student’s own work. In cases where help was received, or teamwork was allowed, a notation on the assignment should indicate your collaboration.

  Violations of academic integrity may also be reported to the appropriate Dean (Dean of Students for undergraduate students or the Dean of Graduate Education for graduate students, respectively).

  If you have any question concerning this policy before submitting an assignment, please ask for clarification. In addition, you can visit the following site for more information on our Academic Integrity Policy: [Students Rights, Responsibilities, and Judicial Affairs](https://info.rpi.edu/dean-students/student-rights-responsibilities-and-judicial-affairs).
