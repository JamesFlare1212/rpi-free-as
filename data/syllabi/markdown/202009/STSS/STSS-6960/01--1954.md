## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

Designed within a cultural conjuncture, this course provides conceptual frameworks and methodologies useful for comprehending current and chronic crises and entering conversations with and about the past 50 years of feminist and antiracist work in STS. What stories do we tell about Feminist and Antiracist STS? What have been the multiple genealogies and trajectories that have contributed to the emergence and enactment of this form of situated knowledge?

Feminist and Antiracist STS is a critical interdisciplinary formation cross-fertilized with cultural studies, studies of colonial discourse and postcolonial studies, Critical Race Theory and Critical Race Feminism, queer theory and sexuality studies, and various disciplinary and adisciplinary formations that playfully and critically inform each of the above. We will explore these pluricentric positions to the greatest extent possible given constraints of time and space. This course is both visceral and virtual. It will be held in EMPAC Studio Beta, and will incorporate artist talks and workshops as an ART_X course. Workshops are encouraged but not required.

## Course Goals

### Goals:

Graduate courses are designed to enable you to broaden and deepen committed engagement with STS as a field, including as-yet unnamed STSs enacted through your own research and conceptual contributions. This course steers clear of “core” concepts, “foundational” theories, “canonical” texts. To ensure you acquire a comprehensive grasp of STS, which works in synergistic relationship with other knowledge formations, we will clarify genealogies, configurations, discourses, situations, and trajectories of feminist and antiracist STS without prescribing yours. The practical goal is intense immersion. Engagement will be assessed through six (6) arcs of assessment (below).

***Week 3* Traveling Theories: Situating Postcolonial Projects**

**Friday, September 18**

Contrapuntal thinking of the sort that Edward Said was doing in *Orientalism* (1978) involves figures whose writings travel across time, space, and ideology, who are “un-mired” and “de-contextualized,” only to be “re-territorialized” in the ways that Fanon used phenomenology; Butler used Luce Irigaray, Foucault, and Monique Wittig; Freud, Marx, and Nietzsche get used by Deleuze, Foucault, and Irigaray, or how Edward Said used Lukacs and Foucault. These wrenching reinscriptions themselves leave texts to be read, traces to be followed, trajectories to be woven in, emplaced, repressed, situated, or overwritten. Our project is to discern where these dynamics are actually happening, and make something of them. Before class, please listen to this April 15, 2020 30-minute think piece by Ruha Benjamin at **https://aas.princeton.edu/news/black-skin-white-masks-racism-vulnerability-refuting-black-pathology?fbclid=IwAR30XJjucvUL4Gi5nKsymGSQ_Y_Oh4GNLeYj9ev8xTtPMCaoxDaAx7eEKOU** **and/or** and Shannon Bell at https://vimeo.com/303354181 and Sut Jhally’s interview with Edward Said on *Orientalism*: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fVC8EYd_Z_g (40:31)

Please read your selections from this special issue of a journal that found itself enacting studies of “colonial discourse,” “minority discourse,” and “postcolonial theory,” based upon the 1982 essay by Said titled “Travelling Theory” (1982). Said indicated that for theory to be alive, it must be grounded in specific historical context, to travel (for which he uses the example of “reification,” which emerged from Georg Lukacs’ Budapest, Hungary, in the 1920s). Patterned movement of theory requires: 1. A point or points of origin, political configuration or social formation, place and time; 2. A distance travelled as ideas move to other times and places along different trajectories and through various contexts; 3. A set of *material conditions* of acceptance or resistance that confront the transplanted theory; 4. A transformation of theory to new uses, new positions.

**Optional Reading:**

***Week 4* Interrogating and Situating Feminist Science Studies continued**

Feminist transformation of science studies came early enough that it may be thought of as foundational, despite ongoing marginalization implied by the label. Feminist scientists, historians, and philosophers of science, the latter schooled in logical positivism, began to critique science studies even as they began to read and produce it. They aimed to change feminist movements as well as labor, pro-democracy, civil rights, Black Power, Third World, women’s health, reproductive rights, and other movements on the late 1960s and early 1970s. Theirs was a critical endeavor emphasizing that science has been a largely masculine domain dominated by modes of expertise, value neutrality, and objectivity that foreclosed tacit knowledge, relational proximity between researchers and researched, and situationally specific elements. They became targets during the “science wars.” By the end of this week, Part I of the course, we will have ‘traveled” through multiple origin stories and into a domain we might loosely characterize as Feminist and Antiracist STS.

## Learning Outcomes

**Arc 1: Structured Provocation (BEFORE CLASS)**

Provocations “trouble” the texts we read for the day by introducing critical projects that are orthogonal or oppositional, occluded or excluded, in relation to the days’ readings. This is your chance to probe, extend, or nuance the texts under collective examination. Provocations are typically short, pointed, written up formally in 3-5 pages and sent to the professor the night before class is to meet. You are responsible to introduce the provocation whenever conversation turns to a place where provocation would advance it. Provocations draw on **additional** material, texts, and contexts, relating the texts for the day with *different* sources, intentions, or characteristics from those under “official” discussion. Provocations may include video, performance, installation art, or writings that provide substantive examples of STS work in art worlds, digital media, or data science.

**Arc 2: Discussion Facilitation (DURING CLASS)**

On the date for which you sign up, you are responsible for introducing materials that will help frame and facilitate discussion for 1 hour. Draw upon the main points of our readings for that day and choose one of the following options: 1. Add context; 2. “Apply” concepts to a specific analytic situation or text of your own choosing; 3. Draw upon additional material to provide genealogy. Above all, keep the conversation moving, and ensure fairness in terms of who gets to speak, how long they do so, etc.

**Arc 3: Rumination (BEFORE AND DURING CLASS)**

Ruminations are critical, written reflections on the readings (required and/or optional) presented in the class where those readings are discussed. You must turn in the 5-7-page paper on which your 15-20-minute presentation should be based. All written work for this class should be polished—i.e. I expect a finished piece of academic writing, on the basis of which you present your reflections to class. You may use presentation software, but if so, that too should be turned in.

**Arc 4: Narration: What Stories Need Telling for the Earthly Survival of STS? (BEFORE AND AFTER CLASS)**

Collaborative writing of a Google Doc, to which you should contribute 45-60 minutes each week will be our way of “storytelling for earthly survival.” We will seek to collectively narrate stories about feminist and antiracist thought in ways that will be useful for STS as a continually emerging field. These will take the form of a collectively authored document, a “future STS Handbook chapter” on intersecting concepts of antiracist and queer feminist practice in STS. We will reimagine what STS might look like if we took co-production seriously; if we thought of STS as what we imagine it to be; and if we enacted that as a process of becoming this semester via an inspirational and aspirational project.

**Arc 5: Synthesis (DURING CLASS)**

Capacity to improvise and synthesize-- to “think on your feet” is essential to success in graduate school and elsewhere. This arc requires that you connect across the components of each of your classes, and provide an oral synthesis of the previous seminar in the first 10 minutes of the next class. You will be opening the next class and synthesizing the previous class by drawing together the streams of our discussion, reminding us where we have been, and raising questions that help build continuities in times of discontinuities.

**Arc 6: Feminist and Antiracist STS: What It Means to Me (AFTER CLASS)**

Your final written assignment for this course is a position paper summarizing your current understanding of the breadth of science studies that comprise the field of STS, which by now you realize is comprised of interlocking “science studies.” Choose a theme around which to arrange your characterizations; add texts and contexts to those discussed in the course (you may also include your provocation); but reflect as fully as possible upon the body of knowledge presented in this course. This reflection should be approximately 20 pages, typed, double-spaced, polished, and deeply documented. It should be written with your portfolio in mind.

***Week 3* Traveling Theories: Situating Postcolonial Projects**

***Week 4* Interrogating and Situating Feminist Science Studies**

**Friday, September 25 >** Highly likely that Professor Campbell will be “traveling” in ways that will make it hard for her to participate in class today. Please discuss the below texts without her so as not to lose momentum. If her schedule permits, she will join you for part of the discussion.

Feminist transformation of science studies came early enough that it may be thought of as foundational, despite ongoing marginalization implied by the label. Feminist scientists, historians, and philosophers of science, the latter schooled in logical positivism, began to critique science studies even as they began to read and produce it. They aimed to change feminist movements as well as labor, pro-democracy, civil rights, Black Power, Third World, women’s health, reproductive rights, and other movements on the late 1960s and early 1970s. Theirs was a critical endeavor emphasizing that science has been a largely masculine domain dominated by modes of expertise, value neutrality, and objectivity that foreclosed tacit knowledge, relational proximity between researchers and researched, and situationally specific elements. They became targets during the “science wars.” By the end of this week, Part I of the course, we will have ‘traveled” through multiple origin stories and into a domain we might loosely characterize as Feminist and Antiracist STS.

***Week 8*** **Interrogating Neuroscience: Feminism Takes on Neuroscience**

**Friday, October 23**

Arguably we could say that feminist constructs of “the body” and corporeality were advanced to get around or beyond older lexicons of critique such as biological determinism, essentialism, and the sciences of reductionism. Among newer lexicons of critique are new materialisms (both feminist and not); queer politics of kinship; and dynamic biologies of animacy, becoming, and plasticity. This week, we will wade into these debates and the onto-epistemological challenges they pose for settled ways of knowing, thinking, imaging, and imagining brains and their bodies. We must also ask after the ‘whiteness” of neuroscience, and the racial projects embedded therein.
