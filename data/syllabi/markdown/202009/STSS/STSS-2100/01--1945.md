## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

In this course, students receive practical hands-on experience in conducting ethical and theoretically-informed research in the fields of STS and sustainability studies. Throughout the semester, students practice a variety of social science research methods through a series of exercises.

## Course Text(s)

### Text Details:

Dixon, J.C., R.A. Singleton, Jr., and B.C. Straits. 2019. *The Process of Social Research, 2nd edition*. New York: Oxford University Press.

Additional readings and assignments will be provided on the course Blackboard site. You should check this site regularly for announcements and new course materials.

You will sometimes be expected to find social science research articles on your own. You can do that by using the JSTOR journal database, which is available through the Folsom Library website.

## Course Goals

### Goals:

Welcome to Investigating Society! This course is designed to provide you with a basic understanding of social science research, which you can apply in your chosen field of study. We will focus on issues such as the logic of research design, conceptualization and measurement, data collection methods available to social scientists, and what social scientists do with data once they have collected it. We will look closely at questions of ethics, politics, and social justice in the research process. I have designed the course to be interactive, with the idea that students learn best by doing. For that reason, the course is structured around four major group projects, in which you will practice research skills such as designing a survey, observing and interpreting everyday social life, and conducting semi-structured interviews.

## Learning Outcomes

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes (CLOs):

- Identify, choose, and use appropriate methods for the collection and analysis of social data.
- Discuss issues of data quality, curation, validation, and uncertainty.
- Demonstrate the link between theory, research, and analysis, identifying appropriate problems to which data can be applied, and discussing limitations, biases, assumptions and interpretations.
- Discuss the ethical issues surrounding data in this field, including, but not limited to, responsible conduct of research, privacy, provenance, privatization, monetization, and social implications.
- Communicate effectively in oral presentations, group discussions, and academic writing.

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes Assessment Measures:

Team Projects - 40% (4 projects, 10% per project) (100 points each)

Students in this course will work in small groups (3-4 students) on four projects. Because we must practice social distancing, teams will meet online and will have weekly scheduled consultations with the professor and/or teaching assistant. Each project will take 2-3 weeks to complete and will conclude with a presentation to the class (see below). The four project topics are listed here, with further details on each assignment to be provided on Blackboard.

- \- Make ethnographic observations
- \- Design a survey
- \- Conduct and analyze semi-structured interviews
- \- Analyze and visualize existing quantitative data

Project Presentation - 5% (50 points)

Each student gives a presentation at some point during the semester. The presentation will represent group work, but the individual student giving the presentation will be evaluated on their oral presentation abilities, including preparing a video for asynchronous viewing (10 minutes in length). The instructor and/or teaching assistant will provide feedback on the first draft of the Project Presentation. The feedback will benefit all members of the student team completing the project, in addition to helping the individual presenter to improve their oral presentation technique.

Chapter Quizzes - 20% (15 points x 13 weeks = 195 points)

Students will take open-book quizzes each week, on the assigned readings. The quizzes will be available on Blackboard and must be completed before 12 noon on Thursday.

Weekly Discussion - 10% (10 points x 10 weeks = 100 points)

Each week, a prompt will be posted in the discussion forum on Blackboard. Prompts will include short readings or videos, followed by questions for your consideration. Students are expected to post a detailed response to the prompt (at least 100 words) and to respond substantively to at least one other student’s comment. Weekly discussions must be completed by the end of the day on Friday. To get full credit, you must participate in at least 10 weekly discussions.

Weekly Question - 5% (5 points x 11 weeks = 55 points)

Students are expected to ask questions about the readings, lecture content, assignments, and ideas that are raised in discussions. Asking questions is how we all learn. Normally this would happen in a classroom, but because many of us are learning remotely this semester, the best way to ask questions is to post them in a discussion forum. Each week a new discussion forum will be created as a place to ask (and answer) questions. Many of your questions will be answered in a subsequent lecture, as well. Questions must be posted every week by the end of the day on Friday. To get full credit, you must post a question on at least 11 weeks of the semester.

Critical Review Essay - 20% (200 points)

This is a written report (2,500 words) about one published research paper, completed as a culminating assignment to demonstrate critical understanding of social research methods. This paper counts as your final exam, and will be due during final exam week. More details about this assignment will be posted on Blackboard.

### Academic Policies:

- ### Academic Integrity

  Student-teacher relationships are built on trust. For example, students must trust that teachers have made appropriate decisions about the structure and content of the courses they teach, and teachers must trust that the assignments that students turn in are their own. Acts that violate this trust undermine the educational process. The Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities and The Graduate Student Supplement define various forms of Academic Dishonesty and you should make yourself familiar with these. In this class, all assignments that are turned in for a grade must represent the student’s own work. In cases where help was received, or teamwork was allowed, a notation on the assignment should indicate your collaboration.

  Violations of academic integrity may also be reported to the appropriate Dean (Dean of Students for undergraduate students or the Dean of Graduate Education for graduate students, respectively).

  If you have any question concerning this policy before submitting an assignment, please ask for clarification. In addition, you can visit the following site for more information on our Academic Integrity Policy: [Students Rights, Responsibilities, and Judicial Affairs](https://info.rpi.edu/dean-students/student-rights-responsibilities-and-judicial-affairs).

### Additional Academic Integrity Course Policy and Penalty Information:

The Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities define various forms of Academic Dishonesty and procedures for responding to them. All forms are violations of the trust between students and teachers. Student-teacher relationships are built on trust. For example, students must trust that teachers have made appropriate decisions about the structure and content of the courses they teach, and teachers must trust that the assignments that students turn in are their own performance. Acts that violate this trust undermine the educational process.

Intellectual integrity and credibility are the foundation of all academic work. A violation of Academic Integrity policy is, by definition, considered a flagrant offense to the educational process. It is taken seriously by students, faculty, and Rensselaer and will be addressed in an effective manner.

Academic dishonesty is a violation of the Grounds for Disciplinary Action as described in the Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities. A student may be subject to any of the following types of disciplinary action should disciplinary action be pursued by the professor: disciplinary warning, disciplinary probation, disciplinary suspension, expulsion and/or alternative actions as agreed on by the student and hearing officer. Violations of academic integrity include, but are not limited to: academic fraud, collaboration, copying, cribbing, fabrication, plagiarism, sabotage, and substitution. Please see the Rensselaer Student Handbook for further explanation of the university’s policy on academic integrity.
