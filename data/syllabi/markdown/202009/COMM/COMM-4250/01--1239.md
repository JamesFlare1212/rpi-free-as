Learning Objectives

- Learn to handle key interactive narrative software.
- Become aware of a variety of interactive narratives and learn to critique examples of interactive narrative.
- Learn to author, edit, and present conceptual documents and production phases associated with interactive narrative development:
- o High Concept
- o Characters
- o Design Outline
- o Synopsis
- o Version #1
- o Version #2
- o Final Version
- o Supporting Material for publication

- Learn to engage in group discussions including feedback and iteration.
- Learn to research specific development studios in relation to prospective employment.

- Identify structural components and stylistic best practices in related media (in this case, prose/short fiction) and how they apply to interactive narrative.

- Practice game story development techniques.
- Practice game dialogue writing techniques.
- Practice editing and revising game narrative scripts.

**Format**

This class is largely project-oriented, guided in part by critiques from the instructor and classroom peers, with a focus on in-class discussion. The course content and exercises build toward a large final project, which will be a complete interactive narrative game lasting 10 minutes, from start to end, excluding other paths. Student will use Twine.

**Academic Integrity**

Trust: Student-Teacher relationships are built on trust. Students must trust that teachers have made appropriate decisions about the structure and content they teach. And, teachers must trust that the assignments that the students turn in are their own. Acts, which violate this trust, undermine the educational process. The Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities defines various forms of Academic Dishonesty and you should make yourself familiar with these. In this class, all assignments that are turned in for a grade must represent the student’s own work. In cases where help was received, or teamwork allowed, a notation on the assignment should indicate your collaboration.

Plagiarism: All work produced in this course must be original and created by the student. Incidents of academic dishonesty or attempted academic dishonesty will result in grade penalties and may be referred to the Dean of Students. While the creation of digital artwork may utilize prior works for inspiration, reference, and source material, assignments in this class are expected to represent the student’s originality and technical ability through the creation of substantially new works. When in doubt, ask your instructor.

**6000 Level**

Those students taking this course at the 6000 level comply with the same 4000 level format, but are required to create a longer game (usually 15 -- but no more than 20 – minutes in duration), and the expectation is for it to be more complex and innovative than at the 4000 level, demonstrating a greater grasp of the subject area and a more assured ability to work within the medium.
