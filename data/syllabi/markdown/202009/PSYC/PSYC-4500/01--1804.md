### Additional Information for the Instructor Section:

The title of the course reflects the course’s content. The course is an inspection of how drugs affect behavior and, in turn, how the modified behavior influences society. There is an emphasis on the effects of addictive drugs.

There were probably no undergraduate courses offered by colleges and universities on alcohol and drug abuse prior to the 1960s. Discussions of drugs and their effects were the providence of pharmacists and physicians (and their educational programs). There was little concern, in the general intellectual community, about topics such as drug abuse and behavioral toxicology. The liberal arts, as taught at most colleges, did not have courses on drugs, poisons, mind-altering agents, etc. This was the case even though conceptualizations concerning drugs have had major influences on virtually every sphere of human activity. Major religious doctrine focuses, for example, on alcohol use. Wars have been, and continue to be, fought over the right to sell addictive drugs. Persons with the ability to sell medicines can, and often do, make fortunes. Countries with the ability to manufacture and distribute drugs often become inordinately rich. Illegal drug-trade and its consequences contribute to major social problems of our society and, furthermore, have done so for some time.

The activities of the ‘60s forced a wider consideration of topics germane to drugs and behavior. The USA experienced an epidemic of illegal drug use. There was widespread debate about the causes of the epidemic and the appropriate responses to it. In response to this new concern, a few college courses were organized. I organized one of the first (titled Alcohol and Drug Abuse). A professor (Oakley Ray, now deceased) compiled sufficient material to put together a textbook (1st edition, 1972), thereby setting the stage for courses with the same title as Ray’s text: Drugs, Society and Behavior.

A need arose for factual, unbiased information on drugs and their influence on behavior and society and that need was met by the text developed by Ray. As relevant information was compiled, courses were developed that considered the broad spectrum of the role of drugs in society. With the development of those courses, a consideration of drugs, poisons, and other mind-altering agents were put into the liberal arts curriculum. The topics had become too important to leave to the providence of schools of medicine.

This course used editions of the text by Ray, for years, but it needed to be supplemented by lectures. Hundreds of Rensselaer’s students have taken the course yearly. **Now there is a new textbook that is a marked improvement over all others being offered.**

It should be noted that this course is not a “drug-prevention-course.” Prevention of alcohol- and drug-abuse is a goal of other kinds of institutions. Our goal should be an unbiased, indeed a scientific, inspection of issues associated with the often politically controversial issues associated with the decision to prescribe for others (e.g., as a parent to a child or as a licensed health-care provider) or to personally take any one of a number of drugs. Often the scientifically derived facts about the effects of a number of drugs lead to the conclusion that their cost-benefit ratio was not favorable, but there is no initial bias upon beginning the inspection of the costs and benefits. Now, there is a new and better text and now a new format for arranging for the student to learn the lessons of that new text. Please see the addendum to this syllabus.

## Grading Criteria

### Criteria Details:

To earn a good grade, you must demonstrate that you have mastered the material of the two textbooks.

100% of the total grade is assessed by course-tests.

There is the requirement to virtually meet during scheduled class periods (i.e. from 6:00 to 7:50 pm, New York time or the same 1hr, 50 min you chose on the day of a class meeting There will be brief lectures during a class-period on most scheduled meetings except when Tests are given. Any time other than when Prof lectures or tests given, you are tasked with reading the textbooks. The rationale for my rather novel way of arranging for learning of the two textbooks’ content will be presented in an addendum following the ordinary details of a syllabus.

There will be four tests. Actually, there are four tests with a retake of each test. Only the score on the retake will be used to calculate grades. Any student answering all questions correctly initially need not to do a retake (a free-time- reward for efficient studying). Since you will generally have most all of the test questions roughly available to you, I anticipate that most students will get all items correct on the retake of a test (will have to study, of course, to do that).

Items of the first tests will be on the subsequent tests. Such is designed to promote learning of critical info, via something similar to practice.

To gage retention of learned material, the last test will be a comprehensive test featuring many previous test items plus new items of last segment of course.

Test 4 (the last test), there will be an opportunity to take the retake the test twice. This is a good opportunity to earn an A.

**Note**: correct answers on retake of Test 1 is scored 1 (1 times number of questions answered correctly or stated differently **the number of points** is the number of correct answers on Test 1)

Correct answers on retake of Test 2 is scored 2 times number of questions answered correctly.

Correct answers on retake of Test 3 is 3 times the number of questioned correctly answered.

Correct answers on retake of Test 4 is 4 times the number of questions answered correctly on the **2nd retake** of Test 4. If Your first attempt to answer Test 4 questions correctly is 100% correct, you have finished the course. If you answer all questions correctly on the first retake of Test 4, you have finished the course. If you take the opportunity to do the 2nd retake of Test 4, and fail to answer all questions correctly, your grade will be number of questions answered correctly times 4 and that number of points will be used in calculating your grade.

The number of test questions increase as tests are given. So, Test 4 will demonstrate retention of learned material.

The number of points achieved by taking the 4 tests will be divided by the possible number of points. That ratio will be used to determine a grade as specified below. Note, if you score 100% on the first try of any test, you need not take any retakes. You end the course early and have time to study for other courses if you can achieve a grade you are satisfied with.

**A grades** are earned by those scoring 100 to 95% of points earned via retakes of tests divided by all possible points.

**B grades** are earned by those scoring 94 to 85% of points earned divided by all possible points that were earnable.

**C grades** are earned by those scoring 85% to 80% of points earned divided by all possible points.

**D grades** are earned by those scoring 79% to 75% of points earned divided by all possible points.

**F grades** are assigned if you cannot answer more than 74% of earnable points, that is, you have not studied sufficiently to earn university-credits for mastering the course.

If you find yourself not attending to the course-material sufficiently during the first parts of the course, I suggest you drop the course and attend to your other courses.

**The questions at the beginning and the end of the assigned chapters will often be part of graded tests.**
