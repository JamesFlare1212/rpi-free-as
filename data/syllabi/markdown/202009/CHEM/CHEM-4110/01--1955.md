## Course Goals

### Goals:

**Course Goals / Objectives:** This course presents advanced concepts in Instrumental Analysis and Analytical Chemistry and is designed to familiarize undergraduate students with fundamental concepts surrounding analytical instrumentation, which reaches into many areas of the physical sciences. Throughout the semester, the instructor will present historical perspectives and scientific discussion as to *how* and *why* many of the modern analytical methodologies were developed. Students will gain an appreciation for the design and construction of analytical equipment which lead to detection and quantification of analytes, interaction of electromagnetic radiation with chemical species, as well as collection and processing of analytical signals.

## Learning Outcomes

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes (CLOs):

- Demonstrate a proficient knowledge of modern analytical instrumentation, its uses and operating principles.
- Distinguish between different measures of performance in analytical measurements such as sensitivity, detectability, accuracy, and precision.
- Explain the principles and underlying processes of analytical techniques and extend this knowledge to explain intrinsic differences in analytical capabilities of the various instrumental methods.
- Effectively handle, process, and interpret large datasets produced by analytical instruments

### Other Course Policies:

**Exams:** A total of four exams will be given during the semester (three in-class exams and an online final exam). Students will have 80 minutes to complete the in-class exams and 180 minutes to complete the online final exam. For any remote exams, students will need to be logged into the course WebEx space with their webcam turned on for the duration of the exam. The in-class exams will not necessarily be comprehensive, but understanding of earlier material will be necessary as concepts in this course will build upon each other. The instructor reserves the right to change this policy if certain key concepts are not being fully understood by the class.

**Make-up Exams:** Make-up exams are available only to those students who both (i) miss an exam for legitimate reasons (e.g., personal illness, death of a family member, or participation in a sanctioned University event), AND (ii) provide acceptable documentation. A valid medical excuse is a letter or note from a physician which specifically excuses you from attending classes. If you miss an exam, it is your responsibility to inform the professor ***within 24 hours,*** via email or telephone, of the reasons for missing the exam, preferably prior to the exam.

**Calculator:** Only ***non-graphing*** calculators will be permitted during exams (scientific calculators are acceptable).

**Homework/In-class quizzes:** A combination of ten (10) homework assignments and in-class quizzes will be given throughout the semester, with approximately one occurring at the end of each non-exam week. Homework assignments will consist of problems from various textbooks and will be posted on the LMS (Blackboard Learn) about one week prior. **Note**: these homework assignments will NOT be thoroughly graded, but checked for completeness and are designed to help students prepare for quizzes and exams. Homework assignments should be **hand-written** and submitted to the LMS before **5 pm** (Eastern time) on **Fridays**. On certain weeks, unannounced in-class quizzes will be given in lieu of handing in homework assignments. The quiz questions will come from the homework as well as the lecture materials. The instructor reserves the right to alter this homework and quiz policy during the course of the semester. Late homework assignments and make-up quizzes will NOT be allowed without a legitimate excuse.

**Data Collection and Analysis Project (D-CAP):** A topic of emerging importance in chemical and instrumental analysis is the handling of multiple, large datasets. Specifically, there are three important aspects to working with such data:

1. Data collection
2. Processing

- Interpretation/presentation of findings

In data collection, it is important to ensure the recorded values are accurate and repeatable. Processing of data is important to minimize contributions from noise sources and enhance signal-to-noise. Proper interpretation and presentation of the important findings in large data in a clear, concise manner is essential to disseminate scientific discovery.

To practice these skillsets, groups of 2 or 3 students will be expected to devise and carry-out a project that explores and utilizes at least two of the three aspects listed above. Each project will culminate in either a formally written report or a presentation to the class. In either case, students should clearly present the basis of the project, data manipulation approaches used, advantages/disadvantages over other methods, and findings from the project data. Project ideas will need to be approved by Prof. Shelley by October 19. The reports or presentations will be due by Dec. 7 (but earlier is always better).

Students are encouraged to base their D-CAP on their own undergraduate research. internship experiences, and/or personal interests. A bigger list of example topics and more specific guidance on the project will provided later in the semester. But, some possible project topics include:

- Building a spectrophotometer from the ground up
- Comparison of data smoothing approaches
- Methods for enhanced data visualization
- Multivariate statistical approaches for data grouping
