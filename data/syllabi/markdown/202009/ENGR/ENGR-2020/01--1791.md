## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

***The Scenario*** You’ve been hired by The Global Design Federation (GDF), a global design firm specializing in online design collaboration that’s intrigued by your social-sciences based design program. They want you to tackle one of the following two complex social problems unique to our time, using your novel skill set.

**“Designing for COVID-19 and/or #BLM”**

You may interpret this theme broadly, as long as there’s a clear focus on social (or environmental) responsibility and social justice.

***Overview*** The focus of this studio is the following:

- Engineering design methods and their critical deconstruction;
- A semester-long design project, completed in two full iterations;
- Further work on your identity as a designer; some attention to aesthetics and the “practical integration of STS”
- New experiences with larger group work[1], and enhanced professionalism (mainly oral & written communication)

Accommodating for COVID-19, and our hybrid format, we also focus on

- Remote collaboration skills
- Prototyping, but with less emphasis than in a typical year (this will require compensation in future studios)
- Addressing interactions with users and user-centered design that was not covered in DIS Studio II.

In contrast to Studios I and II, our focus will be on a single, semester-long design project carried out in two full design iterations. The course will be built around the following units:

- Problem identification and formulation
- Concept generation & rapid prototyping
- Evaluation and client communications
- Design process audit and iteration
- Final prototype and presentation

Each unit will run for approximately three weeks. See “Course Schedule,” for details.

***Format*** Because of COVID-19, I’ve made several accommodations to Studio

***(COVID-19*** III as it normally runs:

- Daily check-ins
- Required attendance, but with asynchronous options
- Increased emphasis on group work / peer learning and support
- Guided exercises (E- series memos)
- TA & instructor support for group work

I actually do research on engineering education, and these changes, along with others embedded into this syllabus, are designed to help maintain student engagement, which research has shown is the greatest challenge with online learning. My design goal for this class is to provide you with as close to the full learning experience of taking Studio III, but with full accommodations for those struggling with online learning, or have less than optimal home study environments (slow Internet, health issues, time zone issues; challenging home environments). Please let me know about your individual situations if any of this applies, and we’ll work out individual accommodations. My goal is not to make the course “easier,” but to make sure that everyone, despite their situation, is able to make progress in the best way possible, while continuing to enjoy the DIS experience. Partly because of our online/hybrid format this fall, on a typical Tuesday we will start by discussing key readings, introducing new concepts, and then getting you set up with your group projects. After the first hour, you will typically be working within your groups, but with required check-ins with the TA and/or instructor (who may act as if they’re a senior design consultant and partner with GDF). Attendance during all three hours of class time is expected, but much of this will be spent in breakout rooms, and further accommodations will be made as appropriate. Please keep in mind that sometimes we may need to reconvene as a class at the end for reporting out, or to discuss course logistics and other matters.

Most of your major presentations and other reporting activities will occur on Fridays. I’m also excited to report that in spite of the pandemic, we’ll be joined this semester by the folks from IBM’s Z Systems Design during our design critiques. (Two of them are PDI graduates.) On Fridays where there are no presentations, the first hour (following a general check-in) will be devoted to discussing an “STS reading,” or else something related to the design profession. (In reality, the STS and non-STS aspects of the course are fully integrated into your design exercises.) The “E-series” memos (see LMS) are an especially important tool this semester for online learning & collaboration. They help this course operate in a partially “flipped” format, so that the instructors are more in a position to answer questions and provide the support you need for your guided inquiry into the aspects of design we cover in Studio III. Please note that you are NOT required to follow any of these exercises to the letter. They offer ideas, and guidance regarding best practices. That said, these exercises are designed to confer a specific skill, and you remain responsible for the learning outcomes we indicate in this syllabus. Decide how you want to handle this as a team. It’s also important to note that the E-Series Memos are not a substitute for the overall design process, which you must develop, follow, and strategically depart from in ways that keep your design project moving forward. One of the first lessons about a real-world design process is that it needs to be both disciplined and fluid. Good design cannot follow the linearity of a fixed sequence of exercises. If you emerge out of this course with an understanding of why that’s the case, you’ve probably earned your “A” for this semester!

Reading, and reviewing as appropriate, all assignments, exercises, announcements, and other handouts (including this syllabus and the class schedule) with appropriate care, is a requirement for this course (and also your career as a professional designer). The degree of detail you find in our assignments will not be dissimilar to the work expectations of a design internship or job.

***Collaboration*** Partly in compensation for COVID-19, but as a matter of good, professional design practice, we want you to collaborate—not only within your team, but across teams. Share what you know with the other teams. Whether we’re thinking in terms of COVID or #BLM, these are grand societal problems where we all stand to get further, and faster, by working together, not apart. Talk about COVID-19, or BLM, and how you approached your background research efforts so that everyone benefits from the knowledge you’re assembling. The competitive aspects of the design, which we may read something about, is a thing you will encounter in the design world. But you may be surprised by how much professional networking and collaboration is also a key to the careers of the best designers. Assume, as the scenario presented to you does, that you work for the same design firm, and that helping other teams is something that will be rewarded. The only requirement is that you give fair credit to all work performed by others, so accurate evaluations of individual performance remain possible.

***Team Size, Project*** This is something we’ll discuss during our first class. One of the major

***Leaders, & Class*** goals of Studio III is to learn how to tackle complex problems using a

***Liaisons*** large design team. This is not an atypical situation in the design world. A new software system developed by Google or Apple might involve several hundred, if not several thousand software engineers. A new sedan from Tesla would have required the work of a core team of several dozen designers, plus hundreds of engineers designing specific components and manufacturing processes. From the brief survey I conducted, the consensus of the class seems to be that smaller teams (3 or less) are easier to manage. Fair enough. It is difficult to schedule group meetings that everyone can make when you have larger teams, and the risk of free loaders goes up with larger groups. That said, I want you to pause to think about what you want to get from Studio III, and whether there is a way we can incorporate large-group work of the sort this course was designed for. You probably won’t have another chance to do something as large in the DIS studios, and the course is in fact designed to function optimally with groups as large as eight (8) or nine (9) students. There are also ways to manage larger groups—indeed, this is one of the things you’d have an opportunity to learn—such as having a project leader, two managers, and separate groups led by the two managers. Then the coordinating meetings only require the three leader/managers to meet. (And meetings are also easier to schedule online.) However, I also don’t know how COVID-19 affects you, and how you are all navigating the pandemic. Your ability to learn engineering design methods and to critique them will probably **not** be greatly affected by working in smaller teams. The quality of your solutions might be impacted by the smaller problem space that, say, four people can study instead of eight. Given the scope of the assignments, smaller teams may also struggle more if you lose one of your teammates (or one or more underperform). That said, I will accept the class’ collective decision about the team size you want to go with this semester. (It must be at least three, and a workable division.) Note that everything in this course is individually graded, including all group work. You will be asked to submit a concise memo describing your group work so credit is distributed fairly across the team. This should reduce the amount of “free loading.” It will be more like the real world.

## Course Goals

### Goals:

- **Ability to effectively apply engineering design methods through their critical deconstruction.** Many of you will have discovered DIS because of your desire to be “more creative,” and to move away from the more formal aspects of engineering. Others will view design as a hands-on activity, as opposed to something that requires extensive research and planning. As one of two ENGR courses in the DIS curriculum, the focus of this course will be on providing you with the skills necessary to participate in a formal design process when this is required by your employer. However, given that DIS (and all of our studios) are simultaneously influenced by STS, you will also be asked to critically analyze and deconstruct engineering design methods, and formal design processes as a whole, so that you know when it is and isn’t useful to use more formal design methodologies. This class requires you to demonstrate this critical ability through your design project, including your reports and reflective writing. The specific design methodologies that we cover will include background research, ethnographic observation and other fieldwork options, stakeholder analysis, problem formulation (mission statement, objectives tree, and design specifications), concept generation, evaluation, rapid prototyping, design audit, iteration, and project management. In addition, your ability to analyze a complex social problem, and to productively apply engineering design methodologies to it, will be central to our evaluation. (40%)
- **Demonstrated prototyping skills, with attention to aesthetics.** We expect every student in Studio III to improve upon the making skills you acquired in Studios I and II. In addition to the foam core models you build during the first rapid prototyping exercise, the primary medium we expect you to use in Studio III consists of 3D printing, woodworking, and one other medium of your choice. For each medium you use, plan on producing rough sketches as well as formal drawings and renders (e.g. using Rhino) appropriate to the assignment. This is an individual requirement. Although you may use extensive collaboration to compensate for lack of individual access to equipment and resources, make sure you make necessary arrangements to engage with prototyping work this semester. We also expect everyone to contribute equally to the prototyping effort--at least at a baseline level--so every DIS student extends their skills in this area. The focus of Studio III will be on the *refinement* of techniques you acquired earlier, versus direct instruction with each media. If you require additional instruction, it will be your responsibility to let us know what you need. We will work with Mike and others as appropriate to make sure you have access to appropriate training videos and modules. We also expect you to begin developing and refining your aesthetic sensibility in a way that is consistent with your individual career goals. Your emerging aesthetic sensibility will be demonstrated through written reports, a design portfolio, and the prototypes that you fabricate. This component has been reduced from 25% to 15% this year because of the more limited access many of you will have to making facilities as a result of COVID-19. (15%)
- **Developing your own identity as a designer, while engaging with STS.** DIS is a social science based design program that incorporates concepts and perspectives from STS. It also draws more broadly on social sciences methodologies, such as ethnography and stakeholder analysis, to enhance your ability to understand and tackle complex problems. Through the course of this semester, you will be asked to continue on your individual journeys of constructing your own identity as a designer, distinct from the industrial design profession as well as the way design is practiced, usually, within engineering. We also expect that the complementary readings in STS that we’ve assigned will influence your design practice in some way. While there is no “right answer” to the kind of identity you should have coming out of DIS, we will be looking to your design journal and other sources to make sure this is something you are actively exploring. (15%)
- **Demonstrated efficacy with online group work.** Because of COVID-19 and the much heavier emphasis on remote learning, we have made remote learning and collaboration its own learning objective this semester. While we will provide various exercises and handouts to facilitate online group work, it will be your responsibility to figure out, as a team, what you need to do to ensure continued engagement and productive group work in an online setting. A good grade is this class is not guaranteed, although effort in the face of the pandemic will be fully recognized. Finding ways to effectively report on problems that you are experiencing with group work, and working with the instructor to resolve them, will also be one component of this learning outcome and associated grade. (15%)

- **Demonstrated improvements in your other professional skill sets.** Most designers work with clients and end-users, and people in customer service, sales, and manufacturing. While group work will be assessed separately this semester, this course places strong emphasis on your ability to speak and write, via effective memos, reports, posters, presentations, and presentation slides. The focus of this learning outcome will be on individual efforts and improvement, not some fixed standard of expected performance. (15%)

## Grading Criteria

### Criteria Details:

There will be a grading rubric for each assignment. The following serves as my uniform evaluation criteria for every assignment:

*Outstanding (A+):* Work that significantly exceeds instructor expectations. Work that demonstrates an understanding of and/or the mastery of a practice that is clearly beyond the expectations of this course. *Strong (A):* Work that exceeds instructor expectations for student work and performance. Highly professional and/or effective conduct in Studio that contributes to the class as a whole. *Solid (B):* Work that solidly meets instructor expectations regarding specified learning objectives associated with each assignment. Also indicates solid attendance\* and good participation in class, as well as contributions to teamwork. Adequate time devoted to assignments outside of class. (\*An asynchronous participation option will be given to all students with circumstances that make regular attendance difficult.) *Developing (C):* Some clear progress towards stated learning objectives, but current ability or knowledge falls short of anticipated learning outcomes. May include identifiable deficiencies related to attendance, professionalism, class participation, effective group work, and/or effort and contributions to the team. *Basic (D):* Demonstrates a basic understanding of the subject, or a rudimentary ability to follow a defined process, but has not achieved a level of mastery where it is advisable to continue to the next studio in DIS. May also include significant deficiencies related to attendance, professionalism, class participation, effective group work, and/or effort and contributions to the team.

**Students will likely not receive a passing grade** in the course until they have completed all major (numbered) assignments with an individual grade of at least a “D.” This includes all team assignments, so if you do not do significant work on every assignment, you might not pass this course (except where specific COVID-19 related accommodations have been granted.)

## Policies

### Attendance Policy:

Attendance: This course is set up as if it were a professional design studio. Unless there are specific circumstances we’ve discussed, you are expected to arrive on time, and to use the entire duration of Studio through effective project management. You may miss up to two classes without explanation or penalty. Beyond this, you must make up your absences, *excused or unexcused,* through specific arrangements with the instructors. Each excess absence that you fail to make up will result in a 1/3 letter grade penalty from your final grade. If you arrive late or leave Studio early without permission, this will count as a partial absence (1/4 up to 10 minutes; 1/2 up to one hour). Anyone with circumstances that make regular attendance in class difficult may make special arrangements with the instructor for asynchronous participation. Because of COVID-19, these circumstances might vary, and may include poor internet bandwidth, health issues (including the health of family members), challenges related to being in a different time zone, or difficult home environments (lack of quiet study spaces; family expectations while living at home). You will still be required to strive to meet all of the learning objectives for the course, but I will work to come up with individual accommodations that are appropriate to your situation. So long as you are willing to make an earnest effort, I will make sure that COVID-19 does not interfere with your progress through our degree program.

Excused absences simply ensure that you are allowed to make up the absence and any associated assignment. To obtain a verified excuse, send the course instructors an email, in advance whenever possible, with the date of your absence and the reason for the absence. You are responsible for all class content and announcements that you miss, and should obtain this information from your classmates.

### Other Course Policies:

Class Participation: Class participation is an integral part of this course and is reflected in the grading structure. However, students who have difficulty speaking up in class (for example, ESL students) may approach the instructors during the first two weeks of the semester to make special arrangements.

Late Submissions: No late papers or projects will be accepted except through specific arrangement with the instructors.

Gender Fair Language & Design: Students in this course are expected to use gender fair language in their writing and oral communications. Gender-specific language is rarely necessary, and can be especially problematic with regards to universal design principles. For example, every time you use a masculine-oriented word to refer to people in general, the implicit effect, even if unintended, is to whisper: *women don’t count.* Essays, reports, presentations, and other work that do not use gender fair language will not receive a passing grade until corrected. If you are unfamiliar with the practice of gender fair writing, you should read "Gender Fair Language” https://www.cyut.edu.tw/~lhli/rmtw/A10.pdf). Gender Fair Design will be integral to the major assignments of the course, and will be addressed through our evaluation rubrics and associated evaluations.

Time Commitment: In general, we expect all students, including project leaders, to put approximately the same number of hours into the course. In addition to the six hours of scheduled studio time, students will typically need to spend an average of an additional 1-3 hours a week reading, and 3-5 hours in a studio, lab, or group meeting conducted online to work on your design project. Students with learning disabilities and language issues are encouraged to speak with us for guidance and accommodations.

### Additional Academic Integrity Course Policy and Penalty Information:

Academic Honesty: Student-teacher relationships are built on trust. Students must trust that teachers have made appropriate decisions about the structure and content of the course, and teachers must trust that the assignments students turn in are their own. Any act that violates this trust undermines the educational enterprise. They contradict our very reason for being at Rensselaer. The *Rensselaer Handbook* *of Student Rights and Responsibilities* defines various forms of academic dishonesty and the procedures for responding to them. You are expected to have reviewed and understand the Handbook. The following additional policies apply to this course:

- Aiding and abetting someone who seeks to commit an act of academic dishonesty is as serious an offense as the act contemplated.
- Some other acts of academic dishonesty that apply to studio based courses such as ours include: submission of any content (whether drawings or written work) not created by and original to you as your own work, and signing an attendance sheet for someone not present in the class.
- Collaboration is encouraged. However, assisting someone else with work where they intend to turn in the work as if it was done on their own will itself be considered a serious violation of academic honesty. (Just make sure to give credit for any assistance given and received; and if you are having trouble doing the work, let us know so we can help you find a solution.)

For this course, penalties for academic dishonesty, especially in cases involving false representation of work, may well result in your failing the course, with a citation placed in your academic record. While the process for assessing the situation is as outlined in the *Rensselaer Handbook,* the initial judgment about a violation, and the penalty if any, will be determined at the discretion of the instructors and what they believe to have occurred. If you have any doubts about a situation, especially regarding plagiarism, please check with us first.

Grade Appeals: All grade appeals must be submitted in writing to one of the instructors within a week of the grades being posted. You are entitled to an evaluation of your appeal within ten business days of submitting your appeal. Please note that appeals are possible only on the basis outlined in the *Rensselaer Handbook*. The full procedure related to grade appeals is listed in the handbook.

## Other Course-Specific Information

### Additional Course Information:

A Note on Group Work: As a second year design studio, successful group work is an expectation for this course. All students much make an attempt to deal with real and potential conflict within their team, and develop skills for working through differences. Those who put in the effort to do so will be recognized for their efforts in situations where group work fails, although you may be asked to describe the situation in a form that allows other team members to respond. All team members should recognize that some of the work of a project leader will remain invisible; as noted in the main body of the syllabus itself, the project leader must assume responsibility, in turn, for making sure that all group members have work to do, and try to make sure that the work proceeds smoothly—or as smoothly as possible in any creative work environment! The team leader must make at least three attempts to make it possible for each individual to contribute effectively to the team, after which the responsibility shifts to the student to demonstrate that they are interested in and able to contribute to their team. The instructors are here to help, so if you are facing difficulties please speak with us as soon as possible. **Early interventions tend to be the most effective.**
