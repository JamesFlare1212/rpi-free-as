## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

This course will introduce you to the field of motion picture studies from aesthetic, critical, reflexive, historical, and conceptual/theoretical perspectives. The aim is to examine, enhance, and transform your personal relationship to film in a dialogue-based setting. Using student-centered learning techniques, the course will reflect your interests as a learning community, with the aim of developing within you a thoughtful, informed, critical, and empathetic approach to motion pictures, informed by an awareness of the global mediascape. We’ll watch lots of movies, and think deeply about how they do what they do.

## Course Goals

### Goals:

1\) You will develop a faculty for critical media engagement with motion pictures, learning how to apply aesthetic, historical, and critical frameworks as tools towards deepening your understanding of motion pictures. You will learn some of the fundamental terms and concepts used in film studies. Of particular importance is learning how to make connections between conceptual issues and cinema as practice (in viewing, critiquing, sharing, and creating).

2\) As a complement to these critical tools, you will enhance your reflexive media awareness—your processual and dialogic understanding of your relationship to motion pictures (and media in general)—so that you can more fully articulate your experience of motion pictures, contextualize it, and empathize with others’ perspectives. You will develop your communicative/generative skills by articulating and refining your thoughts in oral, written, and presentational forms, and (if you are so inclined) by creating media works in various formats as a way of engaging with concepts we encounter in this class.

3\) Although this class focuses on film/motion pictures, it’s lessons can be applied to, and contextualized in, the larger mediascape; you should gain a deeper understanding of how media shapes us, as individuals, as groups and communities, and as cultures.

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes Assessment Measures:

Assignments / Assessment Categories Explanations:

‘A Filmic Experience’ Short Essays

[20,000 total]

In the first week of class, as a part of our introductions, you will reflect on and write about a filmic/motion picture experience that was very important to you. You will be given a few guidelines on things to think about, but mostly this is meant to be as vivid a recollection of the film as you can muster. Later in the semester, you will have the option to return to this film as a potential ‘cinematic exploration’ where you reconsider that film/experience in light of the kinds of studies we’ll have done thus far.

Preparation + Presence & Professionalism (Participation through Negotiated Self-Assessments)

[100,000 total; 5 X 20,000 (10,000 for Preparation + 10,000 for Presence & Professionalism)]*

This class is based in discussion and community; your active participation is essential.

At the end of each 3 week period, 5 times over the class, you will write brief self-assessments based on our criteria (I’ll give you a template to use); I’ll respond to that self-assessment based on my notes, and give you a medal grade (open to discussion/negotiation).

1\) Preparation for Class: This is a measure of how thoroughly you’ve prepared for the classes, including the thoughtfulness you put into mini-assignments demonstrating clear understandings of the reading materials, your decision to do to do further daily research to feed those responses, and your participation in online discussion boards and forums. For the most part, any item that appears on the semester schedule in all caps as something to ‘READ’ or ‘PREPARE’ or ‘’WATCH’ denotes something that will have an associated mini-assignment falling into this category.

2\) Presence & Professionalism: This includes your full attendance to class, mentally as well as physically (including your use of electronic devices, apps only in the service of the class), as well as your conscientiousness in communicating with your classmates for group work, and your courtesy/professionalism in corresponding with the professor about potential absences or concerns. This also includes various ways you can add ‘contributions’ to the discussion / dialogue / community of the classroom. ‘Contributions’ is left open-ended, because some of you will often offer comments in full-class discussion; some of you will be more active in small break-out groups; some of you will make comments on online forums, or answer with a greater degree of detail than necessary in the mini-assignments (this is where ‘Preparation’ spills over into ‘Presence’); some of you will find other ways to contribute to our community’s examinations of superheroes across media.

Keep in mind, you’ll be self-assessing and negotiating ‘Participation’; using my notes, I will be keeping you honest in that self-assessment. This is also a chance for you to reflect on the class, and give me direct feedback on how the class is going. Your comments will shape the class.

\* Note: The final self-assessment will include a reflection on the final project process and the course itself as a whole—a chance to provide me feedback that will help me to shape the class in the future.

‘You’re the expert’ Days – Group Class Leading

[30,000 total]

During weeks 4-9, in small groups of 2-4 students, in connection with a chapter of your choosing, you have the option to collaborate to design an activity or set of activities for the class. Those activities should engage us with the content of the chapter that we are studying. I will demonstrate an example of one or two versions of what you might do, but you should feel free to be creative with your approach; the primary goal is for you to connect the content of that chapter in an experiential way. The secondary goal, and certainly effect, of the exercise is that it will make you even more of an expert on that particular topic.

Essay Test - Film Concepts from Weeks 2-9

[60,000 total]

This will be a take-home essay test in which you apply concepts and terms from the course to a film of your choosing, constructing a precise articulation of your understanding of the film through those terms, using evidence from the film to back up that understanding. In week 9 of the course, just ahead of the test, we as a group will discuss and shape the parameters for the test; collaboratively, you will have a good deal of input into what kind of an ‘essay test’ will best allow you to demonstrate your knowledge.

Film History Timeline Activity

[30,000 total; includes on-time submission of contributions and application of critical lens]

We will spend the better part of weeks 11-12 enacting a whirlwind tour of the history of cinema. As a group, we will construct a google slides document tracing some of the contours of film history. After an initial discussion in week 10 of early film, we will break the historical chapter (Ch.2 in textbook) into roughly 20 subsections, and cover 5 each class over 4 classes. Students have the option to choose 1 film/period in the first week, and 1 film/period in the second week, so that every participating student will cover 2 films/periods (you can’t choose to do just half of the activity). On your own, or as a pair (depending on how many students participate), you will have guidelines provided as to how you cover it, using critical tools we’ll have studied. When all is said and done, this activity will give us an overarching perspective on the development of motion pictures, and a sense of the complexity of that history.

Cinematic Explorations / Spinoffs and Sequels / Revisiting A Filmic Experience

[Variable – Roughly 5 x 1,000 ~ 10,000 points possible, depending on the scope of the projects,

With a 50,000 point maximum across the semester]

Beginning in week 4, and continuing through week 11 of the class, you will be given the option of doing up to five ‘cinematic explorations.’ I will provide examples below and in class, and extra reading materials and suggestions in a folder on the LMS. You will provide some kind of a report—either a written essay, a slides document, or a ‘product’ in another medium of your choosing—and you will debrief the rest of the class. The idea behind these is to give you a chance to explore some aspect of cinema that you are passionate about, to have you share it with the group, to give you ‘credit’ for it as a part of your learning in the class. Often, too, I’ve found that students will do one of these ‘explorations’ and then find that they are inspired to expand into their final project; this is highly encouraged.

Examples might include: Applying a chapter’s concepts to a film of your choosing; doing a creative project that explores a chapter’s concepts more experientially (like an editing project or mash up inspired by the editing chapter, or a photography project inspired by the cinematography chapter); reading a substantial critical, historical, theoretical, or economically-based article on film and sharing its main points and significance with the rest of the class; we’re coming up to the 2020 election, and you could watch a political film or documentary and share that with the class. If you come up with something completely different, suggest it to me and I’m sure we can work something out; if you have an idea for a topic but aren’t sure where to look, talk with me and I can help you to find something.

Finally, there is at least one special exploration that all of you are invited to do during the Film Essay Test Week—revisiting the film that inspired your ‘A Filmic Experience’ essay, and reconsidering it with the critical distance of time and new knowledge. How might your understanding of that film change?

Final Project Proposal & Conference

[20,000 total; includes on-time, detailed submission of proposal plus conference with professor]

By the beginning of week 11, you will prepare a proposal for your final project, and then we will have a short individual conference to discuss it. I will provide a flexible template, and you should be as detailed as you can; however, the direction you describe in the proposal isn’t set in stone, and it is likely to evolve as you work. I’ll give you feedback to help shape that project, and hopefully be able to provide you with some resources. Your proposals will help me to determine how to group you into collaborative cohorts for the final project preparation period.

Final Project

[60,000 total]

For the final week of the semester, you will all prepare a culminating final project. This project will be an in-depth engagement with some aspect of cinema that you are deeply passionate about. It could be tied to the (tongue-in-cheek) themes of “Why not save the world of film?” or “How is film changing the world?”—i.e., exploring how film shapes us as a culture—but your project need not be limited by that potential focus. You will have an enormous amount of leeway in the shape of the project. In general, though, it will fit into one of the following three options:

Option 1: You will write a finely-honed Research Paper or Critical/Rhetorical Essay of approximately 1600-2500 words (6.5-10 pages). Whether you choose to write an academic research paper or a rhetorical essay, or use another writing format inspired by the work we’ve done in the class, you will be asked to use outside sources. You may be to use one of the earlier essays from the class as a springboard toward the final, though this will mean expanding its scope—in this case, please consult with the instructor.

Option 2: You will create a brief Essay in a Non-Traditional Format, such as a ‘video essay,’ a ‘videographic essay,’ or an essay in another medium. There is a brief section on video essays in the text giving a sense of how these might be structured, and I will show student examples.

Option 3: You will engage in a Creative Project, instead of the final paper. This creative project must engage with conceptual/theoretical issues we’ve dealt with in class and will be accompanied by a brief Reflective Essay (2-3 pages) that outlines your process and elucidates the theoretical connections you’ve made through the creation of the work.

For Options 2 & 3, keep in mind that while I have media production experience and may be able to give advice to you on your process, this is not a production-related class and we won’t be covering technical processes in detail. Therefore, you should work in a medium that you already feel comfortable with or be prepared to learn that medium on your own.

For all final project options, you must include research sources that you will cite in a proper MLA formatted ‘works cited’ page.

Cinema Panels

[20,000 total; 10,000 for the group & 10,000 for your portion of the presentation]

As a part of the final project process, you will be arranged into 4~5 person cohorts; the cohort members will provide each other feedback during the preparation phase. Each cohort will have a full class day to: 1) present, describe, or share your projects and 2) engage the class through activities or discussion—you will work as a team to create an experience for us In general, most students do individual projects…but if you have a particular degree of chemistry and find a way to create a truly collaborative project, that is great! Please discuss with me the possibility of collaborative projects during the proposal stage.

## Grading Criteria

### Criteria Details:

ASSESSMENT: ‘CINEMA POINTS’-BASED ‘GAMEFUL’ GRADING

Introduction:

Grading for this class will unfold a little differently than you’re used to. Using what might be called a ‘gameful’ approach, your performance in the class is based on the points that you earn, much like in a game system (a video game, a table-top role-playing game, or what have you). Like in some games, or in many popular films, there’s even a ‘clock’ defined by milestones in the semester; time runs out quickly!

Everyone begins with 0 points. At the start of the semester, there are 390,000+ points to be earned. As you accomplish tasks in the class, you build up points towards your final grade. There are central tasks for everyone to complete, and there are some ‘cinematic explorations’ that are opportunities to apply knowledge in new ways, as well as opportunities for you to create your own explorations You can often revise or expand daily assignments or required tasks into such explorations, or transform/expand earlier assignments into your final project.

‘Medal’ Based Scoring

Assignments will be assessed using a ‘medal’ system. For most daily and small assignments, that ‘medal’ will reflect the following categories. For certain larger projects, we may establish a more detailed rubric for grading, or reach an understanding through discussion and through my pedagogical expertise. In many cases, particularly for larger assignments, you may be invited (or ask) to revise an assignment in order to improve its medal.

Medal Points % Description

Diamond* 120% A sparkling exemplar for the class, full of dazzling insight

Platinum* 110% A perceptive, clever, and resilient exploration of assignment

Gold 100% Weighty, polished, addressing all required aspects of assignment

Silver 90% A response with clarity and strength, but maybe a tiny tarnish

Bronze 80% Relatively solid response; some clarity, some weaknesses

Copper 70% Mostly complete, but may bend under the stress of examination

Nickel 60% Partial or relatively flimsy assignment that needs strengthening

Tin\*\* 50% Dull or lifeless response, crumples in the breeziest of discussions

*To receive a ‘Platinum’ medal requires that you go above and beyond the requirements of the assignment, and the ‘Diamond’ requires even more in-depth work of great quality.

\*\*Below ‘Tin’ means you simply failed to turn in the assignment, or it’s too incomplete to grade

NOTE: You should recognize that the total of all the required elements of the class is 280,000, which is not enough for an A if all of your work is in the ‘Gold’ category (it’s a B). In other words, if you want an ‘A’ in the class, you will need to do some of the optional ‘Expert Leading,’ ‘Timeline Activity,’ ‘Cinematic Explorations’ assignments, or go over and above the requirements on many of the assignments.

### Additional Academic Integrity Course Policy and Penalty Information:

Educational relationships are built on trust, and acts that violate this trust undermine the educational process; a key component of this trust is that students should not claim the work of others as their own. The Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities and The Graduate Student Supplement defines various forms of Academic Dishonesty, and you should make yourself familiar with these. In this class, all assignments that are turned in for a grade must represent the student’s own work. Submission of any assignment in violation of this policy will result in a grade penalty and disciplinary action.
