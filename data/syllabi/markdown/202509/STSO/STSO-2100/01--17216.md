### Additional Information for Course Information Section:

This course is designed to provide you with a basic understanding of qualitative social research, which you can apply in your chosen field of study. You will gain hands-on experience with data collection methods such as interviews and participant observation, and you will practice analyzing data such as historical documents, interview transcripts, and cultural texts. We will look closely at questions of ethics, politics, and social justice in the research process. I have designed the course to be interactive, with the idea that students learn best by doing. For that reason, the course is structured in a “flipped” manner, meaning that class time is mainly dedicated to working on exercises. I expect you to gain necessary background knowledge through reading the assigned texts outside of class.

## Unit 1: Advanced People Watching

An essential feature of qualitative research is the practice of *noticing* what other people don’t consciously register. There are many good strategies for accomplishing this, which we will work on together this semester. In this first unit of course work, you will observe a public space on campus or connected to student life (e.g. sporting events, the dining hall, public performance events at EMPAC, Vasudha garden, Folsom Library, the student union, etc.). What do you notice when you observe this social setting with the eyes of an alien explorer?

## Unit 2: The Purpose and Ethics of Qualitative Research

This unit is an introduction to qualitative research, with an emphasis on the unique ethical challenges that can arise when collecting and using qualitative data about people.

9/8 - What is qualitative research? What is it not?

· Read for 9/8: Preface and Chapter 1: Introduction in the textbook.

9/11 and 9/15 - How can qualitative research be unethical? How can it serve powerful interests? We will consider how anthropologists helped colonial rulers and how sociologists invaded people’s privacy, among other topics. We will ask how our own experience and positionality affect the knowledge that we produce.

· Read for 9/11: “Research Adventures on Indigenous Lands”

9/18 - What mechanisms exist to ensure that research is conducted ethically? We will examine the history and purpose of Institutional Review Boards.

## Unit 3: Building a Team of Knowledgeable Sources

In this unit, we will start to ask research questions and practice a research approach that focuses on *sources* (people) rather than free-floating information. We will apply this approach to a literature search (9/22). We will also consider methods of *sampling* primary sources that are commonly used in qualitative research (9/25).

Based on research conducted in and outside of class, students will begin writing a literature to inform a research project about an issue or experience affecting RPI students (9/29).

In the final part of this unit, we will dig into some of the underlying principles of qualitative research. How do qualitative researchers design their studies (10/2)? How do we know what we know (10/2)?

· Read for 9/22: Chapter 9 (Reviewing the Literature)

· Read for 9/25: Chapter 5 (Sampling)

· Prepare for 9/29: Short literature review for peer review

· Read for 10/2: Chapter 2 (Research Design)

## Unit 4: Analyzing Existing Qualitative Data

In this unit, we will practice some of the most important methods of data analysis used in qualitative research. This will include working with archives and historical documents (10/9), conducting “content analysis” on cultural texts (10/16), and “coding” transcripts of interviews (10/20 and 10/23).

Students will use these methods, as relevant, to write the “preliminary analysis” section of their research proposal, and receive peer review (10/27).

· Read for 10/9: Chapter 16 (Archival and Historical Research)

· Read for 10/16: Chapter 17 (Content Analysis)

· Read for 10/20: Chapter 18 (Data Analysis and Coding)

· Prepare for 10/23: Chapter 19 (Advanced Codes and Coding)

· Prepare for 10/27: preliminary analysis section for peer review

## Unit 5: Producing Original Qualitative Data

In this unit, we will practice some of the most important methods of data collection used in qualitative research. These include in-depth interviewing (10/30 and 11/3), conducting focus groups (11/6), participant observation (11/10), and deep ethnography (11/13). Because we would need ethics approval to actually collect original data, we will conduct simulations in class. We will also learn more about these methods by studying research reports by scholars who have employed these methods effectively.

· Read for 10/30: Chapter 10 (Intro to Data Collection)

· Read for 11/3: Chapter 11 (Interviewing)

· Read for 11/6: Chapter 12 (Focus Groups)

· Read for 11/10: Chapter 13 (Participant Observation)

· Read for 11/13: Chapter 14 (Deep Ethnography)

## Unit 6: Mixed Methods and Situational Analysis

It is often necessary to employ a mix of research methods to fully understand a topic. For example, researchers might synthesize what they learn from interviews, participant observation, and content analysis of documents to fully understand a situation.

In this final unit (11/17, 11/20, 12/1, 12/4, 12/8, 12/11) students will work in teams to carry out a “situational analysis” of a chosen topic. The team’s grade will be based on a presentation during the last week of class meetings.

# Using Artificial Intelligence

ChatGPT and other AI tools are around and are the elephants in the (class)room. **I expect you to be honest and original**, not only because that is the ethical thing to do, but also because I want you to learn and grow to your full potential. Don’t forget that ChatGPT is a language model, not a factual model. You will *not be successful* if you attempt to use AI to (1) fact check; (2) write a paper; (3) or replace critical thinking.

Additionally, it is worth considering the environmental impact of AI. Each ChatGPT query consumes energy at a rate ten times that for an ordinary Google search [source: https://theconversation.com/ai-supercharges-data-center-energy-use-straining-the-grid-and-slowing-sustainability-efforts-232697]. The growing use of AI strains our power grid and hampers efforts to reduce fossil fuel use.

There are some circumstances where using AI may be reasonable. For example, ChatGPT can give feedback on your writing, helping you to improve your grammar. This is especially helpful when English is not your first language. If you have found other creative ways to use AI to assist with your learning, feel free to come to my office hours to discuss it with me. **If you use an AI assistant like ChatGPT to help with any part of your assignment, you must include a footnote that explains how you used this tool.**

# Discussion Strategies

A good portion of our class meetings will focus on student discussion. To make this effective for everyone, I ask everyone to adhere to the following guidelines.

· Your contribution might be a question; you don’t have to be certain about an idea to make a meaningful contribution to the conversation.

· Talk to each other, not just to the discussion leader or professor.

· Listen and respond to other statements before introducing a new idea.

· Ask questions if you do not understand what someone has said, or you can paraphrase what another student has said for clarification (“I think you said this; is that right?”).

· Refer to evidence from the text to support your ideas.

· Pay attention to your “airtime”—how much you have spoken in relation to other students.

· Don’t interrupt.

# Inclusive Language

The Department of Science and Technology Studies aspires to create a learning environment in which people of all identities are encouraged to contribute their perspectives to academic discourse. This course has guidelines on language and terminology that aim to foster an inclusive and welcoming environment, strengthen academic writing, enrich discussion, and reflect best professional practices.

Gender-inclusive and non-sexist language: Language is gender-inclusive and non-sexist when we use words that affirm and respect how people describe, express, and experience their gender. Gender-inclusive/non-sexist language acknowledges people of any gender (e.g. first-year student versus freshman, chair versus chairman, humankind versus mankind). It also affirms non-binary gender identifications and recognizes the difference between biological sex and gender expression. Students may share their preferred pronouns and names, and these gender identities and gender expressions should be honored.

Non-racist language: The labels and definitions of racial and ethnic categories vary across history, societies, and even situations. In this classroom, we will avoid racial and ethnic slurs and strive to use words that affirm and respect how people describe, express, and experience their race, ethnicity, ancestry, or cultural heritage. Students may share the terminology that they prefer to describe their racial and ethnic identities, and these preferences should be honored.

Non-ableist language: Ableist language, which devalues people with physical and mental disabilities, permeates everyday language. However, there are many options for replacing common ableist rhetoric with more thoughtful alternatives. You are encouraged to read this guide and reflect on what you are communicating when you use ableist terms. https://www.autistichoya.com/p/ableist-words-and-terms-to-avoid.html

# Sexual Misconduct and Title IX

Title IX is a federal law prohibiting sex discrimination. The Title IX section of the Education Amendments Act of 1972 states, “No person in the United States shall, on the basis of sex, be excluded from participation in, be denied the benefits of, or be subjected to discrimination under any education program or activity receiving Federal financial assistance.” Under Title IX, sexual misconduct is considered a form of sex discrimination.

Sexual misconduct includes, but is not limited to: sexual harassment, sexual assault, dating and domestic violence, intimate partner violence, and stalking. Sexual misconduct is not tolerated at Rensselaer, and it is a violation of Institute policy, and state and federal law.

Faculty and other employees are considered mandated reporters when it comes to experiences of interpersonal violence (sexual assault, sexual harassment, dating or domestic violence, and stalking). Disclosures of interpersonal violence must be passed along to Rensselaer’s Title IX Coordinators who can help provide support and academic remedies for students who have been impacted. For more information or to report sexual misconduct, see https://sexualviolence.rpi.edu/.
