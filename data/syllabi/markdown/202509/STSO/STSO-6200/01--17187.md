## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

This PhD-level course investigates the entanglements of science, technology, feminism, and queer theory, with particular attention to questions of racial, colonial, and social justice. Rather than treating science and technology as neutral domains, the course foregrounds their role in shaping and being shaped by power, identity, and inequality. You will engage with foundational and contemporary scholarship in feminist, queer, Indigenous, crip, and decolonial science and technology studies (STS), developing critical tools to interrogate how knowledge, artifacts, and practices emerge within broader struggles over agency, subjectivity, and justice.

The first unit, **(Dis)Orientations**, asks what is feminist and queer about STS, revisiting central debates on standpoint, intersectionality, materiality, and agency. Through close readings of canonical and experimental texts, you will examine how science and technology become sites of both contestation and subversion, and how feminist and queer critiques have unsettled dominant epistemologies.

The second unit, **STS in/for Times of Crisis**, shifts the focus toward the politics of care. Here, you will explore how care has become both a vital framework and a fraught practice in technoscience—ranging from health infrastructures and telecare technologies to algorithmic systems and necropolitical regimes. Readings will address how care is distributed, denied, technologized, and racialized, as well as how it might be reclaimed as a resource for justice in times of crisis.

Across both units, the course asks: How might feminist and queer approaches to science and technology transform not only our critiques but also our practices of inquiry, ethics, and solidarity? By the end of the semester, you will be equipped to critically analyze science and technology as sites of power and possibility, and to imagine forms of care and responsibility adequate to the crises of our time.

**ARCS OF ASSESSMENT**

**Arc 1: Structured Provocation and Discussion Facilitation**

Provoking others to insight is one the most valuable skills practiced in graduate school.

On the date for which you sign up, you are responsible for introducing the reading materials for the day so as to provoke discussion, which you will facilitate for approximately one half of the 2 ½ hour class period (factoring in a 20-minute break). Facilitation should draw out main points, but beyond that you may contextualize the texts; apply concepts raised therein to a specific analytic situation of your choosing; provide background or genealogy by drawing upon additional material; or generously critique the reading by drawing on other texts and contexts. Provocations integrate texts with *different* sources, intentions, or characteristics than those under “official” discussion. Provocations may include video, performance, installation, artistic, or digital projects that provide substantive examples of the work of STS in the world.

**Arc 2: STS in the Present: 4S Report**

You will each have 10 minutes in which to report your insights from attending the 4S conference. Please turn in a short (3-5-page) paper on these reflections on October 15, when you present your insights as part of our collective conversation about 4S. Many of the authors we are reading this semester present at 4S. You may also report on insights generated at the 6S pre-conference meeting.

**Arc 3: Implosion**

This assignment is a 7-10-page paper turned in on November 19 based on Joe Dumit’s “Writing the Implosion.” For this assignment, the student selects a specific artifact, ‘fact’, ‘thing’, or process, and researches the non-obvious relationships of connection between “labor, professional, material, technological, political, economic, symbolic, textual, bodily, historical, and educational” dimensions of that artifact, ‘fact’, or process. Based on the conceptual work of Gilles Deleuze, Michel Foucault, and Donna Haraway, implosion projects offer a unique way into thinking about science and technology.

**Arc 4: Synthesis**

The ability to improvise and synthesize by “thinking on your feet” is essential to success in graduate school. This arc requires that you draw connections across the components of each class, and provide an oral synthesis in the last 20 minutes of class that draws our streams of discussion together and raises questions for us to consider during the week we are apart. The goal is to create continuities and convergences across sessions.

**Arc 5: Final Written Assignment: Science Studies: What’s It to Me?**

Your final written assignment for this course is a position paper summarizing your current understanding of the breadth of science studies that comprise the field of STS, which by now you realize is comprised of interlocking “science studies.” Choose a theme around which to arrange your characterizations; add texts and contexts to those discussed in the course (you may also include your provocation); but reflect as fully as possible upon the body of knowledge in this course. This reflection should be 12-15 pages, typed, double-spaced, polished, and documented--written with your portfolio in mind.

## Grading Criteria

### Criteria Details:

**Participation (10%)**

In a discussion-based seminar, student attendance and active participation is required and crucial to creating a successful learning community. This means coming to class ready to discuss the assigned material with observations, questions, and comments, as well as participating fully in any small and large group activities. Please be present not only physically but also mentally. I expect each of you to come to class having finished all the readings and assignments for the day. I acknowledge some of you may be uncomfortable in large group discussions, and so I recognize engaged listening and note taking as valid forms of participation. Still, consider preparing one specific point about the readings to address in class each day, or challenge yourself to speak up in class once a week in some way.

During class discussion, you should be intentionally aware of your participation, if you find you are speaking repeatedly, step back and allow others the chance to speak up; conversely, if you notice that you are not speaking during class discussions, find ways to contribute to the conversation e.g. draw upon your notes for questions or previously formed thoughts.

**Facilitation (15%)**

You will each sign up to for two weeks as facilitators. You must demonstrate thorough knowledge of the week’s readings and facilitate the discussion via a handout, questions, and/or a creative exercise. You are especially encouraged to incorporate relevant artistic work, video clips, art, blog posts, and current events. The facilitation should take between 10-20 minutes and you are free to use this time as you see fit. During the facilitation exercise, you should consider yourself

in the shoes of a course instructor and lead the discussion in a way that you would if you were a teacher. For this exercise, you should avoid simply summarizing the readings; instead, you should share what you what find interesting, challenging, or confusing. Don’t forget to

- allow & encourage everyone to speak
- ensure that others are respectful in listening and responding even if there is

disagreement

- generate important and debatable claims and conclusions about the

reading

- present back to the class the group’s best points with adequate

explanation/context

- pose questions or solicit comments on specific points/issues

**Concept-to-Research Map (25%): Due on Week 8**

Choose one concept/theory/method from the first unit (e.g., standpoint, intersectionality, agency, orientation). In a 3–4 page essay, trace:

1. Where this concept shows up in your own research field.
2. How the course authors use it differently.
3. A speculative section on how adopting/reframing it might alter your methodology, ethics, or findings

This assignment invites you to take one of the central concepts we’ve explored in the first unit—such as *standpoint*, *intersectionality*, *orientation*, *agency*, or *subversive technology*—and connect it directly to your own research project. Think of this as a “conceptual bridge” between the texts we are reading together and the intellectual architecture of your dissertation, fieldwork, or emerging project. Rather than writing an abstract essay, you should be specific: trace where this concept already appears (or is absent) in your field or discipline. How do authors in your area of study use or misuse the concept? How does the version we’ve encountered in feminist and queer STS scholarship challenge, complicate, or reframe it?

The essay should have three components:

1. **Conceptual grounding** – Briefly explain how the concept is taken up in the course texts.
2. **Field application** – Identify how this concept shows up in your research area, either explicitly or implicitly.
3. **Speculative reorientation** – Propose how thinking with the course version of the concept might shift your methodological, ethical, or theoretical commitments. For example: How would Ahmed’s notion of “orientation” change the way you read your archives? How would Harding’s standpoint epistemology alter your approach to data?

**Purpose**

This assignment helps you start weaving the course’s theoretical strands into the fabric of your own research. It is meant to be exploratory, not definitive—an experiment in thinking across domains. The goal is for you to leave with a clearer sense of how feminist and queer STS concepts can reshape the questions you are asking and the ways you pursue them.

.

**Archival/Artifact Re-Reading (25%): Due on Week 12**

For this assignment, you will select one *piece of evidence* from your own research—this could be an archival document, a passage from fieldnotes, a data visualization, a technological artifact, a policy text, an interview excerpt, or even a photograph. Re-read and re-analyze this object through the lens of *care* as theorized in our course readings (e.g., Puig de la Bellacasa, Murphy, Martin/Myers/Viseu, Pols, Oudshoorn).

Your task is not only to describe the object, but to ask: how does a *care-centered* reading change what this object means, what it hides, or what it does?

When working on your re-reading, you may draw from questions such as:

1. **Visibility and Invisibility of Care**
   - o Where is care present, absent, or obscured in this artifact?
   - o Whose care practices are visible, and whose are backgrounded, erased, or devalued?
2. **Relationality and Responsibility**
   - o What kinds of relationships of care (between people, institutions, technologies, or nonhumans) are implied in this artifact?
   - o Who or what is positioned as responsible for care, and who or what is excluded from that responsibility?
3. **Infrastructures of Care**
   - o How does this object reveal infrastructures that enable or foreclose care?
   - o Does it highlight bureaucratic, technological, or affective systems that distribute care unevenly?
4. **Care as Politics**
   - o How does this artifact demonstrate that care is not only nurturing, but also contested, withheld, or weaponized?
   - o What forms of power, inequality, or violence intersect with the care relations it encodes?
5. **Methodological Reflection**
   - o How would analyzing this artifact through *care* change the way you approach your research more broadly?
   - o What new ethical or methodological commitments emerge if care is taken seriously as an analytic?

**Purpose**

This assignment asks you to let *care* reorient your interpretation of your own research material. By focusing on what care makes visible, fraught, or possible, you will practice using care not only as a topic but as an epistemological and methodological tool for STS.

**Final Paper** (25%)

You have complete freedom with the format and content of your final paper, as long as it incorporates the topics and readings we will cover in this class. Although you might think feminist, queer, crip, and black onto-epistemologies of STS are not relevant to your work, I would like you to push yourself out of your comfort zone and consider how every academic investigation can immensely benefit from these perspectives and their take on social justice. Your final can be a portion of your portfolio, a paper you would like to submit for publication, a dissertation chapter, literature review etc. So, think of what you’d like to get extensive feedback for. Everyone will read others’ final papers and provide detailed feedback for them.
