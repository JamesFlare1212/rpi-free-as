## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

Is it just me, or is it getting pretty hot out there? The water is rising, there’s plastic in the fish, the pipes keep breaking, and the storms are only getting stronger. Our communities face a growing number of challenges, from the extreme (recovering from an oil spill, a hurricane, a wildfire) to the mundane (repairing a roof, turning on the air conditioning, finding an apartment, taking a shower, breathing outside). For sustainability minded people - or for anyone paying attention - we see distressing daily signals of many interlocking ecological, social, and political crises. How did we get here, and what on Earth shall we do?

This course provides students with a wide-ranging understanding of the relationships between people, nature, power and difference that define our socio-ecological worlds. Drawing primarily on concepts and analytical methods from the fields of critical geography, political ecology, political economy, and environmental justice, this class will prepare students to understand & engage complex, intersectional socio-ecological issues related to uneven patterns of development, exposure, vulnerability, and opportunity.

# LEARNING OUTCOMES:

Students enrolled in this course should be prepared to do a great deal of reading, writing, and speaking in small groups and as a class. This course is aimed at preparing students to do upper level work in the critical social sciences, to pursue graduate or professional research related to issues in sustainability or just transition or hold leadership in real life decisions as engineers and planners. This course will prepare students to:

- Apply analytical tools from research in/on critical geography, political ecology, political economy, and environmental justice to environmental issues and their emergent strategies.
- Formulate and pursue timely research questions about social, environmental, and economic crises and development strategies at local, regional and global scales.
- Orient themselves in emergent environmental issues by researching critical histories of places, regions, industries, economies, and development patterns.
- Communicate about the environment and society to a broader audience.

- ### Academic Integrity

  Student-teacher relationships are built on trust. For example, students must trust that teachers have made appropriate decisions about the structure and content of the courses they teach, and teachers must trust that the assignments that students turn in are their own. Acts that violate this trust undermine the educational process. The Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities and The Graduate Student Supplement define various forms of Academic Dishonesty and you should make yourself familiar with these. In this class, all assignments that are turned in for a grade must represent the student’s own work. In cases where help was received, or teamwork was allowed, a notation on the assignment should indicate your collaboration.

  Violations of academic integrity may also be reported to the appropriate Dean (Dean of Students for undergraduate students or the Dean of Graduate Education for graduate students, respectively).
