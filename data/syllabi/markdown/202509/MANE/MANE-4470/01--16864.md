## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

An introductory course on the principles of radiation protection (health physics) for upper-level undergraduate students. It provides a fundamental understanding of radiation interactions in matter, the biological effects of radiation, internal and external dosimetry, occupational and environmental radiation protection, health physics instrumentation, regulations, waste management, risk assessment, and radiation shielding. This course will help you to understand – and implement – nuclear engineering workflows related to health physics (or radiological engineering) that are needed in research and industry today.  
There are three core concept areas that this course will cover:

- \- Radiation Physics:
  - Underlying physical principles associated with radiation, radioactivity, interactions with matter and the transfer of radiation energy.
- \- Radiation Protection
  - Biology and the effects of radiation interactions, measures and quantification of radiation risk, and the principles of radiation protection.
- \- Applications
  - Integrated systems of radiation protection/mitigation and their regulations. Computational methods for shielding design and issues associated with specific industries such as power reactors, medical facilities and accelerators.

These three core concept areas can be broken down further into the following topics, which will tie into our lecture schedule. Additional class time will be devoted to in-class discussions and presentations.

1. Sources of Radiation
2. Radiation Physics
3. Radiation Kinetics
4. Radiation Transport
5. Radiation Interaction with Matter
6. Radiation Interaction with Living Matter (Radiobiology)
7. Radiation Dosimetry, Safety Standards, and Regulations
8. Radiation Detection
9. Internal Radiation Safety
10. External Radiation Safety

In addition to publicly available computational tools, students will be expected to develop their own computational tools for relevant homework assignments and projects. **For students who are unfamiliar with python, this course may prove very challenging.**

## Learning Outcomes

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes (CLOs):

- Students who complete this course should be able to describe the mechanisms by which radiation interacts with humans.
- Students who complete this course should be able to prepare shielding design for common radiation fields and situations.
- Students who complete this course should be able to understand how radionuclides are incorporated into and affect the body.
- Students who complete this course should be able to calculate and interpret the dose to an individual from exposure to radiation or radioactivity.
- Students who complete this course should be able to choose appropriate radiation detection equipment for different circumstances or situations.
- Students who complete this course should be able to be familiar with the regulatory environment common to Federal and State agencies.
- Students who complete this course should be able to understand the basic components of a radiation protection program.
- Students who complete this course should be able to apply the principles of health physics to various operational areas, including medical, power reactor, research, and accelerator facilities.
- Students who complete this course should be able to design radiation shielding to meet dose limits and other constraints.

### Additional Academic Integrity Course Policy and Penalty Information:

Student-teacher relationships are built on trust. For example, students must trust that teachers have made appropriate decisions about the structure and content of the courses they teach, and teachers must trust that the assignments that students turn in are their own. Acts, which violate this trust, undermine the educational process. The Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities defines various forms of Academic Dishonesty and you should make yourself familiar with these.

In cases where help was received, or teamwork was allowed, a notation on the assignment should indicate your collaboration and, if requested or if you choose to, their individual contributions.

That said, I strongly encourage collaboration in studying and conducting assignments together to maximize your learning. At the end of each collaboration process, students must have their own copy of the deliverable in which you identified the collaboration. Prior to submission, you should review your deliverable to match your own unique style and approach to organization and communication.

Specific to this course, academic dishonesty can take the following forms:

1. Taking an exam with any assistance from another person, regardless of who they are, unless the instructor specifically states that it is a group activity (e.g., a group oral exam).
2. Using any materials (such as a crib sheet or a calculator) that you did not bring for you and you alone to use on an exam, unless given explicit permission by the proctor (e.g., typically for extraordinary circumstances such as the failure of your calculator during an exam, etc., though such assistance should not be expected or relied on).
3. Using any materials (such as a text, extra crib sheets, a laptop, a cell phone, any electronic device – wireless or otherwise, etc.) that are not explicitly permitted during an exam.
4. Interaction with a wireless device (e.g., a cell phone or tablet) during an exam (except when explicitly allowed by the proctor) will be considered an illicit data exchange and will result in a zero for the entire exam.
5. Exiting the exam room without permission, or not returning in a prompt and timely manner when given permission. “Prompt and timely” is at the discretion of the instructional team.
6. Submission of a deliverable that, except for short phrases or properly referenced material, consists of any content not created by and original to you.
7. Indicating someone as “present” (e.g., on an attendance sheet or submitting an in-class exercise) who is not present in class at the time the indication is made.
8. Collaboration is encouraged. However, assisting someone else with work where they intend to turn in the work as if it was done on their own will itself be considered a serious violation of academic honesty. Give credit for any assistance received, and have sufficient content of your own making according to the expectations of the deliverable.

The penalty for submission of an exam that is in violation of this policy is failure of the course and referral to the Dean of Students.

Submission of a deliverable that is in violation of this policy will result in a grade of zero on the assignment for the first offense. If there is a subsequent infraction the student fails the course and is referred to the Dean of Students.

MCNP 6.3 will be used for educational purposes associated with a university nuclear engineering program, in which the instructor is a licensed MCNP user. For specific use, this software will be used to model various Monte Carlo radiation transport scenarios such as multi-particle shielding configurations, dose calculations, and buildup factors determination and it pertains to neutron, and photon shielding for reactor and accelerator applications. Specific examples include determining shielding needed to satisfy regulatory requirements for dose to operators/public during the design of a small modular reactor and the design of a new photonuclear target.
