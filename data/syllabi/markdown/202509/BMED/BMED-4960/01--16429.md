## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

**Description:**

This course provides both an introductory overview and practical project-based experiences in the new field of bioelectronic medicine, in which neural pathways innervating organs are monitored or modulated for diagnostic or therapeutic purposes.

The beginning of the course will cover the predecessor technologies of the new field, including the principles underlying traditional nerve stimulation and recording electrodes and systems within pacemakers, deep brain and spinal cord stimulators. Within this discussion of devices and tools, we will cover and review classical neurophysiology of a peripheral nerve, action potential generation and neural communication, and the difference between spontaneous neural activity and an evoked compound action potential.

This background will prepare students for learning about the origins of the new field, in which other (non-neuromuscular) nerve pathways to peripheral organs have been mapped and then exploited for therapeutic modulation over the last decade. Special attention will be paid to important neuroimmune pathways discovered within the spleen and lymphoid tissues, and neurometabolic pathway within the liver, pancreas, and gastrointestinal tissues. For each of these examples, important non-neuronal synapses will be described between nerve cells and resident immune and endocrine cells. Careful examination of both afferent and efferent nerve functions within these tissues will be discussed, the importance of these pathways in maintaining homeostasis, and dysfunction within the pathways that emerge in chronic disease. New tools for recording and stimulating nerves will be discussed, including non-invasive ultrasonic and electromagnetic devices, and the process by which these tools are tested for clinical use.

## Course Goals

### Goals:

In this course students will apply and integrate the knowledge they have learned in science (e.g., cell and molecular biology through basic physiology) and engineering (e.g., modelling and analysis of uncertainty and programming for BME) to understand the interdisciplinary field of bioelectronic medicine. Homework and exams will be geared toward reviewing and reemphasizing (or reexamining) some of those core skills within this newly introduced biomedical engineering discipline/sub-field. Class projects and evaluations will allow the student to become immersed in the systems level and interdisciplinary thinking that is required for work in the bioelectronic medicine field, and an opportunity for creative application of knowledge (as will be required of them in follow on academic and/or industrial research work). There will also be the opportunity for students to get their first experience in how this interdisciplinary knowledge must be conveyed and refined to write successful research reports and grant applications.

## Course Content

### Content Details:

Students who successfully complete this course will be able to:

1. Demonstrate a firm understanding of how nerves innervate various organs, the neurotransmitters and neuropeptides used to communicate with the end-organs, and the functional dependencies of this communication on end organ function.
2. Both invasive and non-invasive methods of recording from and stimulating peripheral nerve pathways will be discussed, and students will have a firm understanding of the principles behind each method of communication with specific nerve pathways
3. Apply engineering principles to analyze nerve activity data, including use of methods of pre-processing, denoising, spike detection, and feature extraction.
4. Identify major neuroimmune and neurometabolic peripheral nerve pathways, and describe the important role they play in homeostasis, health, and disease.
5. Demonstrate the understanding of the non-traditional/neuron-to-non-neuronal synapses, and the role of supporting cell types (such as satellite glia) on nerve communication

### Additional Academic Integrity Course Policy and Penalty Information:

**Academic Integrity:**

Student-teacher relationships are built on trust. For example, students must trust that teachers have made appropriate decisions about the structure and content of the courses they teach, and teachers must trust that the assignments that students turn in are their own. Acts, which violate this trust, undermine the educational process. The Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities define various forms of Academic Dishonesty and you should make yourself familiar with these. In this class, all assignments that are turned in for a grade must represent the student’s own work. In cases where help was received, or teamwork was allowed, a notation on the assignment should indicate your collaboration. Submission of any assignment that is in violation of this policy will result in a penalty of receiving an F for the course.

If you have any question concerning this policy before submitting an assignment, please ask for clarification.

**Potential Places Where Academic Dishonesty May Take Place In This Course:**

1. Academic Fraud: The alteration of documentation relating to the grading process. For example, changing exam solutions to negotiate for a higher grade or tampering with an instructor’s grade book.
2. Collaboration: Deliberately facilitating an act of academic dishonesty in any way or form. For example, allowing another student to observe an exam paper or allowing another student to “recycle” one’s old term paper or using one another’s work in a paper or lab report without citing it as another’s work.
3. Copying: Obtaining information pertaining to a graded exercise by deliberately observing the paper of another student.
4. Cribbing: Use or attempted use of prohibited materials, information, or study aids in an academic exercise. For example, using an unauthorized formal sheet during an exam.
5. Fabrication: Unauthorized falsification or invention of any information in an academic exercise. For example, use of “bought or “ready-made” term papers, or falsifying lab records or reports.
6. Plagiarism: Representing the work or words of another as one’s own through the omission of acknowledgment or reference. For example, using sentences verbatim from a published source in a term paper without appropriate referencing, or presenting as one’s own the detailed argument of a published source, or presenting as one’s own electronically or digitally enhanced graphic representations from any form of media.
7. Sabotage: Destruction of another student’s work. For example, destroying a model, lab experiment, computer program, or term paper developed by another student.
8. Substitution: Utilizing a proxy, or acting as a proxy, in any academic exercise. For example, taking an exam for another student or having a homework assignment done by someone else.

There is a zero-tolerance policy concerning academic dishonesty. Any of the above or other instances where I believe academic dishonesty has occurred will result in the student receiving an F in this course. Those students who I believe are guilty of academic dishonesty will be reported to the Dean of Student’s Office.
