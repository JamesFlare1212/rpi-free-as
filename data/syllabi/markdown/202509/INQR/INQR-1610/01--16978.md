## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

Studio I is the first course in the Design, Innovation, and Society introductory studio sequence. The course offers ways of thinking about design in the world and its relationship with society, including questions of class, gender, race, and disability. Through readings, discussions, diverse assignments (such as writing, sketching, and skill-sharing), and sprint design projects, students will be introduced to fundamental design concepts and ideas. They will be able to articulate an initial understanding of their personal identities as designers. Key concepts introduced: design processes, problem definition, concept ideation and selection, concept sketching and visualization, and low-fidelity prototyping.

## Course Goals

### Goals:

Studio I is an introductory course in a series of three, scaffolded, design studios (that means the learning outcomes build on one another in that sequence). I do not expect that you will have anything except personal experience with and/or interest in design ideas, nor do I expect that you will be able to articulate how design and society interact, what key concepts of design are, what design processes look like, or to be a star artist, conceptual thinker, or prototype builder. You are here to begin to learn these skills. Most of the assigned readings will be in the “academic register,” and if you’re not used to reading, synthesizing, and analyzing scholarly articles, some discussions and assignments might feel challenging. That’s okay! These are skills and knowledge that we will build up throughout the semester, as well as in the rest of the studio sequence, including Studios II and III. Be sure to read directions closely and seek additional assistance during my office hours, in the lead-up to assignment deadlines. What I do expect, however, is that you treat every peer, instructor, and concept we encounter with respect and curiosity. You are encouraged to ask questions and clarifications on issues while being aware of where these questions may be coming from. We will be discussing design and technology from perspectives you might not yet be familiar with, and a lack of familiarity often brings discomfort.

Although discomfort can make conversations difficult and stilted, we will work together to create a safe and inclusive classroom and campus environment for all individuals. Norms and languages change over time and are often culturally and locally bound. We have a duty as participants in a shared public world to think and speak carefully. Mistakes can and will be made in all human interactions, and while we may want to explain intentions when we slip, we live in a world where perceptions and unintended effects do matter.

I also expect you to come to class prepared, pay close attention to the details in this syllabus, and be aware of what each week holds. Assignments vary, and projects will start and end quickly. Please take a careful read of this syllabus and keep it easily accessible for reference throughout the semester.

## Learning Outcomes

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes (CLOs):

- Successfully naming and using key steps of design processes to design and re-design artifacts and create low-resolution prototypes of these products. This includes the ability to sketch concept drawings for design pin-ups and the use of paper, cardboard, and scavenged materials for prototypes.
- Communicating effectively via written (reports), oral (presentations), and visual (sketches) means the decisions and reasoning behind each step taken in the design process.
- Articulating the definition and significance of design concepts, including aesthetics, form, function, prototype, etc.
- Producing written work that accurately summarizes and dialogues with introductory concepts from the field of Science & Technology Studies to discuss the intersections between design and society.
- Participating in collegial academic discussions and critique, having learned to provide feedback for their peers and to accept and use critique addressed to them about the work they produce during the semester.

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes Assessment Measures:

Sketchbook:

Through the course, you will have to submit two kinds of sketchbook assigments: In-class Sketches and Project Sketches. In other words, in addition to the six sketch assigments linked to the three design projects of the course, you have four additional in-class sketches due for the semester, for a total of 10 sketch exercises in the semester. Each sketching assignment is worth one point.

These sketches are a way to exercise your observation and visual representation skills with artifacts. Sketching is a practice like any other, and engaging with it daily, or at the very least weekly, will help you flex your design muscles. These sketches are graded on a pass-fail basis. If you complete all 10 sketches (the six linked to projects, described above, plus the additional four here), you will receive full marks for these.

Object timeline (due Sep 18)

This is an individual assignment. Each member of the team will create a timeline of your artifact, illustrating its evolution over the years (decades, perhaps even centuries).

You should have at least eight representations of the artifact in your timeline, but it is up to you when your timeline begins and ends! Each team member will focus on a specific span of years to avoid overlap.

Your object timeline is worth 5 points. This assignment is not graded on artistic ability (e.g., “how good” of an artist you are). It is graded on your ability to convey visual information and thinking through visual layouts.

This is a pass/fail/excellent assignment. You will receive zero if it is not submitted, 3 points if it is submitted with minimal effort in presentation, or 5 points if effort was applied.

Object’s Supply Chain Manifest Visualization and its Documentation (due Sept 22)

This is a collaborative mapping project and a space for collective reflection. On Monday, September 15 (week 4), you will participate in a tutorial on how to use Manifest, an open-source tool for mapping supply chains. In preparation for this tutorial, research the supply chain of your object at three different points in your timeline and bring at least 90 data points (30 for each moment visualized) to class.

On Monday, September 22, you will have time during class to work on producing the documentation for others to reproduce your design process. This includes organizational charts, lists of materials and supplies, step-by-step instructions, protocols, visual representations, and FAQs. You will submit the assignment (supply chain visualization and documentation) at the end of the session.

This assignment is worth 10 points. It is not graded on artistic ability (e.g., “how good” of an artist you are). Instead, it is graded on your ability to convey visual information and think through visual layouts.

This is a pass/fail/excellent assignment. You will receive zero if it is not submitted, 3 points if submitted with minimal effort in presentation, or 5 points if effort is demonstrated.

Final Presentation and Pin-Up: Useless Redesign (due Oct 2nd)

This is a collaborative assignment. With your team, you will produce a final presentation and your first design pin-up that presents your artifact redesign. Your design pin-up is a well-polished visual representation of your design. You should create it on paper and ink, not pencil (I recommend making rough sketches in your personal sketchbook to prepare for the pin-up).

As a sketching exercise, the success of this is not judged through your artistic talent, but rather on the time and craft you put into this pin-up, and how you have thought through visual communication in the layout of the sketches.

Your pin-up is graded out of 5 points, based on quality of visual communication (2 points), effective use of space (2 points), and tidiness of work (1 point). Each pin-up should have anywhere between 5 and 9 renderings of your redesigned artifact. These redesign representations should be shown from different angles, but can be presented in either orthographic and/or perspectival views, and should communicate use, highlighting both general lines and details of the design. There should be minimal text use, with any text use being limited to information and specifications that cannot otherwise be captured on paper (e.g., specific types of materials). Color can be used strategically to communicate the product's goal and make it visually appealing.

Your presentation is graded on a scale of 5 points. The presentation should last approximately 10 minutes.

The presentation should cover

1\) A brief history of the artifact you're redesigning

2\) Your ideation process regarding how to make it useless (are you making it useless to someone in particular?)

3\) What constraints or affordances have been introduced to this new redesign?  
Observation Notes (due Oct 16 before class)

This is an individual assignment. For the first week of this project, you will keep observation notes. Watch out for disruptions to your day and routine: uncomfortable objects, interfaces that create friction, and artifacts that you wish were otherwise.

At the end of the week, you’ll submit five entries of polished notes (i.e., you’ve converted your notes from the quick jots you maybe taken “out in the field”). Each entry should be about 200 words long. I’m expecting you to describe the instances of frustration you encounter with objects around you: what are the qualities of that frustration? Texture? Function? Fiddly? Unsatisfying? Etc. For whom was it so frustrating? Why for them/you in particular, what is the process of “misfitting” that is occurring here? You need to have this assignment completed before class on Oct 12, as we will use it to identify your partners.  
Redesign Pin-up + Presentation (due Oct 27 or Oct 30)

This presentation consists of the pin-up of the ultimately selected artifact for this project. All the same rules regarding pin-up, as detailed in Project 1, apply. Your pin-up is graded out of 5 points, based on quality of visual communication (3 points), effective use of space (1 point), and tidiness of work (1 point).

Your presentation is graded on a scale of 5 points. The presentation should be no longer than 10 minutes in length. The presentation should cover: 1) What object you’re redesigning; 2) For whom you’re redesigning it; 3) Why you are redesigning it; 4) How you’re redesigning it (describe affordances and constraints); 5) How you intend to test the prototype.

Design Report + Prototype (due Nov 6)

This will be your first go at a design report. It is graded out of 7. Each design report contains:

- A title page that gives the name of the redesigned product, the names of group members (or individual), an image of the idealized product, and a short abstract that describes the artifact in three or four sentences.

- Judicious use of visual images: your sketches, your pin-up, and good-quality pictures of your prototype. These should support and illustrate the information provided in your report.

- An introduction that lays out what you will be discussing in the report.

- A design process statement: an 800-word statement that describes your design process in thinking about the project. The design process should include references to problem definition, ideation and concept selection, and prototyping.

- A user statement: This should be a 500-word discussion wherein you are identifying who your user group is (in this case, likely to be you). You should be as specific as possible in this discussion, focusing on what makes the needs of this specific user different from other groups that therefore demand this redesign. What makes them different not only from other obviously different groups, but also from groups that are similar. How you are responding to these needs with this specific redesign should be discussed. Beware of stereotypes, be critical in your initial assumptions about specific user groups and work towards refined, polished discussions.

- Next steps: In taking this product forward, how would begin thinking about building and testing the artifact? What have you learned from the first prototype you have made, and how would you test it further?

- A brief conclusion that summarizes what you have accomplished in this project.

Your prototype is graded on a scale of 3 points. It should be done in either paper or cardboard or a mix of both. No other materials are permitted for this first attempt at prototyping. It is graded on the following: 1) Whether it follows the material requirements; 2) Whether it matches the description of prototyping described in the report (e.g., you say you are testing a mechanism, I need to be able to test that mechanism); 3) Creativity in and quality of assemblage.

Your final project of the semester is your first “free rein” where you design an entirely new object. You will be working in small groups (you can indicate preferences, but I will get the final say in the group formations). You will be putting together all the sections of the design process you have learned this semester: problem definition, ideation, concept selection, and prototyping. The theme for this project is simply “The Future”. We will be integrating design concepts that go beyond many standard design classes, namely the concept of speculative design, through which you can practice important skills (such as defining a problem and ideating solutions) while using design as a means of exploring questions about the future.

When designing for the future, consider several key factors:

- What does the future hold, and what ‘future’ is it? You will get to “design” your own parameters of the future!
- For whose future will you be designing?
- Who are you as a designer in the future?

Essay: Designing the future (due Nov 20)

This is a definition essay, written collaboratively with your group mates. Following our discussion on how one’s vision of ‘disaster’ or ‘apocalypse’ might already be another person’s reality, you are to write a 1500-word essay discussing the following topic:

How does your design project relate to real-world situations?

You are expected to discuss, 1) what your definition of the future is for this project (and why, what reasons lead you to believe that this is what the future holds), 2) for whom you are intending to design and why them, what problems will surface for that demographic in this speculative future, and 3) how this ‘future’ might already exist in the world. For the third point, you should provide at least three academic, peer-reviewed sources that discuss this real-world situation (this will serve you well for defining your problem space and providing design inspiration!).

Pin-Up + Presentations (due Dec 1st)

This presentation consists of the pin-up of the ultimately selected artifact for this project. All the same rules regarding pin-up, as detailed in Project 1, apply.

Your pin-up is graded out of 5 points, based on quality of visual communication (3 points), effective use of space (1 point), and tidiness of work (1 point).

This pin-up needs to be scanned, reworked, and refined in Adobe Photoshop, following the tutorial provided by Professor Jim Malazita. It is expected that you will integrate this digitized version of your pin-up into your presentation (detailed below). Your presentation is graded on a scale of 5 points.

The presentation should last approximately 10 minutes. In your presentation, I expect you to cover: 1) A brief overview of your future; 2) A discussion of who in that future you are designing for, 3) The problem definition for your project; 4) The ideation and concept selection process that brought you to the proposed pin-up; 5) What your team will be doing for the prototyping stage.

Final Report + Prototype (due Dec 8th & 11th)

For more information on what is expected in your final report, see the Project 2 Design Report entry above. Your final prototype for this project may incorporate any scavenged materials (you may not buy anything for it!). The exact breakdown of points applies to this project as it does to Project 2.

Engagement

As mentioned in our class agreements above, your Engagement grade is not merely an attendance grade. It constitutes 10 points of your grade because I expect you to attend and actively engage with the material. To succeed in this class requires diligence in participation and engagement. It doesn’t only mean “speak” when we are conducting our synchronous discussions; it also means actively contributing to our space in Blackboard by sharing your thoughts on readings and uploading homework assignments in a timely manner. You will be graded on a scale for engagement, detailed below. Two points of your engagement grade are awarded for coming to office hours at least once in the semester.

- Excellent (10): You have come to office hours at least once. You consistently contribute to class, referencing readings, past class discussions, and knowledge you bring from other spaces. You are respectful and encourage your colleagues to participate, asking them clarifying and engaging questions, and providing feedback on others’ projects. You submit weekly homework diligently and in a timely fashion.
- Good (8): You have come to office hours at least once. You are present and prepared for class and engage sporadically with some references to readings and past class discussions. You provide some feedback to your colleagues’ projects, but could engage in our conversations more actively. You mostly submit weekly homework in a timely fashion.
- Adequate (6): You have come to office hours at least once. You are present and prepared for class, but your contributions are limited, with questions and comments often confined to clarifications or superficial observations about the readings. You rarely engage in Q&A for your colleagues’ presentations. You are inconsistent in submitting your weekly homework.
- Minimal (3): You have come to office hours at least once. However, you are often unprepared for class or do not contribute, or contribute with single comments in discussions (i.e., “I agree with them.) Your weekly homework is rarely submitted or is done sloppily.
- None (0): You have not been present during office hours, are unprepared for class, and do not contribute to discussions. Your weekly homework is rarely done.
