## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

This is an undergraduate course that will explore research findings on canine cognition and the application of these findings to dog training, behavior modification, and creating a positive dog-human bond.

## Course Goals

### Goals:

Students will learn about the methods, challenges, and limitations of studying canine cognition and behavior. They will understand models of canine cognition deriving from the application of research methods. And they will understand how scientific findings can be applied to improving the human-dog bond, especially through science-based training.

## Course Content

### Content Details:

Topics include, non-exhaustively: Learning theory; training philosophies; communication and body language; fear, stress and anxiety; emotions; consent; aggression; multidog management; punishment; domestication; early development (puppies); olfaction; play; breed differences; olfaction; dopamine; MRI studies of dogs; oxytocin; problem solving; and models and visualizations used to explain dog behavior.

## Learning Outcomes

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes (CLOs):

- Learning outcomes related to course content include: understanding the methods, challenges, and limitations of studying canine cognition and behavior; understanding models of canine cognition deriving from the application of research methods; and understanding how scientific findings can be applied to improving the human-dog bond, especially through science-based training.
- Learning outcome #1 related to the CI requirement: Students will understand linguistic and psychological principles of effective communication; they will learn associated linguistic terminology; and they will be able to demonstrate their learning on exams.
- Learning outcome #2 related to the CI requirement: Students will be able to analyze specific communication situations, such as: (a) how authors of primary literature (specifically, about canine cognition and dog training) write for their peers; (b) how professional writings can be simplified for non-professional audiences; (c) how non-professionals can skim professional texts with utility; and (d) how professionals can teach non-professionals using videos, written pieces, and visual models.
- Learning outcome #3 related to the CI requirement: (a) Students will be able to communicate effectively in more than one course-relevant genre, including: (a) asking and answering questions about the lectures and readings; (b) writing short, analytical pieces with iterations; (c) creating visual models of select aspects of course content; (d) discussing course content in small groups and collectively composing presentable summaries; (e) orally presenting analyses; (f) simplifying passages of the primary literature for a non-professional audience.
- Learning outcome #4 related to CI content: Students will be able to write clearly and succinctly. Most written assignments will be one or two paragraphs. The focus will be on formulating a compelling thesis and supporting it in several well-crafted, logically organized sentences. Students will not be permitted to use AI-based writing tools on any assignments. They will revise submissions until they have reached an acceptable quality threshold.

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes Assessment Measures:

- Class activities will count for 30% of the grade, fulfilling the CI requirements of the course. Students must be present in class to carry out these activities; they cannot be made up at home. It is expected that there will be around a dozen such activities. Examples include: writing paragraphs by hand in class; participating in class discussions that analyze writing; creating a graphical model of some topic or information; simplifying academic prose to make it more understandable; and participating in small-group discussions followed by orally presenting key points to the rest of the class.
- There will also be 3 exams worth 25%, 25% and 20% of the grade, respectively.
- Exams will not be cumulative and there will be no final exam.
- Most exam questions will be super-short-answer questions, defined as T/F, multiple choice, or matching. Students will not be required to recall (i.e., pull out memory) vocabulary, though they will need to be able to recognize it.
- At the end of the semester final grades will be assigned as follows: A *= 92 or higher; A- = 91-89; B+ = 88-86;B = 85-83; B- = 82-79; C+ = 78-76; C = 75-73; C- = 72-69; D+ = 68-66; D = 65-61; F = 60 or lower.*

## Policies

### Attendance Policy:

- Students are expected to attend every class meeting.
- Many class meetings (around a dozen) will involve graded work. If you’re not there, you will get a 0 for the work--though you will have one free miss for a class assignment (not an exam). You will not be permitted to make up classwork outside of class. Why? ChatGPT and its cousins.
- If you are sick on the day of an exam or graded class assignment, please go to the medical center to get an excuse. If you do not have an official excuse, you will get a zero with no option for a make-up.
- If you anticipate not being able to attend an exam for some other officially excused reason, please let me know at least 2 weeks in advance so that I have time to reschedule the exam for everyone.
- If you miss an exam due to an officially excused illness or other excused reason, you will take it together with the next scheduled exam. The exams are not that long, you'll have plenty of time.

### Other Course Policies:

- To be marked present for class, you need to attend the entire class period.
- The classroom will be a technology-free zone unless otherwise instructed. This keeps students from distracting themselves and everyone around them, including me.
- You should always bring paper, scratch paper, a pen, a pencil with eraser, and your computer to class. We will sometimes be using computers for class exercises.
- You must keep a log of study time if you plan to come to office hours or if you anticipate that you might not be satisfied with your grade. Without a history of *what* you have studied, *how*, and *for how long*, we have nothing to do discuss. (Office hours can’t be used as a replacement for making yourself concentrate on your own.)
- I do not consider “going over my notes” to be serious study time. You need to do something active when studying. I will give you tips the first day of class.
- Please schedule your study time so that you can ask me questions during the class meeting prior to each exam.
- You may drink from non-spill containers. No eating, please.
- If I am late to class, please wait for 30 min., then you can leave. You can use technology during that time. If you leave before 30 min. you will be counted absent.
- All work must be done independently unless otherwise announced. You may not consult the internet, LLMs, any other AI tools, or other people for help or to look up answers.
- A standing homework assignment is to study the materials presented in class in preparation for the next exam. Readings and problem sets might also count as homework assignments.
- You should not plan travel so that you miss any classes because the exam dates are not set in stone.

- ### Academic Integrity

  Student-teacher relationships are built on trust. For example, students must trust that teachers have made appropriate decisions about the structure and content of the courses they teach, and teachers must trust that the assignments that students turn in are their own. Acts that violate this trust undermine the educational process. The Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities and The Graduate Student Supplement define various forms of Academic Dishonesty and you should make yourself familiar with these.
