## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

*This laboratory course reiterates the important concepts learned in the Physics of Light class and provides hands-on experience to instruct students in the use of different types of light meters and laboratory instruments.*

*This course continues instruction in the various theories and laws of the physics of light Learning the following two topics is important for students enrolled in the MS in Architectural Science degree with a concentration in lighting.*

1. *Radiometry is the science of the measurement of radiant energy and photometry is the science of the measurement of light as perceived by the human eye. This laboratory class concentrates on the measurement aspects and teaches the different instruments and laboratory measurement equipment that are necessary to quantify electromagnetic radiation and light. Furthermore, it teaches industry recommended and best laboratory practices.*
2. *Optics is an important topic that studies the behavior and properties of light. Learning the science of how light moves from one place to another via different media and its behavior when it interacts with objects is fundamental for the application of light for general illumination. An important aspect of this is the understanding the optical elements needed to redirect light. As with most lighting concepts, successfully learning these concepts is mostly achieved though observation and experimentation in a laboratory or realistic setting.*

*This is the only course within the School of Architecture that teaches these above concepts for architectural students who need to understand and measure lighting in the built environment.*

*Even though optics and radiation may be topics taught in the Physics department, the application of the theory and quantification of light required in architecture are not taught. There is no other course at RPI that teaches these topics in the context of built environment and its occupants.*

*The required qualified faculty, staff, facilities, and instruments needed for this laboratory course are available at the School of Architecture’s Lighting Research Center.*

## Course Goals

### Goals:

The primary objective of this course is to provide the students with basic understanding and hands-on measurements involved in the Physics of light, to introduce some of the important concepts in lighting, and addresses the science of measuring visible light.

## Learning Outcomes

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes (CLOs):

- integrate theory and practice through the operation of experiments and through the acquisition and use of experimental data
- take accurate and meaningful lighting measurements and perform experiments and report results in a professional manner
- conduct uncertainty analysis for quantified experiment data and information. apply quantified experiment data and information in the design of luminaires and architectural lighting systems
- apply their knowledge of photometric quantities and measurement in the performance of lighting research
- apply their knowledge of colorimetry to lighting research, design, and evaluation
- independently design and conduct experimentation, data collection and analysis to synthesize the concepts and outcomes learned in the theory and laboratory classes

### Additional Academic Integrity Course Policy and Penalty Information:

***Academic integrity:*** *(Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights & Responsibilities, Spring 2025)*

*Intellectual integrity and credibility are the foundation of all academic work.* *A violation of Academic Integrity policy* *is, by definition, considered a flagrant offense to the educational process. It is taken seriously by students, faculty, and Rensselaer and will be addressed in an effective manner.* *If found responsible for committing academic dishonesty, a student may be subject to one or both types of penalties: an academic (grade) penalty administered by the professor and/or disciplinary action through the Rensselaer judicial process described in this handbook.* *Academic dishonesty is a violation of the Grounds for Disciplinary Action as described in this handbook.*

*A student may be subject to any of the following types of disciplinary action should disciplinary action be pursued by the professor: disciplinary warning; disciplinary probation; disciplinary suspension, expulsion and/or alternative actions as agreed on by the student and hearing officer. It should be noted that no student who allegedly commits academic dishonesty will be able to drop or change the grade option for the course in question and is not eligible to have the opportunity to sit for an “F Examination” for the course.*

*The definitions and examples presented below are a sampling of types of academic dishonesty and are not to be construed as an exhaustive or exclusive list. The academic integrity policy applies to all students, undergraduate and graduate, and to scholarly pursuits and research. Additionally, attempts to commit academic dishonesty or to assist in the commission or attempt of such an act are also violations of this policy.*

***Academic Fraud***

*Academic Fraud is defined as the alteration of documentation relating to the grading process; for example, changing exam solutions to negotiate for a higher grade or tampering with an instructor's grade book.*

***Collaboration***

*Collaboration is defined as deliberately facilitating an act of academic dishonesty in any way or form; for example, allowing another student to observe an exam paper or allowing another student to "recycle" one's old term paper or using one another's work in a paper or lab report without citing it as another's work.*

***Copying***

*Copying is defined as obtaining information pertaining to a graded exercise by deliberately observing the paper of another student; for example, noting which alternative a neighboring student has circled on a multiple-choice*

*exam.*

***Cribbing***

*Cribbing is defined as the use or attempted use of prohibited materials, information, or study aids in an academic exercise. Examples include using an unauthorized formal sheet during an exam*

***Fabrication***

*Fabrication is defined as the unauthorized falsification or invention of any information in an academic exercise. Examples include the use of "bought" or "ready-made" term papers, or falsifying lab records or reports.*

***Plagiarism***

*Plagiarism is defined as representing the work or words of another as one's own through the omission of acknowledgment or reference. Examples include using sentences verbatim from a published source in a term paper without appropriate referencing, or presenting as one's own the detailed argument of a published source, or presenting as one's own electronically or digitally enhanced graphic representations from any form of media*

***Sabotage***

*Sabotage is defined as the destruction of another student's work. Examples include destroying a model, lab experiment, computer program, or term paper developed by another student.*

***Substitution***

*\Substitution is defined as utilizing a proxy, or acting as a proxy, in any academic exercise. Examples include taking an exam for another student or having a homework assignment done by someone else.*
