## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

## This course introduces experimental methods in economics and related social sciences, with a focus on choice behavior experiments, field experiments, and survey methods. It also covers econometric techniques commonly applied to experimental data. Students gain a comprehensive understanding of the research landscape in experimental and behavioral economics. By the end of the course, students will have the skills and knowledge required to design their own experiments and conduct high-quality research using experimental methods.

## Learning Outcomes

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes (CLOs):

- Identify and understand the elements of experimental design
- Analyze and critique an experimental design
- Analyze human motivation and behavior across different economic paradigms
- Evaluate existing work on selected topics in behavioral and experimental economics
- Develop a research question, review the literature, design an experiment, and write a grant proposal

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes Assessment Measures:

## Course Assessment Measures

***Leading Paper Discussion:***

- On each paper discussion day, we will discuss two papers. Each discussion will be led by one student (the discussion leader). Every student will serve as discussion leader twice during the course.
- Each student will submit a list of at least six potential papers. From this list, I will select two papers for you to lead. Selected papers should primarily rely on primary datasets such as incentivized economics experiments (lab or field) or survey experiments. Papers must be published in reputable, peer-reviewed journals in economics, management, or closely related social sciences.
- As a discussion leader, you are expected to read your assigned paper thoroughly—including appendices and experimental instructions, if available—and come prepared to lead an in-depth discussion in class. Leaders should prepare a concise set of slides that summarize the motivation, research questions, methodology, findings, and contributions. In addition, leaders should develop a list of thoughtful questions to guide class discussion and actively engage their peers in conversation.
- In addition to preparing their assigned paper, leaders are also expected to read the second paper scheduled for that day and contribute meaningfully to its discussion.

**Grading:** Your grade will be based on (i) the depth of your understanding of the paper and (ii) your ability to foster an active, engaging, and inclusive discussion environment.

***Discussion Participation:***

- On each paper discussion day, we will discuss two papers in class. All students are responsible for reading both papers in advance.
- Students are expected to take an active role in class discussions by asking the discussion leader questions, offering thoughtful comments, and sharing their perspectives on the readings.

**Grading**: Your grade will be determined by the quality of your participation in the discussion of both papers. High-quality participation includes demonstrating careful reading of the papers, engaging respectfully with peers, and contributing in ways that enrich the conversation.

***Data Analysis Replication Report:***

- Each student will find an experimental paper published in a high-ranking economics (or closely related) journal where the data used in the paper has been posted online. Then students will replicate the **main** **tables** (must have at least 3) in the paper using the original data. The purpose of this practice is to get a better understanding of how experimental data is structured and analyzed.
- Students will then submit their R or Stata code along with the tables they produced (as a Microsoft word document). Discuss any discrepancies you might have encountered.
- *Some eligible journals: American Economic Review, Quarterly Journal of Economics, Journal of Political Economy, The Review of Economics and Statistics, The Review of Economic Studies, Journal of the European Economic Association, The Economic Journal, European Economic Review, AEJ: Applied Micro, AEJ: Economic Policy, AEJ: Microeconomics, Experimental Economics, Games and Economic Behavior, Journal of Economic Behavior and Organization, Management Science*

***Call for Proposals:***

- Students will identify at least three grant opportunities or calls for proposals that support research in economics, management, or related fields.
- Students must enter the relevant information into the shared Google spreadsheet provided for the course.

***Research Idea Submissions and Discussion:***

- Each student will develop a research idea and submit the following components (as a word document):
  1. A short paragraph on the motivation (Why is this important? Why should we care?)
  2. A clearly articulated research question(s)
  3. A preliminary plan describing how the research question could be investigated
- On the dates indicated in the course calendar, students will email me their research ideas by class start time. Then, the first half of class will be dedicated to discussing these ideas.
  1. Each student will have 5 minutes to present their idea.
  2. Students may use slides, but this is optional.
  3. Following each presentation, we will spend approximately 5 minutes providing feedback and discussion.
  4. Students are expected to take this feedback seriously and thoughtfully consider it in refining their research ideas.

***Grant Proposal:***

- Each student will select one of the grant opportunities identified earlier in the semester. Students may choose any grant from the compiled list.
- Students will then prepare a full research proposal. The proposal should be written clearly and concisely, and it must include the following components (there may be more, see my note at the end of this section):

1. **Project Summary** (1 page)
   - The Project Summary must not be an abstract — it needs to be a stand‑alone, compelling narrative that encapsulates the proposal's broader vision (following the NSF guidelines)
   - The audience includes both specialists and a broader, scientifically literate but non‑expert reader.
   - Project Summary composed of three separate sections, each clearly labeled and distinct:
     1. **Overview**
        1. Briefly describes what the project will accomplish if funded.
        2. Includes the objectives and the methods to be employed.

- Should not merely echo the full proposal, but offer a compelling “elevator pitch” of the work.

1. **Intellectual Merit**
   1. Explains how the proposed research will advance knowledge within its field and possibly beyond.
   2. Highlights originality, potential to transform the field, and significant contributions.
2. **Broader Impacts**
   1. Describes potential benefits of the research to society at large, beyond academia. Could include improvements in education, workforce diversity, public engagement, policy, or societal well‑being.

2. **Introduction (≈ 2–3 pages)**
   - Motivation: why is this an important and timely question?
   - Comprehensive Literature Review: what is already known in the literature?
   - Contribution: how will this project add to existing knowledge?
   - Broader Impacts: how will it contribute to policy, practice, or society more generally?

3. **Research Question(s)**
   - State the primary research question(s) clearly and directly.
   - If relevant, include secondary or exploratory questions.

4. **Experimental Design (≈ 2–3 pages)**
   - Provide detailed information on the experimental design
   - Explain how you will run the experiment and collect your data (think about your outcome variables and control variables separately and explain how you will measure them).
   - Discuss any potential issues/challenges you may expect to encounter and how you plan to overcome them

5. **Data Analysis Plan (≈ 1 page)**
   - Identify your primary and secondary outcome variables
   - Specify the statistical methods you plan to use.
   - Clarify how you will test hypotheses and interpret results.
   - Address potential data challenges (e.g., missing values, attrition, robustness checks, quality checks etc).

6. **Timeline**
   - A realistic schedule for conducting the study (e.g., programming, pilots, recruitment, data collection, analysis, writing).

7. **Budget (≈ 1 page)**
   - Provide a clear breakdown of expected costs (e.g., participant payments, survey administration, software, personnel, travel).
   - Justify why each expense is necessary for the project.

**Please Note**: Although each proposal must include the above items, the proposals must also be customized for the specific grant you are applying for. You should read the call for proposals very carefully and ensure that you include **all required components** specific to that grant.

- **Formatting Guidelines:** All proposals must adhere to the following formatting rules: Times New Roman, 12-point, single-spaced text, 2-inch margins on all sides, pages are numbered, a consistent citation style (APA, Chicago, or another standard style). ***Submit as a Word document (.docx)!***

**Grading Criteria:** Your grade will be based on the following components:

- *Clarity and Structure (10%):* Overall writing quality, organization, and professionalism of the proposal.
- *Adherence to Guidelines (10%):* Following the specified grant proposal requirements.
- *Significance of Research Questions (25%):* Importance, originality, and potential contribution of the research questions.
- *Experimental Design (25%):* Soundness, rigor, and feasibility of the proposed methodology.
- *Overall Quality of the Proposal (20%):* Strength of the literature review, data analysis plan, broader impacts, budget, and any other required components.
- *Presentation (10%):* clarity and effectiveness of your presentation

- ### Academic Integrity

  Student-teacher relationships are built on trust. For example, students must trust that teachers have made appropriate decisions about the structure and content of the courses they teach, and teachers must trust that the assignments that students turn in are their own. Acts that violate this trust undermine the educational process. The Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities and The Graduate Student Supplement define various forms of Academic Dishonesty and you should make yourself familiar with these. In this class, all assignments that are turned in for a grade must represent the student’s own work.
