## Course Goals

### Goals:

1. Students will develop fluency in software platforms and the basic programming necessary to develop and visualize digital models with CAD modeling and data management, through a series of tutorials and practical exercises.
2. Students will simulate and analyze architectural geometry with site specific conditions by linking design criteria to environmental data sets through parametric modeling and computer simulation.
3. Students will develop comprehension in aspects of architecture and ecology through the parametric manipulation of building morphology, the analysis of monitored climatic data sets, and the application of principles of sustainable design.
4. Students will gain fluency in the discourse of digital architectural modelling by exploring the potential of parametric computer modeling and data driven design.

## Course Content

### Content Details:

## Schedule & Topics

Weeks 1, 2, 3 - Introduction to Parametrics

Parametric Modeling for CAD, User Interface, Parametric Geometry

Introduction to parametric modeling and information modeling and imbedding data in CAD geometry.

Data types:

- Primitives: int, double, string, boolean
- Vectors: point, vector, plane
- Wires: line, polyline, rectangle, polygon, arc, circle, curve

Assignment #1: Algorithms in Nature

List & Data Management - Order list maintenance for parametric linking.

- Lists - indicides
- Data trees - paths

Geometric Transformations

- Euclidean: mirror, move, rotate, orient
- Affine: scale, project
- Morph: surface, box, twisted box, map to surface
- Assignment #2: Parametric Tiles

Nurbs v. Mesh v. Subdivision Surface - Introduce the fundamental differences between the 3 major types of modeling methods.

- Surface
- Mesh
- Assignment #3: Geometry Mapping of Tiles in Fields

Weeks 4, 5 - Data MappingScripted and Parametric Curves and Surfaces – methods to plot points, planar curves, and surfaces.

Responsive Patterning & Data Fields – create patterns or objects that will interact / respond to other objects or vectors.

Advanced Mesh and Tiles – populate multiple mesh components based on the data embedded in mesh surface.

- Assignment #4: Attractors on facade

Data Driven Form – embedding external data into rhino geometry.

- Assignment #5: Data driven modules

Week 6 - Simple optimization

Optimization - understanding the basic concepts of inputs (constants and variables), fitness functions, and goals.

- Assignment #6: Optimization: FAR

Week 7 - Multi Fitness Optimization

Multi Fitness Function Optimization - utilizing weighting in fitness functions to satisfy multiple objectives in a design

- Assignment #7a: FAR & Sky exposure plane.

- Assignment #7b: Weather Data & Context - Diagram Industry City

Week 8 - Climate Metrics

Climate Data – types of weather files and included data points.

Sun path diagram

Parametric Solar Simulation – implement your own solar position algorithm to effectively integrate design and analysis.

- Assignment #8: Daylighting & Shadow Studies
- Assignment #10: Optimization - Final assignment

Weeks 9, 10 - Environmental Parametrics

Data Visualization– methods to plot points as tools for performance visualization.

Parametric Climate Analysis – Generation of climate analysis tools for design decision making.

Climate Data Profiles – Ordering climatic data points as inputs for parametric linkage.

- Assignment #9: Daylighting Interior

Weeks 11, 12, 13 - Simple Energy Modeling3

Energy Modeling – Introduction to architectural energy modeling.

Materials – Applying material properties and proper pre-simulation model setup.

- Assignment #11: Final assignment cont. Optimization Diagrams

Constructions – Adiabatic surfaces, shading surfaces and zonal analysis.

Weeks 14, 15 - Design Optimization3

Daylighting Design Optimization – Iterative daylighting optimization setup using evolutionary solver.

- Assignment #12: Final assignment cont. 3D models

Prototyping – Model and file setup for fabrication and digital prototyping.

Assignment #13: Final assignment cont.

- ### Academic Integrity

  Student-teacher relationships are built on trust. For example, students must trust that teachers have made appropriate decisions about the structure and content of the courses they teach, and teachers must trust that the assignments that students turn in are their own. Acts that violate this trust undermine the educational process. The Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities and The Graduate Student Supplement define various forms of Academic Dishonesty and you should make yourself familiar with these. In this class, all assignments that are turned in for a grade must represent the student’s own work. In cases where help was received, or teamwork was allowed, a notation on the assignment should indicate your collaboration.

  If you have any question concerning this policy before submitting an assignment, please ask for clarification. In addition, you can visit the following site for more information on our Academic Integrity Policy: [Students Rights, Responsibilities, and Judicial Affairs](https://info.rpi.edu/dean-students/student-rights-responsibilities-and-judicial-affairs).

### Additional Academic Integrity Course Policy and Penalty Information:

The following statement regarding academic dishonesty is taken verbatim from (2014-2016 Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights & Responsibilities, August 2014).

Intellectual integrity and credibility are the foundation of all academic work. A violation of Academic Integrity policy is, by definition, considered a flagrant offense to the educational process. It is taken seriously by students, faculty, and Rensselaer and will be addressed in an effective manner.

If found responsible for committing academic dishonesty, a student may be subject to one or both types of penalties: an academic (grade) penalty administered by the professor and/or disciplinary action through the Rensselaer judicial process described in this handbook.

Academic dishonesty is a violation of the Grounds for Disciplinary Action as described in this handbook. A student may be subject to any of the following types of disciplinary action should disciplinary action be pursued by the professor: disciplinary warning; disciplinary probation; disciplinary suspension, expulsion and/or alternative actions as agreed on by the student and hearing officer. It should be noted that no student who allegedly commits academic dishonesty will be able to drop or change the grade option for the course in question and is not eligible to have the opportunity to sit for an “F Examination” for the course.

The definitions and examples presented below are a sampling of types of academic dishonesty and are not to be construed as an exhaustive or exclusive list. The academic integrity policy applies to all students, undergraduate and graduate, and to scholarly pursuits and research. Additionally, attempts to commit academic dishonesty or to assist in the commission or attempt of such an act are also violations of this policy.

- Academic Fraud: The alteration of documentation relating to the grading process. For example, changing exam solutions to negotiate for a higher grade or tampering with an instructor’s grade book.
- Collaboration: Deliberately facilitating an act of academic dishonesty in any way or form. For example, allowing another student to observe an exam paper or allowing another student to “recycle” one’s old term paper or using one another’s work in a paper or lab report without citing it as another’s work.
- Copying: Obtaining information pertaining to a graded exercise by deliberately observing the paper of another student. For example, noting which alternative a neighboring student has circled on a multiple-choice exam.
- Cribbing: Use or attempted use of prohibited materials, information, or study aids in an academic exercise. For example, using an unauthorized formal sheet during an exam.
- Fabrication: Unauthorized falsification or invention of any information in an academic exercise. For example, use of “bought” or “ready-made” term papers, or falsifying lab records or reports.
- Plagiarism: Representing the work or words of another as one’s own through the omission of acknowledgment or reference. For example, using sentences verbatim from a published source in a term paper without appropriate referencing, or presenting as one’s own the detailed argument of a published source, or presenting as one’s own electronically or digitally enhanced graphic representations from any form of media.
- Sabotage: Destruction of another student’s work. For example, destroying a model, lab experiment, computer program, or term paper developed by another student.
- Substitution: Utilizing a proxy, or acting as a proxy, in any academic exercise. For example, taking an exam for another student or having a homework assignment done by someone else.
