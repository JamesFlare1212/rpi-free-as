## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

This course introduces students to the concepts and techniques of creating responsive environments using microcontrollers, sensors, and actuators. Students will learn to program and systems that respond to human presence and create interactive physical prototypes that challenge the understanding of sentience in a technological context, while expanding the possibilities for architectural and environmental design. The course will emphasize the integration of physical and digital systems, fostering an iterative design process that includes experimentation, prototyping, and refinement. Through the lens of both contemporary practice and historical context, students will engage with works from influential designers and theorists, exploring the intersection of spatial design, technology, and human interaction.

## Learning Outcomes

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes (CLOs):

- Conceptual Understanding of Interactive Design Principles & Theory(Assessment Measure 1, 2, 5, 7)Students will have the ability to critically analyze and apply design principles of interactive systems with an emphasis on discussions of sentience and multi-loop responses.
- Technical Knowledge of Responsive Systems(Assessment Measure 3, 4, 5, 7)Students will have the ability to program and fabricate interactive systems including sensor data input, actuator control and physical integration.
- Conceptual Communication(Assessment Measure 3, 5, 6, 7)Students will have the ability to communicate design concepts effectively through diagrams and technical drawings that demonstrate an understanding of the development of both digital and physical systems.
- Fabrication of Full Scale Installation Prototype(Assessment Measure 2, 3, 4, 6, 7)Students will have the ability to fabricate a final project that demonstrates an understanding of the technical, conceptual & communicative skills developed throughout the course.

## Policies

### Attendance Policy:

Pin-ups  
Periodically students will be required to present their work publicly in a formal review  
process. These presentations are a critical part of the architectural education. It is an  
opportunity for the student and the faculty to judge the project and critically evaluate the  
process of design. It may be appropriate at points in the semester to evaluate only  
selected projects to convey information relevant to the entire class. In this situation it is  
important for each student to witness and participate in the review process. Even if your  
project is not being reviewed, the criticism and discussion should inform the evaluation  
of your own progress and design.  
Desk Critiques  
The design studio environment involves two-way communication. The role of the studio  
critic is to evolve and strengthen the student’s design process. Every time the student  
meets with the faculty, he or she must have new iterations of work based on previous  
conversations. If the student has not produced new work, the faculty reserves the right  
to skip the critique and continue with other students who have produced new material.  
Attendance  
Attendance is required at all class meetings during the semester. Two (2) unexcused  
absences from class will result in the lowering of the grade by one (1) letter. Four (4)  
unexcused absences from class during the term result in an automatic F. Whether an  
absence (health, immediate family death, officially approved field / sport trip, etc.) is  
excused or unexcused is determined by the Students Experience Office (4th Floor  
Academy Hall, x8022, se@rpi.edu), upon presentation of required documentation,  
according to established policies and procedures (see http://  
http://se.rpi.edu/policies/ea/).  
Students are required to work in studio during studio hours and have the required  
materials and basic tools to develop the assignments. Work includes participation in site  
visits, design and development, pinups, presentations, discussions, and field trips.  
Failure to work in class with undivided attention and disruptive behavior (tardiness,  
leaving early, lack of participation, general socializing, etc.), as well working on  
assignments from other classes during class, will count as absences.

- ### Academic Integrity

  Student-teacher relationships are built on trust. For example, students must trust that teachers have made appropriate decisions about the structure and content of the courses they teach, and teachers must trust that the assignments that students turn in are their own. Acts that violate this trust undermine the educational process. The Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities and The Graduate Student Supplement define various forms of Academic Dishonesty and you should make yourself familiar with these. In this class, all assignments that are turned in for a grade must represent the student’s own work. In cases where help was received, or teamwork was allowed, a notation on the assignment should indicate your collaboration.

  Violations of academic integrity may also be reported to the appropriate Dean (Dean of Students for undergraduate students or the Dean of Graduate Education for graduate students, respectively).

  If you have any question concerning this policy before submitting an assignment, please ask for clarification. In addition, you can visit the following site for more information on our Academic Integrity Policy: [Students Rights, Responsibilities, and Judicial Affairs](https://info.rpi.edu/dean-students/student-rights-responsibilities-and-judicial-affairs).

Interdisciplinary collaboration is a critical component of this course. All assignments that
