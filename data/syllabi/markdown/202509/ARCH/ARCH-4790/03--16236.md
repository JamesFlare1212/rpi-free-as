## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

*Architecture is the will of the age conceived in spatial terms. Living. Changing. New.*

Mies van der Rohe, 1923

*More than in any other realm of human life the so-called artist must learn only one thing in order to be creative: not to resist himself, but to re-sist, without exception every human, tech-nical, social, economical factor that pre-vents him from being himself-in that respect any artist in any field is unique, be-cause he is given the inherent right of resistance to falsehood, presumptuousness, to mediocrity, to fashions, in short, to conformity. The real artist will never shirk from this responsibility and from the devastating consequences that might follow his being himself. The artist is never of his time. He is always against his time. If he would be only of his time, he is already dead. Time is temporary, and every moment lived is already past. The artist's work represents eternal truth. What his contacts with eternal truth are the sub-conscious, the super-conscious, the mastery of techniques-he doesn't know, nobody can teach it because nobody knows.*

Frederick Kielser, Art Is the Teaching of Resistance, 1959

**Studio Brief**

The foundations of humanist architecture are under siege. The degradation of

democratic public space is an existential threat that architecture must directly confront.

The assault on life and truth is no longer concealed but laid bare and transparent. The vandals of democracy now weaponize public institutions to corrode it from within. We

witness the steady rise of overt authoritarianism, ecological collapse, and the mass

normalization of dehumanization, violence, and death. Truth is replaced by falsehoods

designed to obstruct coherent narratives. Meaninglessness reigns. Nothing is certain.

This present nihilism thrives in a global theater of mutual destruction – between nature

and society – plunging us deeper into a destabilized, chaotic, and endless era of

physical and digital warfare, threats, and biological annihilation. Architecture must confront this

existential condition with a responsibility to protect. What are the new threats it must

address? What are the new structures of security and defense?

In this period of rapid change, massive disruptions to our physical and social infrastructure have exposed both the fragility of our systems and the failure of many institutions to serve the public equitably. Institutions, which should form the bedrock of a democratic society, have revealed deep structural shortcomings, leaving some communities disproportionately vulnerable. The Covid-19 pandemic not only disrupted the flow of social and economic exchange but also rendered portions of our infrastructure obsolete. The nineteenth-century myth of continuous upward progress has come to a standstill, giving way to a moment of accelerated transformation in which systems, institutions, and the built environment are being reconfigured, and new methods of communication, contact, and exchange are being invented.

Institutions must reorient themselves to confront the deliberate degradation of public life and public space. The future demands more than adaptation—it demands reinvention. How will we communicate in what comes next? What spaces and programs are essential to foster humanist communication and social cohesion amid the turbulence of rapid technological development? How will humans engage with their simulated counterparts—robots, AI agents, algorithmic voices?

In a world of tools that amplify communication while obscuring its sources, how will truth prevail? We must insist on rebuilding a healthy society through cultural production and the arts. The Institute of Contemporary Art must claim its place as a vital, constructive force—an active platform for imagining, testing, and enacting new forms of life, contact, and exchange.

Students will encounter art firsthand, exploring its varying degrees of presence, and will design spaces that put the artist first and the engaging public.

This studio will explore and reimagine a contemporary art center for the present and future. What does a contemporary art center look like in 2025 and beyond, and how might we imagine entirely new kinds of centers for cultural production? What forms will art take in the coming years, and how will emerging technologies transform the ways we create and engage with it? These questions invite us to reflect not only on the kinds of art we value personally, but also on what forms resonate with society at large, and why. At their core, art and cultural centers have long served as protected spaces of communication, places where we commune with the public and where artistic forms become the nucleus of engagement. Amid a fragmented, distracted, and often destructive society, how can these centers nurture deeper dialogue while creating spaces for meaningful connection? What is the language of the future? How will we communicate? What media will we use to communicate ideas? What should an ICAs embody today, and how might they expand their role in the future?

Should art be understood as a social science, integrated into systems of health care and holistic therapy, or positioned as a vital platform for climate change action and the sciences? Should art provoke change and be an agent for social transformation and action?

What should a center for contemporary art and culture present today? Students will design a new institute of contemporary art including complimentary programs such as a library, education spaces, artist residence and studio space, café/bar, and exhibition / performance spaces. Each student will define both current and future forms of art or cultural production, select a specific type of art for their institute, and orient its programming toward disciplines such as visual arts, music and sound, film, architecture, performance, writing, and language.

**What is an ICA?**

An ICA is less a museum of objects and more a cultural platform—a flexible, discursive, and experimental institution designed to provoke dialogue, support artists, and expand how society engages with art and ideas.

# Comparative Chart: ICA vs Museum vs Gallery

| Aspectss | Institute of Contemporary Art (ICA) | Museum | Commercial Gallery |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Core Mission | Platform for contemporary art and cultural experimentation | Preserve, research, and display art/history for education and heritage | Promote and sell artworks in the art market |
| Collections | Usually no permanent collection; focuses on temporary works | Maintains permanent collections as core identity | No permanent collection; works are for sale |
| Exhibitions | Rotating exhibitions, commissions, interdisciplinary projects | Mix of permanent displays and temporary exhibitions | Rotating shows tied to market demand |
| Artist Support | Strong support for emerging and underrepresented artists; commissions new work | Supports artists through acquisitions and occasional commissions | Supports represented artists with sales, promotion, and exposure |
| Public Engagement | Workshops, talks, performances, screenings; fosters dialogue | Education programs, tours, school outreach; emphasis on history | Collector events, openings; limited educational programming |
| Funding Model | Non-profit; supported by grants, philanthropy, and memberships | Often government or foundation-supported; ticketing revenue | Privately funded through sales and commissions |
| Responsibility to Society | Civic laboratory addressing urgent cultural, political, and social issues | Guardians of cultural heritage, ensuring long-term preservation | Drives the art market but limited civic or educational role |

**ICA Definition**

An **Institute of Contemporary Art (ICA)** is a cultural institution dedicated to the presentation, research, and promotion of contemporary art across diverse media—visual arts, performance, film, architecture, design, sound, writing, and other interdisciplinary practices. Unlike traditional museums, ICAs generally do not maintain large permanent collections; instead, they emphasize temporary exhibitions, experimental projects, and discursive programming that respond to current cultural, political, and technological contexts.

**How They Function**

- **Exhibitions & Programs**They organize rotating exhibitions, performances, screenings, lectures, workshops, and symposia that highlight emerging, mid-career, and established artists. The focus is often on innovation, risk-taking, and dialogue rather than canonized or historical displays.
- **Platforms for Experimentation**ICAs function as laboratories where artists can test new ideas and audiences can engage with evolving forms of cultural production. This may involve site-specific commissions, participatory projects, or cross-disciplinary collaborations.
- **Education & Public Engagement**Many ICAs provide educational programs, artist talks, and community partnerships. Their role is not only to exhibit art but also to foster critical discourse and promote cultural literacy among diverse publics.
- **Global & Local Networks**ICAs often serve as nodes in international art circuits while remaining grounded in their local communities. They collaborate with other institutions, residencies, and biennials, but also support local artists and issues relevant to their city or region.

**Responsibilities**

- **Cultural Stewardship**: Advancing contemporary art practices by giving visibility to underrepresented voices, experimental forms, and new media.
- **Public Access & Dialogue**: Ensuring accessibility (physical, financial, intellectual) and creating spaces for open conversation about contemporary issues.
- **Support for Artists**: Offering residencies, production support, research opportunities, and platforms for presentation.
- **Innovation & Risk**: Unlike collecting museums, ICAs take responsibility for pioneering new forms and initiating cultural debates, especially when they are challenging or provocative.
- **Community Role**: Acting as civic and cultural anchors—providing inclusive spaces where art intersects with social justice, climate change, technology, and identity politics.
- **Accountability & Sustainability**: Managing governance, funding (often through a mix of public grants, private philanthropy, memberships, and events), and ensuring the institution operates transparently and responsibly.

**Grading Criteria**

Students receive a midterm status grade following the mid-review and one formal grade at the end of the semester. Throughout the term, verbal feedback is provided during weekly presentations, desk critiques, and formal reviews with invited critics. Attendance for the full duration of all reviews is mandatory. If you must miss a class, notify the instructor in advance.

Because each exercise builds on the previous one, grading is cumulative throughout the semester. The final grade reflects both overall progress and the quality and quantity of work presented at the final review. Midterm reviews provide formal assessments.Work is evaluated for inventiveness, preparation, resolution, effort, engagement, and the clarity of graphic and verbal presentations.

**Archiving**

At the end of the course, students must submit all final digital work to the instructor. Semester grades will be released only after this material is received. In addition, a selection of exemplary projects will be chosen for inclusion in the institutional publication.

Scale

[standard of measure or estimation] late 14c., "series of registering marks; marks laid down to determine distance along a line," (in Chaucer's description of the astrolabe), from Latin scala "ladder, flight of stairs," from *scansla, from stem of scandere "to climb" (see scale (v.1)).

The noun in the classical Latin sense is rare, though Middle English had it as "ladder used in sieges" (c. 1400). The meaning "succession or series of steps ascending or descending" is from c. 1600; that of "standard for estimation" (large scale, small scale, etc.) is from 1620s.

The musical sense of "definite and standard series of tones within a certain range," typically an octave (1590s), and the meaning "proportion of a representation to the actual object" (1660s) are via Italian scala, from Latin scala.

Hence also the verb scale in the sense of "measure or regulate by a scale" (1798), "draw, project, or make according to scale" (by 1885), and scale down "cut or decrease proportionally in every part" (by 1887). Related: Scaled; scaling.

Domestic (adjective)

early 15c., from M.Fr. domestique (14c.) and directly from L. domesticus "belonging to the household," from domus "house," from PIE \*domo-/\*domu- "house, household" (cf. Skt. damah "house;" Avestan demana- "house;" Gk. domos "house," despotes "master, lord;" L. dominus "master of a household;" O.C.S. domu, Rus. dom "house;" Lith. dimstis "enclosed court, property;" O.E. timber "building, structure"), from \*dem-/\*dom- "build." The usual IE word for "house" (It., Sp. casa are from L. casa "cottage, hut;" Germanic \*hus is of obscure origin). The noun meaning "household servant" is 1530s (a sense also found in O.Fr. domestique). Domestics, originally "articles of home manufacture," is attested from 1620s. Related: Domestically. Domestic violence is attested from 19c. as "revolution and insurrection;" 1977 as "spouse abuse, violence in the home."

Domesticate

1630s, of animals; 1741, of persons, "to cause to be attached to home and family;" from M.L. domesticatus, pp. of domesticare "to tame," lit. "to dwell in a house," from domesticus (see domestic). Related: Domesticated; domesticating.

Extract (verb)

"to draw out, withdraw, take or get out, pull out or remove from a fixed position, literally or figuratively," late 15c., from Latin extractus, past participle of extrahere "draw out," from ex "out, out of" (see ex-) + trahere "to draw" (see tract (n.1)). Related: Extracted; extracting.

### Attendance Policy:

**Attendance**

Attendance is required for successful completion of the course and will be recorded at every class session. Lectures, readings, discussions, and reviews form the primary source of course material, and students are responsible for all content covered during class. Unexplained absences will negatively affect the final grade.

**Attendance Policy**

Consistent attendance and participation are required. Attendance at the final presentation and all reviews is mandatory.

- **Two (2) unexcused absences**will lower the final grade by one letter.
- **Four (4) unexcused absences**during the term will result in an automatic **F**.

Excused absences (e.g., illness, immediate family death, officially approved field or athletic trips) must be verified through the **Student Experience Office** (4th Floor, Academy Hall, x8022, se@rpi.edu) with appropriate documentation, following established policies and procedures:http://se.rpi.edu/policies/ea/

### Academic Policies:

- ### Academic Integrity

  Student-teacher relationships are built on trust. For example, students must trust that teachers have made appropriate decisions about the structure and content of the courses they teach, and teachers must trust that the assignments that students turn in are their own. Acts that violate this trust undermine the educational process. The Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities and The Graduate Student Supplement define various forms of Academic Dishonesty and you should make yourself familiar with these. In this class, all assignments that are turned in for a grade must represent the student’s own work. In cases where help was received, or teamwork was allowed, a notation on the assignment should indicate your collaboration.

  Violations of academic integrity may also be reported to the appropriate Dean (Dean of Students for undergraduate students or the Dean of Graduate Education for graduate students, respectively).

  If you have any question concerning this policy before submitting an assignment, please ask for clarification. In addition, you can visit the following site for more information on our Academic Integrity Policy: [Students Rights, Responsibilities, and Judicial Affairs](https://info.rpi.edu/dean-students/student-rights-responsibilities-and-judicial-affairs).

### Additional Academic Integrity Course Policy and Penalty Information:

**Statement of Academic Integrity**

The *Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities* defines forms of academic dishonesty and outlines procedures for addressing them. Academic dishonesty violates the trust between students and instructors:

- **Students**must trust that faculty have made appropriate decisions about course structure and content.
- **Instructors**must trust that the work students submit is their own.

Cheating, plagiarism (failure to properly attribute or cite the work of others), and willful damage or theft of another person’s work all constitute violations of academic integrity. A first offense will result in a failing grade for the course and will be reported to the Dean of Students for disciplinary action.

For full details, see the *Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities*:https://info.rpi.edu/dean-students/05/21/2019/rensselaer-handbook-student-rights-and-responsibilities

**Active Participation**

Students are expected to participate in a consistent, productive, and meaningful manner. This includes arriving on time to class sessions, completing all assignments by the required deadlines, and engaging fully in group discussions, presentations, and critiques. Active participation also extends to working diligently during in-class sessions and collaborating effectively with peers on team projects. The course will include both individual and group work, and students are expected to contribute constructively in both settings.
