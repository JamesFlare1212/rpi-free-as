## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

The title of this studio is based on the phenomenally successful 1960 “Think Small” ad campaign (for the Volkswagen Beetle in America), and the 1973 publication, “Small is Beautiful: a study of economics as if people mattered”, E.F. Schumacher’s influential book.

The content of this course will engage with the decade-long rise of the Tiny House in popular culture, online, and the notion that the ADU may be a kind of urbanist savior (other synonyms for the Tiny House include the Mother-in-Law-Apartment, Granny Flat, Urban Cabin and Guest House).

Why small, and why now? Or, again?

In this design studio, we will initially examine the history and culture of Tiny Houses while we design site-specifically for Troy. Barbara Nelson, Executive Director of the Troy Architecture Project (TAP Inc., a non-profit based on River Street) has recently been awarded a $1.8 million federal grant from New York State. Troy homeowners with a range of income maximums will be able to apply for $120,000 toward the design and construction of an ADU, aka “tiny house”.

In partnership with TAP, students in Small is Beautiful will work with real Troy clients on the design of their ADU. More than a theoretical exercise: there is the real possibility that some projects designed in the studio could get built. At the very least, your dialogue with TAP will greatly influence the design process. And you will get to experience Troy in a radically new way: from the inside.

TAP will guide us through the application screening process, client conversations, site visits and design development. The goal (initially as individual designers, and later in teams of 2 or 3) is to design ADUs, spec materials and learn the process of developing a budget. Perhaps our greatest challenge will be to introduce a contemporary architectural language to Troy, which—as a social entity contained by gorgeous 19th century buildings—can be resistant to change.

For more information on TAP, Inc.:

-https://www.tapinc.org/

## Course Goals

### Goals:

STUDIO OBJECTIVES

To develop an awareness and understanding of the role of the architect. To understand the political role of the architect within an economic structure, especially that of a not-for-profit community-based firm.

To develop an awareness of the importance of the designer/client dialogue. To explore computation and other strategies (such as data mining and A. I.) as part of the research required to establish viable programs for said site. To leverage these tech approaches in tandem with traditional methods such as precedent studies, personal and analytical writing, sketching, model-making and other visioning tools.

To collaborate on an architectural program of sustainability, restoration and improvement, mindful of historical contexts and emergent materials. To see architecture as an agent of interpersonal and cultural exchange.

To engage the site first as a set of limits and adjacencies, both physical and programmatic.

To design an architectural context of site and construction that integrates and invigorates a neighborhood condition, an economic lever for the property owner thereby creating a productive urbanism of sustainability.

## Course Content

### Content Details:

2\. INTRODUCTION: SMALL IS BEAUTIFUL STUDIO

A Note on Vertical Studios

Architecture, like art, has many definitions, some predicated on historical continuity, others on the tendency to break rules (thereby establishing new ones). Many of these definitions are useful in understanding precedent, moments of transition (technologically, philosophically, and in terms of practical application), and others remain in the realm of the theoretical. A good pedagogical approach might be one where particular “schools of thought” and “trends” are valued less than remaining open to the spectrum of ideas.

Vertical studios (or option studios) in Rensselaer’s School of Architecture are a perfect illustration of that pedagogical spectrum. Entered into after two years spent in core studios, the verticals allow professors to bring specific research topics to their students in an environment of exploration and speculation that builds on the values of the core. These areas of research might be recent to the professor or the result of a decades-long engagement. They often entail new technologies, emerging building/construction typologies, philosophical paradigm shifts or can leverage allied arts and traditions such as sculpture, painting, music, dance and literature. As a practicing artist, I have exploited my contacts in the worlds of visual art, manufacturing, performance, NGO’s (working with professional organizations in the region) in order to bring my students in contact with real clients and real sites. “Small is Beautiful” might be the purest example of this idea so far.

Getting back to definitions: on principle, I have always operated these studios on the premise that architecture is a material intervention into a physical space that results in a social change. Whether a studio might culminate in a real-world construction at a 1:1 scale or utilize a site that we are unable to visit, there is always a desired result of an (ultimately) buildable project that could function somewhere on Earth, responding to the laws of gravity and other factors (realistic budgets, existing materials, looking critically at contemporary social problems, etc).

This semester’s Small is Beautiful studio is no different: we will look critically at the increasingly troubling trend of national housing shortages, the need to rethink zoning laws, the implications of a livable wage and the growing number of unhoused persons globally, but particularly in the U.S and specifically in Troy, NY. Perhaps ADUs can contribute to solutions. How can ADUs be part of a socioeconomic and cultural revolution? How can—what will likely be the smallest structures you will design in the SoA make a meaningful contribution to the discussion of these massive social concerns?

A Note on Collaboration

Students will work individually at first, then form partnerships based on shared interests in sites explored via dialogues with property owner and TAP Inc.’s architects and designers. Throughout the semester there will be grades given for individual work, the team work, and students will assess their partner’s contributions. The Small Studio is organized around two intertwined collaborative concepts: Working with a client and working with TAP.

2\. SITE

Conceptually, each team’s site is a location to be discovered, and as such has no current latitude/longitude coordinates. We will have to wait for TAP to assign us clients, and we do not know if theat will be 5 or 15. The ultimate size of the studio will also play a role: as you know we thrive best in a student/teacher ratio between 12:1 or 15:1. My understanding is that the numbers may be higher this semester, for variety of reasons. This is unusual for a design studio. As mentioned, the site—at the moment—is Troy itself. Even we are assigned discreet sites and clients, we will also mapping strategies and other resources to assess viability, access, proximity to other programs and desirable landscape conditions.

Scale

The rough size and shape of the site has NOT been predetermined: it will be a developed - or undeveloped - parcel of land AT A MINIMUM equivalent to the area of the owned lot. You will have to determine those dimensions, exclusive of parking. Knowledge of setbacks will guide what can and cannot be built on site, how large, how it relates to neighborhood context, etc.

3\. PROGRAM

As with the search for a site, determining program will be the dialogical responsibilities of each team. The site will first be developed as a domestic landscape with limited programs, determined by the terms of the NYS grant AND the client, as well as counsel from TAP’s architects. You will direct the clients to better understand both the potential of their site AND your value as designers. It is likely some clients will have some building experience: most property owners do. A phased development will privilege the inherent geometries, sun/ shading and topography of the site.

## Learning Outcomes

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes (CLOs):

- Learning Outcome #1: Analytical Skills – Ability to read an existing site via GIS software and site photography and distill an architectural/landscape architectural idea from regional cultural, agricultural, industrial and other influences, such as historical educational presence and tourism. Ability to communicate reading of an existing geographical condition in a series of clear graphically annotated diagrams. Ability to read and assess the potential and context of a site. Ability to communicate reading of a site in a series of clear graphically annotated diagrams (Demonstrated by assessment measures #1, 2, and 3).
- Learning Outcome #2: Speculative Skills - Ability to use architectural drawing, modeling, and visualization in an exploratory manner to generate and test innovative architectural ideas. Ability to succinctly describe the intent of these architectural ideas in writing. (Demonstrated by assessment measures #1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, ).
- Learning Outcome #3: Design / Build Directive Skills - Ability to conceive and convey a clear architectural design proposition, which instructs and conveys instructions and directives towards the physical realization of the proposal. (Demonstrated by assessment measures #3, 4, 5, 7, and 8). This includes the research and development of a highly precise and itemized budget including site prep, construction, labor and materials.
- Learning Outcome #4: Collaborative Skills – Ability to collaborate and communicate with other students as well as institutional partners (for example, a cultural organization or a not-for-profit agency such as our semester partner, TAP Inc.) and collaborators beyond the classroom setting. In this particular case the students could conceptually align themselves with another “regional” partner (such as a regional timber manufacturer, a craftsperson or entity such as RCHS, with particular historical and material expertise. The class will function largely in small teams of 2 o3 depending on sites/clients and will be expected to conduct themselves as small “firms” with name, logo, mission statement and self-assigned design paths.

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes Assessment Measures:

1. Precedent Analysis:

This will involve the careful study of historically significant ADUs, specialized structures (like RVs, military vehicles, vernacular architecture, installation art and small-scale dwellings, legal and otherwise). Building analysis will include terms of materiality, local/cultural construction techniques, cultural relevance. The analysis will include both graphic and written elements and will serve to inform future design decisions in the studio. Slide deck presentations will be individually made by the students.

2. Site Analysis:

The clients’ sites will be identified and selected by TAP after meeting NYS Grant requirements such as maximum incomes and will be identified by the teams via GIS (and other methods: Street View, documentaries, journalist reports) and tin-person site visits, perhaps combined with client interviews. This site analysis will include a careful study of the existing qualities and features of the location starting with street address, site, structures on site, landscaping, street context, neighborhood context, City of Troy context, Rensselaer County context, Hudson River context. This will include an understanding of the proximate dimensional, material, spatial, and programmatic aspects of the place (extended site). Desk presentations AND slide decks to the entire studio.

3. Accumulation and Documentation of Budget data:

Using contemporary cost estimate strategies (including web sites, interviews with contractors, TAP data, contemporary labor costs, standard-to-sustainable building material estimates, reclaimed materials (often FREE), students will create a detailed budget for their ADUs, being mindful of the grant cap of $120.000 USD.  
4\. Three-Dimensional Imagery:

We will rely upon the efficiency and effectiveness of three-dimensional images such as axonometric drawings and perspectival renderings to clearly covey and express spatial and programmatic ideas. These will be preceded by hand drawings and quick graphic exercises.  
5\. Project Manifesto:

Writing and other forms of language will allow us to shape the conceptual framework of the project. Select readings will also be made available to inform and stimulate a critical lens around the questions of ownership, (see Harvard ADU video), architectural symbolism and cultural meaning (See Umberto Eco’s “The Name of the Rose”) and more prosaic aspects such as property rights, setbacks, building permits, landscaping, utilities, sustainable materials, cooperation with neighbors, growth timelines, aging-in-place and extended family users and renters. Conducting interviews with the clients will also lead to analysis of their history, wants, financial limits, stylistic preferences, stance on sustainability, succession and other legal issues.  
6\. Physical Models:

Physical models will be employed to capture the volumetric, material, and scalar dimensions of design proposals. Models should be built so that they enable possibilities of play, transformation, and design discovery. Therefore, there are 2 distinct classes of models; those models for (design iterations) and those models of (final representation(s).

7\. Preliminary Design Proposals:

A crucial part of the design process will involve the testing of multiple design schemes, developed both by the team and by its individual members, leading to strategies to critically arrive at an ideal proposition. This process will involve quick and extended studies towards the final.  
8\. Final Design Proposal:

The final design proposal must convey a detailed and concentrated understanding of both the conceptual and practical aspects of the site, its previous conditions and the successful deployment of a design negotiated between the team members and the client(s).

- ### Academic Integrity

  Student-teacher relationships are built on trust. ### Additional Academic Integrity Course Policy and Penalty Information:

Academic Integrity The Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities defines various forms of Academic Dishonesty and procedures for responding to them. All forms are violations of the trust between students and teachers. Student-teacher relationships are built on trust. For example, students must trust that teachers have made appropriate decisions about the structure and content of the courses they teach, and teachers must trust that the assignments that students turn in are their own performance. Acts that violate this trust undermine the educational process.

Policy on collaboration and cheating/plagiarism Interdisciplinary collaboration is a critical component of this course. All assignments that are turned in for a grade must accurately represent the student’s own work, and properly cite authorship of work that was not solely completed by the student. Any miss-representation of work shall be evaluated and could result in a failing grade for the assignment. A subsequent infraction by the student will result in a failing grade for the course.
