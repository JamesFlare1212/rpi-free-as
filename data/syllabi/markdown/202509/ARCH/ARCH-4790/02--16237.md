## Course Goals

### Goals:

This Vertical Studio will explore architecture in the public realm through the lens of circularity, adaptability, and ephemerality.

Buildings - like the ground they are grafted onto and materials that pass through them - are malleable formations of matter that are concentrated and reconfigured, over and over, from one place to another, through processes that are often invisible to us. As such, this studio’s inquiry will challenge the traditional view of buildings as permanent structures, singular objects, with fixed use types. It will take up the notion of architecture as a “moving project”, conceptually and literally. This effort will combine speculative thinking with pragmatic, material-based work, orienting its study towards a creative exploration of the multivalent ways that architecture changes over time.

The studio’s design project is a new urban center in Troy, N.Y. that includes a public waste recovery/reuse center and intermodal transit hub. It is a multi-use, multi-building complex that will be located at the intersection of the Hudson River and the Poestenkill Creek. This area is a gritty, leftover post-industrial landscape where urban systems (ecology + mobility) converge and the entanglement of planetary-scale realms (biosphere + technosphere) appear in curious, almost invisible ways. Much of this place could be characterized as full of a certain kind of emptiness, one in which architecture is conspicuously absent. It’s most visible physical feature is a massive salt pile used to de-ice roads in the Capitol Region. The city recently announced that this pile will be removed by 2029, raising the question:

What should come next…and what might come after that?

## Learning Outcomes

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes (CLOs):

- OUTCOME 1: Research 1a. ability to use / develop specialized knowledge - Students will conduct and implement discipline-specific research on the field of regenerative design and circular building construction methods.
- OUTCOME 1: Research 1b. ability to use / develop broad and integrative knowledge - Students will conduct and implement interdisciplinary research on the urban transformation of the city of Troy, historically and today, identifying social and ecological factors, architectural and landscape typologies, and spatial and material conditions.
- OUTCOME 2 Intellectual Design Skills 2a. ability to design - Students will learn to differentiate between, and demonstrate the ability to design for, different types of architectural change (use, symbolism, etc.), different durations/frequencies of change (temporary/permanent, continuous/incremental, etc.), and various realms of impact (natural systems, human systems, etc.)
- OUTCOME 2 Intellectual Design Skills 2b. ability to design using information resources - Students will learn to analyze a program brief and existing site conditions through the use of base documentation and demonstrate the ability to use this information in the design of a public building on a complex urban site with precision, clarity, and impact.
- OUTCOME 2 Intellectual Design Skills 2c. ability to design engaging diverse perspectives - Students will learn to identify the diverse perspectives of project stakeholders and demonstrate the ability to integrate alternative worldviews and lived experiences into a design project: a municipal public works facility and public waterfront park.
- OUTCOME 2 Intellectual Design Skills 2d. ability to design with ethical reasoning - Students will learn to make informed value judgements regarding the appropriate allocation of limited resources (material, spatial, ecological, financial, etc.). Students will demonstrate the ability to articulate the ethical dilemmas inherent in the choices of where, when, and for whom these resources will be distributed and how they are integrated into a project design.
- OUTCOME 2 Intellectual Design Skills 2e. ability to design with quantitative fluency - Students will learn to evaluate a building's constructive anatomy. Students will demonstrate the ability to interpret and synthesize different types of information (written/numerical/visual), establish and calculate numerical areas/space allocations, analyze multiple "test-fit" scenarios, and determine the project's scope of work.
- OUTCOME 2 Intellectual Design Skills 2f. ability to design with communicative fluency - Students will learn to differentiate between different constituencies/audiences (architectural, client, and general public) and demonstrate the ability to articulate a design problem, problem-solving methods, and solution in these different contexts.
- OUTCOME 3 Collaboration 3a ability to collaborate with team - Students will demonstrate the ability to work effectively in 2-person teams using collaborative methods and interpersonal skills. Students will demonstrate the ability to develop a strategy for group work (knowledge/info sharing, ideation, workflow, project management, etc.) that recognizes different group member’s strengths, documents the strategy so that others may understand it, implements the strategy, communicates and evaluates the results, and modifies the strategy as needed.
- OUTCOME 4 Civic and Global Engagement 4a. ability to engage the civic realm - Students will demonstrate the ability to engage issues of public interest/concern, specifically the expansion of public works services in Troy/Capitol Region and related issues of community engagement and public space. Students will develop skills of thoughtful listening, engaged dialogue, and critical thinking to develop designs aimed at making positive change for the common good.
- OUTCOME 4 Civic and Global Engagement 4b. ability to engage the global realm - Students will demonstrate the ability to engage complex public issues associated with sustainable, regenerative design aimed at transforming cities into active parts of a global carbon sink in response to the climate crisis.
- OUTCOME 5 Design Execution 5a. Midterm Review - Students will demonstrate the ability to implement their learning in Outcomes #1-4 in a clear, complete, and compelling way in a presentation that includes diagrams, mappings, orthographic drawings, axonometrics, and renderings.
- OUTCOME 5 Design Execution 5b. Final Review - Students will demonstrate the ability to implement their learning in Outcomes #1-4 in a clear, complete, and compelling way in a presentation that includes diagrams, mappings, orthographic drawings, axonometrics, and renderings.
- Additional grade requirements Attendance AEFIS student assessment (required) incomplete assessments will result in no grade for the course

### Academic Policies:

- ### Academic Integrity

  Student-teacher relationships are built on trust. For example, students must trust that teachers have made appropriate decisions about the structure and content of the courses they teach, and teachers must trust that the assignments that students turn in are their own. Acts that violate this trust undermine the educational process. The Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities and The Graduate Student Supplement define various forms of Academic Dishonesty and you should make yourself familiar with these. In this class, all assignments that are turned in for a grade must represent the student’s own work. In cases where help was received, or teamwork was allowed, a notation on the assignment should indicate your collaboration.

  Violations of academic integrity may also be reported to the appropriate Dean (Dean of Students for undergraduate students or the Dean of Graduate Education for graduate students, respectively).

  If you have any question concerning this policy before submitting an assignment, please ask for clarification. In addition, you can visit the following site for more information on our Academic Integrity Policy: [Students Rights, Responsibilities, and Judicial Affairs](https://info.rpi.edu/dean-students/student-rights-responsibilities-and-judicial-affairs).

### Additional Academic Integrity Course Policy and Penalty Information:

**STATEMENT OF ACADEMINC INTEGRITY**

The Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities defines various forms of Academic Dishonesty and procedures for responding to them. Academic dishonesty violates the trust between students and teachers. Students must trust that teachers have made appropriate decisions about the structure and content of the courses they teach. Teachers must trust that the assignments students turn in are their own performance. Violations of this trust undermines the educational process. Cheating, plagiarism (defined as the lack of attribution or citation of the work of others), willful damage or theft of another person’s work violate academic integrity. The first instance of the breach of this trust will merit a failing grade in this course and the violator(s) will be reported to the Dean of Students for disciplinary action.
