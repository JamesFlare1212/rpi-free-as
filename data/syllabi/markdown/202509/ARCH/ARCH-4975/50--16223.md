## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

This course explores the development of Renaissance and Baroque art and architecture in Rome, situating monuments, artists, and urban planning projects within their cultural, political, and religious contexts. Through a combination of lectures, site visits, and discussions, students will critically engage with key works, historical texts, and architectural treatises to gain a comprehensive understanding of Rome as a center of innovation and artistic exchange.

## Learning Outcomes

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes (CLOs):

-  Identify and analyze the main architectural and artistic typologies of Renaissance and Baroque Rome (churches, palaces, piazzas, villas).
-  Recognize the stylistic contributions of major artists and architects including Alberti, Bramante, Raphael, Michelangelo, Bernini, and Borromini.
-  Explain the historical, religious, and political contexts that shaped artistic production in Rome between the 15th and 18th centuries.
-  Interpret primary sources, including treatises and biographies, and connect them to the monuments studied.
-  Critically evaluate urban planning projects and master plans as reflections of ideological and cultural change.
-  Conduct on-site visual analysis and engage in scholarly discussion about works of art and architecture.

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes Assessment Measures:

All students are expected to be engaged in project-related course work for the full duration of class. The following schedule establishes the structure of work, products required, and important events above and beyond the daily course content delivery.

- **Class Participation**. Regular attendance and active participation in class activities are essential to be a successful learner. Therefore, you must prepare carefully for class by completing all the assignments in advance. The class participation and homework grades can be further affected by the student’s active participation.

Being absent does not excuse you from completing the assigned work or the alternative assignments that your instructor will assign.

- **Exams. There will be two in class multiple choice and ID test and a final exam**.

The final exam will ask you to recognize 10 works of art (from a menu of choices) while placing them in a historical, social, cultural, and stylistic context. You will also be expected to use terminology and methodologies that are specific to the field of art history and to the topic that you are discussing. An oral presentation is also due.

Each exam should be about 1000 words.

- **Weekly Assignments**. You will be asked to create a discussion on a topic as indicated in the syllabus. Each analysis should be about 500 words and should follow a specific methodological approach.

- **Final Research Paper**. This course will teach the process of writing and rewriting – drafting editing and revision – to help students mature as disciplinary thinkers/ writers and to continually improve the relationship between content and clarity.

There will be one 6/8-page research paper related to the requested oral presentation. The paper will focus on the work of a specific architect or a work of architecture.

The paper will need to identify a specific historical methodology as well as standard discipline-based terminology.

**Criteria to evaluate the final exam:**

Each question/work of art will be evaluated as follows:

- 25 points: identification
- 25 points: description and exact contextualization in the proper time frame
- 25 points: critical analysis of the subject matter and its meaning
- 25 points: quotes from bibliography or citing from texts.

TOTAL: **100 POINTS**

**Required Readings**

- ¾ Weekly course handouts.
- ¾ Primary sources: Alberti, On the Art of Building; Vasari, Lives of the Artists; Palladio, Four Books of Architecture.
- ¾ Supplementary articles distributed by instructor.

**Assignments**

1. **In-Class Presentation: Renaissance and Baroque Masters**

Each student will deliver an in-class presentation on one of the masters included in the course (e.g., Alberti, Bramante, Raphael, Michelangelo, Palladio, Bernini, Borromini, etc.). The presentation must be provided:

- ¾ Biography: concise overview of the master’s life and training.
- ¾ Major Works: survey of significant projects, illustrated with images.
- ¾ Historical Context: connection to patrons, politics, religion, or cultural developments.
- ¾ Legacy: influence on later architecture and relevance to contemporary architectural discourse.
- ¾ Format & Requirements:
  - ¾ 15–20 minutes presentation with PowerPoint or equivalent visuals.
  - ¾ A written summary of 2–3 pages (double-spaced, 12 pt. font) submitted on the day of the presentation.
  - ¾ Bibliography with at least three scholarly sources (books, articles, or primary texts).

**Due Date: schedule to be determined in Week 1.**

Evaluation Criteria:

1. Content depth and accuracy: 40%
2. Organization and visual clarity: 20%
3. Critical analysis beyond description: 20%
4. Oral delivery (clarity, timing, engagement): 20%

2. **On-Site Presentation: Monument in Context**

During scheduled site visits, students will prepare a short lecture (10–12 minutes) delivered on-site, focusing on a specific monument or architect being studied that week. The presentation should demonstrate the ability to connect direct observation with broader historical and stylistic analysis.

Format & Requirements:

Oral presentation delivered at the monument.

Content must address: Architect/master responsible, historical background, key innovations, significance.

A 1-page handout (bullet points, dates, bibliography, images if appropriate) distributed to classmates.

**Due Date: Assigned throughout Weeks 6–12 (schedule to be finalized in Week 3)**.

Evaluation Criteria:

Accuracy and clarity of architectural/historical content: 40%

Ability to link monuments to course: 30%

Oral delivery and engagement: 20%

Quality of handout: 10%

### Additional Academic Integrity Course Policy and Penalty Information:

**Academic Integrity**

The Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities defines various forms of Academic Dishonesty and procedures for responding to them. Academic dishonesty violates the trust between students and teachers. Students must trust that teachers have made appropriate decisions about the structure and content of the courses they teach. Teachers must trust that the assignments students turn in are their own performance. Violations of this trust undermines the educational process.

**Policy on collaboration and cheating/plagiarism**

Cheating, plagiarism (defined as the lack of attribution or citation of the work of others), willful damage or theft of another person’s work violate academic integrity. The first instance of the breach of this trust merits a failing grade in this course and the violator will be reported to the Dean of Students for disciplinary action.

Interdisciplinary collaboration is a critical component of the course. All assignments that are turned in for a grade must accurately represent the student’s own work, and properly cite authorship of work that was not solely completed by the student. Any misrepresentation of work is evaluated and could result in a failing grade for the assignment. A subsequent infraction by the student results in a failing grade for the course.
