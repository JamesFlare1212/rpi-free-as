## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

Architectural Design Studio 3 (ADS 3) builds on the spirit of innovation and experimentation emphasized in Architectural Design Studios 1 and 2, while introducing more architectural constraints. Bachelor of Architecture students in ADS 3 are expected to demonstrate how design skills acquired in ADS 1 and ADS 2 are used to resolve program, structure, and accessible route design in a small free-standing art gallery building.

The course will be framed by three main topics: 1) A Relation Between Art and Architecture, 2) How a Museum or Gallery Presents Art, and 3) The Meaning and Significance of Landscape.

1. **A Relation Between Art and Architecture**

*This old debate [about the appropriateness of Art Nouveau ‘total design’] takes on a new resonance today, when the aesthetic and utilitarian are not only conflated but all but subsumed in the commercial, and everything – not only architectural projects and art exhibitions but everything from jeans to genes – seems to be regarded as so much **design.***

\- Hal Foster

*Design and Crime, 2002*

As aesthetic and cultural enterprises art and architecture are closely related. But while art generally maintains purposeful uselessness to emphasize its aesthetic capacities, architecture is always at some level utilitarian. Relationships between the aesthetics of art and the utilitarianism of architecture are highlighted when designing museums, galleries, and other spaces for art. In his essay *Design and Crime*, the art critic Hal Foster implies this question in a general sense – how much is one willing to let the aesthetic and utilitarian concerns of architecture become conflated?

It could be argued that many of the most visible contemporary museum projects, Ateliers Jean Nouvel’s National Museum of Qatar for example, are quite extreme conflations of aesthetics and utility, and that in many high-profile contemporary museums architecture becomes at least as much of an aesthetic statement as the art it contains. In ADS 3 students will design a new gallery building on the grounds of Manitoga, The Russel Wright Design Center. Manitoga is the restored and preserved home of Russel Wright and his family located in Garrison, NY. In designing the gallery building students will investigate relationships between art and architecture. Over the course of the semester, students must formulate a position regarding how and how much a building to house art should be aestheticized in relation to the art it contains.

2. **How a Museum or Gallery Presents Art**

*When the term **museum** was first used in the Renaissance in reference to private collections, it evoked a different experience from what we now know. In one of the museum’s earliest incarnations, the **Cabinet of Curiosities**, natural and art objects were jumbled together on the walls and ceilings, cupboards and drawers of one or two rooms. Their purpose was to surprise and delight: viewers had to find the special objects that attracted them and then make their own connections…*

\- Victoria Newhouse

*Towards a New Museum, 1998*

Ideas of what a building dedicated to showing art and design should be, and how it should present art to visitors, are many and have shifted over time. Today, different museums and galleries present art and engage visitors in different ways. In the book Towards A New Museum, Victoria Newhouse describes several categories of the contemporary museum including: *cabinets of curiosities* that surprise, delight, and ask visitors to make connections between objects; *sacred spaces* that invoke qualities of ecclesiastical architecture in order to raise the experience of viewing art to religious ritual; *monographic museums* that educate visitors about a particular artist’s development; *museums as entertainment* that attempt to engage touristic rather than academic culture; and *museums as environmental art* that become active rather than passive players in how art is viewed and experienced.

In designing a new gallery building for Manitoga, students must consider how the existing institution and the new proposal falls into one or more of these categories – or seeks to define a new category.

3. **The Meaning and Significance of Landscape**

*The reappearance of landscape in the in the larger cultural imagination is due, in part, to the remarkable rise of environmentalism and a global ecological awareness, to the growth of tourism and the associated needs of regions to retain a sense of unique identity, and to the impacts on rural areas by massive urban growth…Today, however, it is not merely an interest in vegetation, earthworks, and site-planning that we see espoused in in various schools of design and planning, but also a deep concern with landscape’s conceptual scope; with its capacity to theorize sites, territories, ecosystems, networks, and infrastructures…*

\- James Corner

*Terra Fluxus, 2006*

Within the field of architectural design, the term ‘landscape’ refers to stretches of land that do not contain buildings. These lands may be highly designed and tightly controlled by human activities, or they may be more wild, and less controlled by human activities. Landscapes are more than just topography and the makeup of the ground; they are agglomerations of biological and geological processes unfolding in time scales from hours to eons. Consider ‘landscape’ as distinct from ‘nature.’ While both these terms refer to culturally constructed ideas regarding environments in the earth’s biosphere, ‘nature’ speaks to the environment in general, and ‘landscape’ specifically highlights the ground as a datum within the environment that is charged with design potentials.

Outside of dense urban development, architecture and landscape are intimately tied together. In the simplest terms, the relationship between architecture and landscape might be seen as a visual and/or formal relationship. For example, a building might appear (as most do) as an object or form that is visually distinct from the surrounding landscape.

On the other hand, a building might be designed so that visually it appears to be more continuous with the landscape, as Russel Wright’s house does through its positioning in relation to the ground, and the continuation of landscape elements to the interior of the architecture.

Visual relationships between landscape and architecture are important, but landscapes are more than just visual constructions; they are geological, biological, and anthropogenic systems. At Manitoga, consider the geological processes that have brought the rocky terrain into being, the biological processes that sustain plant and animal life on the site, and the anthropogenic processes that have dramatically transformed the site first through resource extraction (logging and quarrying), and then again through a highly refined design process undertaken by the Wrights to realize the architecture and landscape seen on the site today.

In designing a new building for Manitoga, consider landscape from both the visual and systems perspectives. Consider how landscape becomes an active participant in the architectural design, and how it plays a part in creating an accessible and inclusive experience for viewing art and design.

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes Assessment Measures:

1. **Precedent Analysis**

To gain an understanding of how other architects on other sites have approached and resolved small museum and gallery projects, the semester will begin with an analysis of this type of building. Students will select one small (approx. 5-20,000 sf) built museum or gallery from a curated list and analyze it in a series of models and diagrams that demonstrate understanding of how the design was conceived and how the building works. Particular attention must be paid to how program, circulation, and structure relate to the architect’s design agenda.

*Assessment Measure #1 - Precedent Analysis:*

A series of notated analytical diagrams that describe the following:

- \- orientation to north and solar exposure using Climate Studio
- \- gross square footage
- \- net square footage
- \- program organization and distribution
- \- horizontal and vertical circulation
- \- site circulation
- \- lighting (approach to natural/artificial light in galleries)

Diagrams will be constructed as overlays on drawings collected from print publications and the internet. Existing floor plans, sections, and site plans do not need to be re-drafted. Scale and format of diagrams to be determined with section critic’s guidance.

Two physical models are required:

- \- structural parti frame model showing entire building at 1/16” scale
- \- corner detail model showing structure and skin, 1/2” scale, 12”x12”x12” max. size

Construct models from basswood, taskboard, and paper as instructed by section critic.

2. **Generative Drawings**

The precedent analysis will lead into a generative exercise that will become the basis of a formal and conceptual language for the semester’s design project. Specifics of this generative exercise will be defined by each section critic individually.

*Assessment Measure #2 – Generative Drawings:*

A set of speculative drawings/models that form the basis of a formal/conceptual design language for the museum design project. Scale and format of drawings to be determined with section critic’s guidance.

3. **Assessment of the Existing Institution**

Manitoga, The Russel Wright Design Center is the former home of the industrial designer Russel Wright, his wife and collaborator, Mary Einstein Wright, and their daughter Ann. The Wrights sited their house, with the assistance of the architect David Leavitt, on a rocky outcropping above pond in an abandoned quarry and logging site. Over a period of years, the 75-arce parcel was transformed from a site of resource extraction into the woodland garden and trail network one sees today. The mission of the institution is stated as:

*MANITOGA / The Russel Wright Design Center presents and interprets Manitoga as the Wrights’ inspiration and design achievement as a leading regional and national center for innovative and sustainable design, architecture, and landscape where we strive to build a creative community and to reach and engage an ever expanding and diverse audience.*

*The Design Center ensures that Manitoga’s extraordinary power of place, the Wrights’ design legacy, and their inspired vision to create Manitoga from a once devastated land are preserved and shared. We celebrate good design for living in creative harmony with nature through tours, programs, and events; to inspire creativity; and to enhance lives of all through design.*

Based on a visit to the site, presentations from the institution’s leadership, learning about the institution from its website, researching its history, and becoming familiar with the Wrights and artists who have been exhibited at Manitoga, students must make their own assessment of how the existing buildings and site work and/or do not work in terms of the museum’s stated mission.

*Assessment Measure #3 – Assessment of the Existing Institution:*

200-word text and 5+ annotated sketches – made by hand on site during the site visit – which describe and assess functioning of the existing buildings and site in relation to the institution’s stated mission.

4. **Site Plan**

Understanding and representing all aspects of an architectural project’s site through drawing is a crucial step in the design process. In this case, the site includes existing buildings and landscape. Draw the Manitoga site, show all relevant site information including:

- \- building footprints
- \- building entrances (service and public)
- \- parking
- \- roads
- \- sidewalks
- \- curbs
- \- curb cuts (if applicable)
- \- vegetation
- \- stairs
- \- contour lines of topography

This drawing must be presentation quality, it may be in color or black and white, it may include rendered elements and/or shadows. This drawing of existing elements will be considered a final presentation quality drawing and it will subsequently be updated to represent the design proposal.

*Assessment Measure #4 – Site Plan:*

Drawing of existing Manitoga site at 1/32” scale. Clearly indicate all site elements listed above using carefully calibrated drawing graphics.

5. **Predesign – Building Massing**

The primary goal of this semester is to design a small freestanding gallery building on the Manitoga grounds with a precise tectonic language, highly considered and resolved interior spaces, and a clear daylighting strategy. Begin the predesign process with an exploration of program and how it informs building massing.

In architecture ‘program’ refers to activities occurring in a building and on its site. At its most fundamental level an architectural program specifies an activity and size for a building (i.e. museum), a zone within a building (i.e. exhibit spaces), and an individual space within a building (i.e. gallery). Decisions regarding design of a building are made in direct response to program. Program for this project is a 5500 square foot (sf) freestanding building containing two main programmatic zones: exhibit space, spaces where art is shown, and public space, non-exhibit spaces which all museum goers can access. Individual spaces that must be included are as follows:

- \- gallery space, 2000sf
- \- education and community space, 1000sf
- \- main entrance and lobby, 1000sf
- \- vertical circulation, 500sf
- \- horizontal circulation, 500sf
- \- service space (restrooms, storage, etc), 500sf

Develop a physical massing model that explores and resolves distribution and organization of program. Note that a massing model works with simplified spatial volumes rather than detailed form, and that this massing model must represent the new building in relation to the existing building and site.

*Assessment Measure #5 – Predesign-Building Massing:*

Physical model showing how program of the new building is organized internally and placed in relation to the ground. 1/16” scale, model materials to be specified by section critic.

6. **Predesign – Response to Context**

In addition to developing massing models, the predesign phase of the project includes developing plan and section diagrams that demonstrate how program and massing relate to context of existing building and site. Consider the following:

- \- how the new building is placed in relation to existing buildings and other site features
- \- how alignments of grids, axes, nodes, or heights (datums) between the new and existing buildings and other site features are created or purposefully broken
- \- how new circulation elements join with and/or alter existing circulation elements
- \- how visitors approach and enter the building
- \- how staff, maintenance and deliveries approach and enter the building
- \- how views from site to building, building to site, and within the building are maintained, created and/or altered
- \- solar orientation of the new addition

*Assessment Measure #6 – Predesign-Response to Context:*

Annotated plan and section diagrams demonstrating how the new addition relates to specific grids, axes, nodes, datums and views in relation to existing buildings and landscape.

7. **Design Synthesis / Parti**

While it is assumed that three-dimensional computer modeling will occur through all phases of project development, two-dimensional orthographically projected floor plans, sections, and elevations will also be used instrumentally in design. These drawings MUST:

- \- be built up from scratch – a three-dimensional model may be used as a reference underlay to help produce the drawing, however it must not be included in the drawing
- \- be used to explore and evaluate design decisions
- \- bring together aspects of the precedent analysis and generative drawings with design decisions made through explorations of massing, programming, and context
- \- be used to explore how natural light is brought into exhibit and public spaces
- \- describe all components of architecture at accurate scale
- \- be used to describe accurate floor, wall, and roof thicknesses
- \- be used to describe simple floor, wall, and roof assemblies
- \- show how all public spaces and restrooms are accessible
- \- be graphically clear through effective use of line weight and line type

Refer to Architectural Graphic Standards as well as the reference file provided to the studio for information regarding architectural drawing standards.

Refer to the 2010 ADA Standards for Accessible Design for the requirements of accessible routes through and around buildings: https://www.ada.gov/law-and-regs/design-standards/2010-stds/#401-general

*Assessment Measure #7 – Design Synthesis / Parti:*

Two floor plans minimum (ground level, upper level), two sections minimum (longitudinal and cross), two elevations at 1/4” scale.

8. **Grading and Accessibility Plan**

The project must include 2-4 accessible parking spaces and an area for delivery vehicles to park and unload in proximity to the building. Designing these areas will include involve grading, which is the process of reshaping land, around the building. Develop a 1/16” scale plan showing the following:

- \- how accessible parking and delivery areas are incorporated into the landscape, and how their relationship to the main entrance of the building, and views to and from the building are carefully considered
- \- topography lines showing how land around the building has been reshaped to achieve the desired design, and provide appropriate slopes for the parking areas, roads, and walkways
- \- how vehicles circulate into and through the parking and delivery areas
- \- an accessible route from the accessible parking spaces to the building

*Assessment Measure #8 – Grading and Accessibility Plan:*

One plan showing parking and grading with 6” interval topography lines at 1/16” scale.

9. **Tectonic Model**

‘Tectonics’ refers to how construction is approached as an art rather than a prosaic technical endeavor. A tectonic approach to architecture implies that assembly, structure, space, and aesthetics are all intimately related. This model must represent at least half the volume of the building (it may be a section model) and how the building sits in the ground. It must explore and show the following:

- \- the structural system of the building
- \- detailed design of interior space development
- \- detailed exterior envelope development
- \- design of at least one daylighting element
- \- accurate floor, wall, and roof thicknesses
- \- simple floor, wall, and roof assemblies

Photograph the model in sunlight to test performance of the daylighting system.

*Assessment Measure #9 – Tectonic Model:*

Physical model of at least half the building and surrounding site, 1/4” scale. Model materials to be determined with section leader’s guidance. A series of photograph documenting the daylighting study of the new building.

10. **Three-Dimensional Imagery**

Three rendered views of the project are required: one aerial view, one eye level view of building exterior, and interior view within the building. Test and save multiple camera options for each view. Once views have been selected, add detail to the three-dimensional model in areas visible in the views. Images must be rendered as photo realistically as possible in Rhino/VRay. Develop a material and texture palette, a technique for handling context, and an overall aesthetic that complements and reinforces conceptual ideas of the project.

Using skills acquired in Digital Constructs 3, use AI Generative Fill in Photoshop to develop background imagery. AI image generation must NOT be used to visualize the building itself. Do not default to a conventional sunny day, blue sky, green vegetation aesthetic. Images must be started at least three weeks before the final review and be developed iteratively until the final review.

*Assessment Measure #10 – Three-Dimensional Imagery:*

Three rendered views of the new addition in relation to existing building and site. Size and format of images to be determined with section leader’s guidance.

11. **Project Manifesto**

A manifesto is a concise, incisive declaration of intent; it is an argument for a position taken. Write a manifesto for the project describes how the project has been designed to address the three main topics introduced at the beginning of the semester:

1. The relationship between art and architecture
2. How the gallery presents art
3. The meaning and significance of landscape

In addition, the manifesto may describe other aspects of the project deemed most important.

*Assessment Measure #11 – Project manifesto:*

300-word text graphically incorporated into final presentation boards.

### Criteria Details:

All assessment measures described above must be submitted for the final review. Course grades will be determined as follows:

- \- Assessment Measure #1: Precedent analysis – 10 points
- \- Assessment Measure #2: Generative drawings – 5 points
- \- Assessment Measure #3: Assessment of the existing institution – 5 points
- \- Assessment Measure #4: Site plan – 5 points
- \- Assessment Measure #5: Predesign-Building Massing – 10 points

- \- Assessment Measure #8: Grading and Accessibility Plan – 10 points
- \- Assessment Measure #9: Tectonic model – 15 points
- \- Assessment Measure #10: Three-dimensional imagery – 10 points
- \- Assessment Measure #11: Project manifesto – 5 points

Section critics will evaluate each assessment measure individually. For example, an ‘excellent’ precedent analysis may receive 9.5 points, a ‘very good’ one 8.5 points, and an ‘average’ one 7.5 points. See grading rubric below for specific descriptions of excellent, very good, average, etc. Total points available for the semester is 100.

At the end of the course, students will be asked to complete a self-assessment survey in which they will evaluate their own performance with respect to each assessment measure. *Completing the Student Assessment at the end of the course is required, as it is essential for the school’s accreditation.* ***Failing to complete this assessment will result in a 5-point deduction from the overall grade.***

At the end of the course, all students will be required to submit all final digital and physical works to Publications. Publications will assign a liaison to the second-year studio; all students will be responsible for submitting work to the liaison. Publications will archive the digital and physical works and return physical works to the students. ***Failing to submit all work to Publications will result in a 5-point deduction from the overall grade.***

Grades will be established according to the following policy:

- \- A (EXCELLENT: 100-94 points = A; 93-90 points = A-) Exceptional performance strongly exceeding the requirements of the course, showing strong academic initiative and independent resourcefulness.
- \- B (GOOD: 89-87 points = B+; 86-84 points = B; 83-80 points = B-) Performance above the norm; accurate and complete; beyond the minimum requirements of the course; work demonstrates marked progress and initiative.
- \- C (AVERAGE: 79-77 points = B+; 76-74 points = B; 73-70 points = B-) Satisfactory work that adequately meets minimum requirements and demonstrates satisfactory comprehension, communication skills, and effort; demonstrates little initiative to investigate the problem without substantial input from the instructor; work shows little improvement.
- \- D (INFERIOR: 69-67 points = B+; 66-64 points = B; 63-60 points = B-) Unsatisfactorily meets minimum requirements; demonstrates minimum comprehension, communication skills, and effort at an inferior level; initiative lacking; improvement not noticeable. (D grades not available to graduate students).
- \- F (FAIL: 59 points and below) Does not meet minimum requirements; fails to adequately demonstrate comprehension, communication skills, and effort.

Section critics will provide students with a mid-semester evaluation. Assessment measures will be evaluated at the midterm and/or final reviews.

## Policies

### Attendance Policy:

Attendance is required at all class meetings during the semester. Two (2) unexcused absences from class will result in the lowering of the grade by one (1) letter. Four (4) unexcused absences from class during the term result in an automatic F. Whether an absence (health, immediate family death, officially approved field / sport trip, etc.) is excused or unexcused is determined by the Students Experience Office (4th Floor Academy Hall, x8022, se@rpi.edu), upon presentation of required documentation, according to established policies and procedures (see http:// http://se.rpi.edu/policies/ea/).

Students are required to work in studio during studio hours, and have the required materials and basic tools to develop the assignments. Work includes participation in site visits, design and development, pinups, presentations, discussions and field trips. Failure to work in class with undivided attention and disruptive behavior (tardiness, leaving early, lack of participation, general socializing, etc.), as well working on assignments from other classes during class, will count as absences.

### Other Course Policies:

**Faculty Lectures**

ADS 3 faculty will give a series of lectures covering major architectural topics being newly introduced this semester including:

1. Architectural program and context
2. Site drawing, planning, and design
3. Orthographic drawing, floorplans, sections, elevations
4. Grading and drawing topography lines
5. Natural lighting systems
6. Tectonics and architectural assemblies
7. Three-dimensional imagery

**Digital Constructs 3**

ADS 3 is accompanied by the co-requisite by Digital Constructs 3, which will take place 2-3:50pm on Wednesdays prior to studio meetings. This course will introduce new, and reinforce known, tools and techniques that support development of studio projects.

**Pin-ups**

Periodically students will be required to present their work publicly in a formal review process. These presentations are a critical part of the architectural education. It is an opportunity for the student and the faculty to judge the project and critically evaluate the process of design. It may be appropriate at points in the semester to evaluate only selected projects in order to convey information relevant to the entire class. In this situation it is important for each student to witness and participate in the review process. Even if your project is not being reviewed, the criticism and discussion should inform the evaluation of your own progress and design.

**Desk Critiques**

The design studio environment involves two-way communication. The role of the studio critic is to evolve and strengthen the student’s design process. Every time the student meets with the faculty, he or she must have new iterations of work based on previous conversations. If the student has not produced new work, the faculty reserves the right to skip the critique and continue with other students who have produced new material.

**SoA Lectures**

Attendance is MANDATORY for ADS 3 students at all School of Architecture’s lectures. On lecture days, class will end early so that all students can be at EMPAC by 5:00pm for the beginning of the lecture. Attendance at SoA lectures will be recorded by the section instructor. Failure to attend lectures will result in a grade deduction.

**Attendance and Timely Completion of Work**

Attendance is required at all class meetings during the semester. Students must notify their section instructor prior to class if they will be absent. For medical absences longer than one week, students must provide a doctor’s note or other supporting documentation to the Student Success Office. The Class Dean will communicate the receipt of the note (with expected end date) to the faculty. Students are responsible for completing any work that they might miss due to illness, quarantine/isolation, or other circumstances. **Students who adhere to these processes will not be penalized per the attendance policy for the course and as detailed in the course syllabus.** However, it must be noted that in the context of a design studio course, missing classes (and/or not presenting new work to discuss at each class meeting) means not receiving feedback. This will hinder project development and very likely result in a lower grade.

Students are required to work in studio during studio hours and have the required materials and basic tools to develop the assignments. Work includes participation in site visits, design and development, pinups, presentations, discussions, and field trips. Failure to work in class with undivided attention and disruptive behavior (tardiness, leaving early, lack of participation, general socializing, etc.), as well working on assignments from other classes during class, will count as absences.

All work must be submitted for review and discussion on the dates noted under each assessment measure. However, final assessment of work will not occur until the final review. Students must respond to feedback received from the section instructor and midterm jury to refine and develop work.

For students with an ADHD diagnosis, extra time applies only to exams, and quizzes completed in class. It does not apply to projects or labs where the students are able to plan more time outside of class to complete assignments. Note that a studio class such as ADS 3 consists solely of project-based assignments. Students are encouraged to attend faculty office hours and tutoring sessions to help plan the completion of studio, lab, or project-based assignments.

**Environmental Hazards**

THE USE OF SPRAY ADHESIVES, PAINTS AND AEROSOLS IS NOT PERMITTED IN OR ON THE GREENE BUILDING. If you must use these materials, use the JEC high bay spraybooth or use these materials outside away from windows and doors and against a backdrop to protect lifeforms and building materials from overspray.

THESE MATERIALS CAN BE HARMFUL TO YOUR HEALTH, THE HEALTH OF OTHERS, AND THE ENVIRONMENT. FOLLOW ALL DIRECTIONS ON THE LABELS. Epoxies and resins shall not be used in the Greene building and if used, should comply with all procedural safeguards and not returned to the Greene Building prior to being adequately off-gassed. Acrylic and other materials, used in the laser cutter or any other process that will create dangerous off-gassing must be adequately off-gassed in the shop off-gassing chamber. This material must be left in the off-gassing chamber for the appropriate time as listed or taken from the Greene Building until the proper time has passed. If any student is caught violating this policy the following may occur:

1. The student may be charged for the cost of cleaning / repairing any damage to the building and its surroundings,
2. The material being worked on will not be included in the review of that project. It will be disqualified from consideration in the evaluation of the project.

WHEN USING ADHESIVES AND OTHER POTENTIALLY TOXIC MATERIALS, FOLLOW THE USER GUIDELINES. IF IT SMELLS BAD - IT IS BAD. IT IS UNACCEPTABLE TO SUBJECT OTHERS TO IT AS IT MAY POSE SERIOUS HEALTH RISKS. IF IT DOESN’T SMELL BAD IT STILL MAY BE BAD. READ AND FOLLOW THE USER GUIDELINES. YOUR RESPONSIBILITY IS NOT ONLY TO YOUR OWN HEALTH, BUT ALSO FOR THE HEALTH OF OTHERS IN THE ENVIRONMENT.

- ### Academic Integrity

  Student-teacher relationships are built on trust. For example, students must trust that teachers have made appropriate decisions about the structure and content of the courses they teach, and teachers must trust that the assignments that students turn in are their own. Acts that violate this trust undermine the educational process. ### Additional Academic Integrity Course Policy and Penalty Information:

**Academic Integrity**

The Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities defines various forms of Academic Dishonesty and procedures for responding to them. All forms are violations of the trust between students and teachers. Student-teacher relationships are built on trust. For example, students must trust that teachers have made appropriate decisions about the structure and content of the courses they teach, and teachers must trust that the assignments that students turn in are their own performance. Acts that violate this trust undermine the educational process.

**Policy on collaboration and cheating/plagiarism**

Interdisciplinary collaboration is a critical component of this course. All assignments that are turned in for a grade must accurately represent the student’s own work, and properly cite authorship of work that was not solely completed by the student. Any miss-representation of work shall be evaluated and could result in a failing grade for the assignment. A subsequent infraction by the student will result in a failing grade for the course.
