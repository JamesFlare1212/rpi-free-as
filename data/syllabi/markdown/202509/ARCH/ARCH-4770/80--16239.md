## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

The way we work and live in urban environments is changing  
profoundly. The last five years has witnessed a profound  
movement of people into and out of cities, changing work habits,  
increases in everyday costs of living, and more hostile and erratic  
weather. This is an incomplete yet daunting list of challenges  
facing urban life.  
New York City is not immune to such changes. In the aftermath  
of the COVID-19 pandemic, the city’s return to regular life has  
yielded mixed results. New York lags the rest of America in terms  
of lost jobs regained. New York only recently returned to pre-  
COVID employment levels. Ridership on the subway system has  
increased, but remains below 2019 levels. Some changes are less  
empirical. Many knowledge workers report increased satisfaction  
due to newly flexible work schedules and less time in the office.  
Many New Yorkers report an increased interest in their local  
neighborhood, and spend more time building relationships outside  
of the workplace. This is only a very abbreviated set of examples  
New York is undergoing. The city is changing, and we would be  
smart to recognize these changes and plan for the future.  
Our interest in this studio is in the built environment in New York,  
and specifically residential neighborhoods. For decades the  
growth and evolution of the neighborhoods in the city has been  
controversial and debated. From the standpoint of city services,  
not every neighborhood is created equal. Some have several  
subways and even ferry access, some are transportation deserts.  
Police presence, traffic congestion, park access, and zoning vary  
significantly across city neighborhoods. These are among the  
issues over which residents and city officials have quarreled for  
decades. I highlight these particular issues because they all have  
a direct impact on the built environment, and have shaped the way  
the city is built today.  
Our focus will be upon issues that will shape New York’s  
neighborhoods in the coming decades. All of them are in some way  
extensions of long-debated topics that are under more urgency  
than in the past.  
1\. housing and public space  
2\. work and transportation  
3\. food and energy  
As noted above, each of these topics are receiving new, potentially  
transformative scrutiny.  
housing and public space:  
People are spending more time at home and less  
time at the office  
People have left the city due to more flexible work  
schedules  
People want larger living spaces to perform work  
People are spending more time in public space, and  
in particular in their local public space  
Public, outdoor space is desired in part as a reaction  
to COVID-19 and in part because people have  
realized they like to be outside  
Even while the population of New York decreases,  
housing costs continue to rise, particularly rent prices  
The development, or lack thereof, of housing and  
public space, is heavily regulated by city and state  
officials  
work and transportation:  
People, particularly those in the ‘knowledge  
economy’, tend to work from home at least several  
days a week.  
Office environments have changed, the overall  
demand for office space has decreased, and offices  
that remain have reconfigured their work space.  
Subway ridership is down, along with that of other  
regional transportation networks.  
More people are riding bicycles  
Congestion pricing in Manhattan could disrupt traffic  
and commuting patterns.  
food and energy:  
There is growing interest in understanding where  
one’s food comes from and how it was cultivated.  
More people are cooking.  
There is a recognition that both food and energy  
consumption are directly related to C02 footprints and  
climate change.  
As energy prices rise more people are interested  
in both alternative energy sources and alternative  
energy grids.  
This studio will consider each of the three topics above in the  
context of a New York City neighborhood. We are interested in  
how the neighborhood of the future will be built and how it will  
operate. This is a projective studio, our goal will be to design the  
neighborhood of the future.  
We will work in groups in this studio. Looking at two neighborhoods  
in New York City, we will divide topics between groups for a deep  
research phase. If you have a strong objection to group work,  
please let me know.  
Research Phase:  
As with most design studios, we will begin with a research phase.  
During this phase, students will dive deep into their topic areas,  
researching the history of each topic as it relates to New York City  
and our focus neighborhoods specifically. We want granularity in  
this research in order to really understand the make up of a  
neighborhood. To achieve these results, we’ll use open source  
data available from the city and other organizations. Synthesizing  
information from this data sometimes required persistent effort. We  
will supplement this data with public reports and studies.  
During the research phase students will be introduced to various  
softwares designed to synthesize and visualize data sets. As  
we research and analyze, the ability to visualize our work will  
strengthen our arguments. The ultimate goal of the research phase  
is to produce a web-based dashboard in which users can view the  
neighborhood and results of your research.  
The research phase of studio at CASE may be longer and deeper  
than those in your other studios. This will not be at the expense of  
your design work. The deeper and more convincing your research,  
the clearer and more compelling your design work will be.  
Sites:  
We will look at two sites in New York City. One site is located in the  
financial district of lower Manhattan. This site contains legacy office  
buildings from the 20th century, some of which are undergoing  
transformation to residential use. The neighborhood is in transition  
as its core business of commercial rental evolves.  
Our second site is a rather typical neighborhood in Brooklyn. It  
contains several blocks of brownstones, some of which have been  
divide and others that are single family unit. Though it appears  
tranquil walking through this neighborhood, it too is undergoing  
change as the habits of its residents evolve.  
Program:  
The program of the studio is to design the neighborhood of the  
future, with heavy emphasis upon our primary research topics. The  
identified neighborhood boundaries are quite small, our goal will  
be to go into considerable detail on these sites.  
Group Work:  
We will work in groups during the research and design phases  
of this studio. Group work is essential to your development as a  
student and as a professional. If you have concerns about group  
work, please discuss with me directly.  
Final Models:  
This studio will produce a final model of our two sites. Each model  
will incorporate contributions from each group, collaboration will  
be key with these models. These models will have a high level of  
detail.  
Final Drawings:  
Final drawings from this studio will include both representational  
drawings - plans, sections isometrics - as well as systems  
diagrams. We will be designing new urban systems, to be  
explained in diagram, the outgrowth of which will be our  
architectural proposals.

## Learning Outcomes

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes (CLOs):

- Research 1a abilty to use / develop Specialized knowledge: (B.Arch+M.Arch) Demonstrate specialized knowledge in an infrastructural or building system. Demonstrate knowledge of the infrastructural or building system's history and current state of the art. Demonstrate an ability to benchmark the performance of an existing infrastructural or building system. (M.Arch) Demonstrate an ability to represent this system through multiple forms of diagraming. 1b abilty to use / develop broad and integrative knowledge: (B.Arch+M.Arch) Ability to make connections between broad research concepts/systems and tie that information to a specific site. Demonstrate an ability to assess the way in which urban or global characteristics can impact a specific site and/or building. (M.Arch) Demonstrate an ability to represent the way in which multiple infrastructural or building systems interact and have altered affects on a specific site.
- Intellectual Design Skills 2a ability to design (B.Arch+M.Arch) Ability to propose a design that is directly responsive to previously conducted research. Ability to propose a project that incorporates influence from multiple stakeholders. (M.Arch) Ability to propose a project that fully considers post-occupancy impact on both users and the surrounding site. 2b. ability to design using information resources (B.Arch+M.Arch) Ability to gather and represent data, and use data as a design driver. Ability to use data to benchmark existing and proposed building/site performance. (M.Arch) Ability to propose a novel building system and estimate its performance based on existing data and proposed changes. 2c ability to design engaging diverse perspectives (B.Arch+M.Arch) Ability to propose a project that is sensitive to all relevant stakeholders as well as historic and potential future impacts the site/project will have on its surrounding community. 2d. ability to design with ethical reasoning (B.Arch+M.Arch) Ability estimate a project's impact on its surrounding community and incorporate community benifit as a design driver. (M.Arch) Ability to estimate the carbon footprint impact of a proposed novel building system. 2e. ability to design with quantitative fluency (B.Arch+M.Arch) Ability to benchmark existing and proposed impacts and benefits of the project/site. (M.Arch) Ability to use proposed impact analysis and a driver for design revision. 2f. ability to design with communicative fluency (B.Arch+M.Arch) Ability to communicate design intention through diagram, drawing, and verbal presentation. Ability to present a convincing case for a project through demonstration of extensive subject matter knowledge.
- Collaboration Ability to work with a partner and collaborate with the entire studio for the duration of the semester.
- Civic and Global Engagement Demonstrate the connection of a project to the larger community and world by incorporating these pressures, discovered during the research phase, as design drivers. Ability to show quantitative analysis of the way in which a project can respond to global demands.
- Design Execution (B.Arch+M.Arch) Ability to design a project that incorporates multiple stakeholders, research interests, quantitative analysis as well as personal preference. (M.Arch) Ability to identify where particular influences exist in a project and how the project impact might change if the ratio of each influence was to change.

### Academic Policies:

- ### Academic Integrity

  Student-teacher relationships are built on trust. For example, students must trust that teachers have made appropriate decisions about the structure and content of the courses they teach, and teachers must trust that the assignments that students turn in are their own. Acts that violate this trust undermine the educational process. The Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities and The Graduate Student Supplement define various forms of Academic Dishonesty and you should make yourself familiar with these. In this class, all assignments that are turned in for a grade must represent the student’s own work. In cases where help was received, or teamwork was allowed, a notation on the assignment should indicate your collaboration.

  Violations of academic integrity may also be reported to the appropriate Dean (Dean of Students for undergraduate students or the Dean of Graduate Education for graduate students, respectively).

  If you have any question concerning this policy before submitting an assignment, please ask for clarification. In addition, you can visit the following site for more information on our Academic Integrity Policy: [Students Rights, Responsibilities, and Judicial Affairs](https://info.rpi.edu/dean-students/student-rights-responsibilities-and-judicial-affairs).

### Additional Academic Integrity Course Policy and Penalty Information:

Policy on collaboration and cheating/plagiarism:  
Interdisciplinary collaboration is a critical component of this course. All assignments that are turned in for a grade must accurately represent the student’s own work, and properly cite authorship of work that was not solely completed by the student. Any miss-representation of work shall be evaluated and could result in a failing grade for the assignment. A subsequent infraction  
by the student will result in a failing grade for the course.
