# GENERATION SHIP Æ: Architecture, Cities, Ecologies, World-Trees, Planets, Starships

RPI Architecture VERTICAL Studio — 14‑Week Syllabus

Instructor: Ed Keller Semester: Fall 25 Studio

Banner / concept image: Orrery‑baroque cosmos (studio anchor). Use as cover plate and as an analytic diagram study in Week 1/2.

## Epigraphs

“I believe that if I were commissioned to design a new universe, I would be mad enough to undertake it.” — Giovanni Battista Piranesi

“Shall I project a world?” — Oedipa Maas

You have received a single, high-stakes commission: conceive a thousand-year city-ship—part living holobiont, part culture-engine—able to leave, wander, and return without losing its soul.

This studio asks you to design a generation ship—and simultaneously to treat architecture and cities as ships and minds. This could be a building, a city, a spaceship, a starship, or even an entire planet imagined as a complex system with governance, metabolism, culture, and memory.

If the generation ship is the speculative vessel through which human futures are carried, then architecture and cities are its terrestrial precursors. Built form and urban fabric act as slow-motion pocket universes: containers of culture, metabolism, and collective memory, engineered to outlast their makers. Cathedrals, pyramids, or arcologies encode temporalities longer than any individual life, binding communities across centuries. Cities enact ark-logics of survival through ecological adaptation and infrastructural resilience.

Architecture, infrastructure, and polity are one organism: repairable, evolvable, and legible to descendants. To read architecture and urbanism as generation ship is to recognize that every gesture, event, emotion, wall, canal, or dome is an interface between past and future, staging continuity amidst entropy. They are not simply shelters but long-haul vessels of thought and survival, cradling civilizations as they drift through time.

The studio synthesizes planetary systems and world‑building (Buckminster Fuller, Piranesi, Reyner Banham), land ethics (Aldo Leopold), and speculative culture (Ursula K. Le Guin, Kim Stanley Robinson) into a design practice that aligns energy, information, and meaning.

We will work with Æ — the world tree—as mythotechnical scaffold and diagram of care: roots in geology, trunk in architecture, canopy in civic life, mycelial and stellar branches across timescales. Freeman Dyson’s tree starship inspires our material program: living structure, evolved to carry passengers and knowledge through deep time and space, with closed-loop ecologies, and spin-gravity or gradient strategies to stabilize bodies and biomes.

Two key concepts can guide the project:

- Artifact — what endures: material assemblies, protocols, archives, habitats; architectures that can be repaired, recomposed, and reinterpreted across centuries.
- Gesture — how worlds speak: signals, rituals, laws and interfaces for multi‑species and multi‑intelligence collaboration (human/animal/vegetal/machinic), including sound and movement as design media.

Ethics. Intergenerational justice is primary. Every system answers to the unborn: maintainability beats novelty; rights of ecosystems and non-human persons are constitutional; governance is fork-able but accountable. We specify contact and non-interference protocols, de-escalation by design, and anti-predatory economies. Failure modes default to safe, local autonomy; memory systems include “ritual resets” to prevent drift into authoritarianism or entropy. The ark must be able to refuse extraction—and still sing.

Philosophy. A generation ship is a pocket universe with ethics. It must encode continuity without stasis—designing rhythms of memory, innovation, and dissent. We treat the ark as a mind that dreams itself forward: fractal redundancies, multi-umwelt signage legible to humans, AI, and plant life; narrative and sonic ecologies that stabilize identity across centuries. “As above, so below” governs: each cabin mirrors the ship; the ship mirrors the cosmos. Our aim: prototype a non‑predatory system ecology [of minds] capable of lasting thousands of years.

## Learning Outcomes

By the end of the semester, these may be your skills and accomplishments:

1. Conceptually and Spatially Model a closed‑loop habitat as a coupled system of energy, material, temporal, and cultural flows.
2. Design architectural topologies for endurance and repair across 1,000‑year horizons (phasing, redundancy, stewardship).
3. Prototype interfaces of gesture (sound, movement, ritual) for cross‑species communication and governance.
4. Articulate an ethical framework for non‑predatory exchange and ecological reciprocity at building, urban, and ship scales.

Compose a clear narrative & evidentiary atlas (drawings, models, simulation, and writing) sufficient for intersubjective critique and future stewardship.

### Additional Academic Integrity Course Policy and Penalty Information:

Statement of Academic Integrity

The Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities defines various forms of Academic Dishonesty and procedures for responding to them. Academic dishonesty violates the trust between students and teachers. Students must trust that teachers have made appropriate decisions about the structure and content of the courses they teach. Teachers must trust that the assignments students turn in are their own performance. Violations of this trust undermines the educational process. Cheating, plagiarism (defined as the lack of attribution or citation of the work of others), willful damage or theft of another person’s work violate academic integrity. The first instance of the breach of this trust will merit a failing grade in this course and the violator(s) will be reported to the Dean of Students for disciplinary action.  
Students engaging in plagiarism will be asked to redo their projects and failing to do so, will automatically be a F in the course.
