## Course Content

### Content Details:

**Spolia-Spolia** Design Seminar

**Course Overview**

Spolia, spolia-spolia, spolia over spolia over spolia over spolia, perpetually unfolding.

01 Spolia (from the latin *spolium*: spoils) are recognizable architectural elements (or groups of elements, or parts of elements, or groups of parts of elements), often made of stone, that are encountered and/or looted from old structures and used anew for the purpose of introducing allegorical ornamentation in an altogether different architectural artifact, celebrating, establishing dominion or appropriation of, resonating with, or overshadowing their provenance, and fundamentally carrying within them multiple elusive meanings that are projected as enigmas. Spolia is applied onto a building that in principle operates as a passive background to its figural presence. And yet, figure and background compete in a struggle of equals, often making it difficult to recognize which piece works as a figure onto which one working as a background. More boldly, spolia constitute material organizations that, through a profanation process that involves the evaluation, recognition, decontextualization, segregation, categorization, organization, and repositioning of elements, migrate from one building into another, triggering and putting at work a perpetually incomplete process of architectural transformation. Spolia carry (or rather, miscarry), through this migration process, an active form of knowledge (that is, a form of knowledge that *transforms* matter), turning the latent capabilities of an architectural material (deliberately distorted by the migration process) into an actual architectural force and a source of a new form of sense that transfers vital and cultural impulses and messages from object to object. Spolia is live material, whose inertness is overtly challenged, recharged, turned into a force, and effectively made a vector of change. Spolia, thus, does not simply decorate a piece of architecture that persist as it used to be, but rather transforms the architecture itself of the building into which it is embedded. In its subtle versions, it incorporates disturbances that, because of their isolation from their context, create sparks that trigger an entire revolution in the concept of the piece of architecture. And, in its wilder ones, it redefines its very organization.

02 Spolia-spolia. The incorporation of spolia into an already spoiled building structure introduces, in this context, a second level of ornamentation (the ornamentation of an ornamentation) and/or of transformation (the transformation of a transformation). This involves that the simple (and often ambiguous) figure-background relation of the single spolia is turned into a self-contradictory manifold of figure-background and figure-figure relations that alternate or overlap, thus disrupting the status of both figure and background, which then turn into one another in an inscrutable manner. A spolia-spolia operation blurs altogether the distinction between background and figure. It is not a matter of relative dominance, but a matter of mutual consecutiveness. What comes before and after, if one becomes the other in a process? What is fundamentally lost here, because of this manifold of ambiguities, is the background itself, the plane of support, the canvas, which happens to set back in direct proportion to the degree in which the uncertainty of the compound grows. This loss creates a point of no return to the source, by which, when iterated, the transformation process is turned into a loop of lineages (and not only multiple segments) of differentiation, flattening the historical process lying behind the linear idea of architectural evolution, and turning it into an instantaneous action where history happens all at once and keeps looping, as if in a limbo, thus squeezing the temporal unfolding of architecture as a discipline into the sudden objectual condition of a single building artifact. The spolia-spolia complex can be thus seen as a strong meta-authorial trans-historical act, the consistency of which is artificial and deliberate, and the form of which can be intensified, manipulated, turned upside down, looped back, skipped over itself, and become redundant at wish. The content of this perpetual act frees allegories from any remnant of a linear historical determinism. Its structural artificiality opens the process of differentiation into the procedural real and flips the dependence of architecture both on the mandates of linear historical narratives and on the dependence of figure-ground compositional methods.

03 Indexicality. The seminar explores, on the one hand, the unfolding of the spolia-spolia method as a mode to create architectural organizations and spaces through iterative indexical differentiation by means of the montage of architectural images (figures, in principle) onto architectural images (backgrounds, in principle), and on the other, the redescription of montage as a vectorial system that simulates etching techniques and computes traits through its scribbling patterns. A series of more-or-less complex but discrete organizational materials, or debris, coming from different ancient Roman ruins, as portrayed by Giovanni Battista Piranesi, are cut off and pulled out of their provenance and indexed in the formal and spatial organization of an etching of an architectural form, as depicted by the same author. This etching works as a background (or rather, a battlefield) of a non-linear iterative process of transformation. For this purpose, the iterative indexical process operates according to a sequential accumulative mode, redefining the architectural form by saturating it with foreign organizational modalities. Each spolia iteration consists of the identification of the architectural debris displayed in an etching, a quick analysis of alternative variations of the form, the construction of a taxonomy of the variety of debris figures, the selection of one of them under a hypothesis of transformation, the recognition of systematic opportunities in the background form that carry potential transformative ideas, the mapping of those, and the systematic running of the debris figures across them, populating the background according to roughly defined rules, first with the collage of images, and then indexing the patterns of those in the pattern of the background, thus transforming it. Iterations accumulate onto the spatial-organizational vectorial configuration, increasing its saturation while avoiding the erasure of previous operations, and rather complexifying their spectrum of sense. Each iteration works both as an autonomous operation, adding a new layer to the organization, and cumulatively, having the new layer relate to the previous state of things of the organization, rather than to the alleged purity of the initial form. The autonomy of the indexical operations resets the sequential order, intertwining the iterative linearity of the process of accumulation.

04 Vectoriality. The background vectorial etching is constructed based on a background etching as drawn by Giovanni Battista Piranesi, and consists of a singular architectural form, focused on a bold architectural form or on an ambiguous interior-exterior spatial atmosphere, in both cases constructed as a conventional human-centric perspective at eye’s level with one or two vanishing points. These are selected from the pool of forms investigated in the Studio as the materials to be integrated in the construction of Archetype-Models. However, in the seminar the background etching works as the only material to be transformed individually by the process of spolia-spolia iterations. The construction of vectorial systems that redraw the background form is framed by the configuration of a patchwork of territories, each of which contains a consistent pattern. The boundaries between these territories can be shared, separate, or intertwined, generating adjacencies between patterns, overlaps that create more complex ones, or voids between them. Patterns are redrawn without variations of line weight or darkness, emphasizing the understanding of the abstract linear organizations through control point systems that characterize their formal and organizational particularity. Patterns are, in this context, drawn as samples, then conceptualized as control systems, and finally run systematically across their bounded territories. Debris, in turn, are first run through these patterns by means of collage techniques, then they are constructed via a similar vectorial procedure, and finally they are indexed into the background pattern through the complexification of the organization. The aim of the spolia-spolia process is to enrich the boldness of the form by recognizing the transformative power of indexing organizational modalities to stretch its capabilities. The created image-based indexical vectorial differentiation therefore works both as one among many lineages of possible systematic variations of an Archetype-Model and as a lighthouse that guides potential transformations, consequently insinuating their horizon.

The work of the Design Seminar is individual, although it resonates in the context of the work of Design Studio teams, using the same images of the same etchings by Piranesi that operate as a material for the development of Archetype-Models, here dissected as divergent representations that contain potential lineages taking part in the same family. The resulting material is thus complementary to that produced by the Design Studio.

**Material**

The working platform of the Seminar consists of two pools of materials: Forms and Debris. Each pool is organized in six groups, in correspondence to the six teams of the studio: Art Forumdome, Fashion Forumdome, Gastronomy Forumdome, Landscape Forumdome, Religion Forumdome, Waterscape Forumdome (there are no Venues framing the individual work of the team members, as the work of the seminar is purely formal-organizational-representational rather than programmatically driven). Within each group, the pool is organized in two sets of three, in correspondence to each student in the team, who will work with one for each of the two Spolia-Spolia iterations.

01 Forms consists of eighteen poignant architectural case studies from the Ancient Roman period, as presented by Giovanni Battista Piranesi in his etchings. These case studies are seen as raw materials whose patchwork organization and etching patterns are used as a background, and as the basis for the development of the architectural proposition. Form case studies are organized in six groups of three, in correspondence to students in teams. Individual students select the same case as in the Design Studio.

02 Debris consists of thirty-six types of medium-to-small-scale components from the Ancient Roman period, as found in Giovanni Battista Piranesi’s etchings. These are seen as varieties and variations of architectural elements, which are indexed, superficially stamped, and systemically embedded in the Forms. Debris case studies are organized in six groups of two types, each having three case studies, in correspondence to students in teams. Individual students select one case of two debris for their indexing.

## Learning Outcomes

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes (CLOs):

- 01 Research 01a_Ability to use/develop specialized knowledge Students live in Rome throughout the semester. Through the direct interaction with canonical architectural pieces, the involvement with urban conditions and phenomena, and the fluid access to museums, artwork, and bibliographical resources in the city, students augment their disciplinary baggage and construct an ability to attentively observe and document multiple forms and scales of knowledge about buildings, cultural artifacts and sites that nurtures their imagination of the architectural project with specific observations. 01b_Ability to use / develop broad and integrative knowledge Students live in Rome throughout the semester, engaging in a culturally immersive process that, aside primarily consisting of regular visits to Renaissance and Baroque buildings in the city center and of participating in real time in the cultural life of the city in the context of its omnipresent history, is fostered by a series of field trips to the outskirts of the city, to Tuscany, and to Sicily, providing a rich and multilayered context of historical and cultural significance to the architectural projects, which deepens the referentiality of their projects.
- 02 Intellectual-visual design skills 02a_Ability to design with imaginative openness Students are conceptually and visually nurtured to develop an ability to ground design decisions on a hands-on visual approach to architecture as material. This approach is articulated by visually studying and literally using the organizational knowledge embedded in existing buildings as sources of design intelligence. 02b_Ability to design with communicative fluency Students are challenged to fluidly communicate with (and be influenced by) different interlocutors at different stages of the course, from local professors of various backgrounds to student colleagues of other universities in the Rome Center, to guest professors that participate in reviews and the lecture series program.
- 03 Design execution 03a_Midterm Review Students demonstrate the ability to implement the learning outcomes of an iterative process of image-based design in a sophisticated and compelling way through a mid-term presentation to guest jurors and an intermediate submission of the production, including: a portfolio presentation and submission containing organizational descriptions, photographic surveys, image gathering and manipulation, and an archive of source material. 03b_Final Review Students demonstrate the ability to implement the learning outcomes of an iterative process of image-based design in a sophisticated and compelling way through a final presentation to guest jurors and an intermediate submission of the production, including: a portfolio presentation and submission containing organizational descriptions, photographic surveys, image organization and classification, and an archive of source material.

**Seminar Policy**

**Pin-ups**

Students are periodically required to present their work in a formal review process. Presentations are a critical part of the architectural education. They are opportunities to judge the project and critically evaluate the design process. It may be appropriate at points in the semester to evaluate only selected projects to convey information relevant to the entire class. In this situation, it is important for each student to witness and participate in the review process. Even if his/her project is not reviewed, the criticism and discussions inform the evaluation of his/her progress and design. Failure to attend one or more pin-ups for the full class period may result in a grade deduction.

**Desk Critiques**

The Seminar environment involves two-way communication. The role of the Seminar professor is to evolve and strengthen the student’s design process. Every time the student meets with the faculty, he/she must have new iterations of work based on previous conversations. If the student has not produced new work, the faculty reserves the right to skip the critique and continue with other students who have produced new material.

### Additional Academic Integrity Course Policy and Penalty Information:

**Academic Integrity**

The Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities defines various forms of Academic Dishonesty and procedures for responding to them. Academic dishonesty violates the trust between students and teachers. Students must trust that teachers have made appropriate decisions about the structure and content of the courses they teach. Teachers must trust that the assignments students turn in are their own performance. Violations of this trust undermines the educational process.

**Policy on collaboration and cheating/plagiarism**

Cheating, plagiarism (defined as the lack of attribution or citation of the work of others), willful damage or theft of another person’s work violate academic integrity. The first instance of the breach of this trust merits a failing grade in this course and the violator will be reported to the Dean of Students for disciplinary action.

Interdisciplinary collaboration is a critical component of the course. All assignments that are turned in for a grade must accurately represent the student’s own work and properly cite authorship of work that was not solely completed by the student. Any misrepresentation of work is evaluated and could result in a failing grade for the assignment. A subsequent infraction by the student results in a failing grade for the course.
