## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

Students in this course will complete an in-depth study of current peer reviewed literature and topics in the field of ecology and environmental biology. Students will read and critique peer-reviewed papers and present and lead discussions on peer-reviewed articles. Topics covered will include populations and biological communities, biodiversity, ecosystem structures and habitats, climate change, and ecosystem attributes

## Course Text(s)

### Text Details:

There is no textbook requirement. We will read and discuss at least one peer-reviewed article per class meeting.

## Course Goals

### Goals:

The goal of this course is to familiarize students with the current research in ecology and environmental biology. Students will learn to analyze and lead discussion on peer-reviewed articles and critically evaluate scientific methods, including experiments, surveys, and statistical and process-based modeling.

## Learning Outcomes

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes (CLOs):

- 1\. Demonstrate mastery in reading and critically reviewing primary scientific literature in ecology and environmental biology by formulating written critiques of articles and summarizing the key points of selected articles. a. Remembering: Recall advanced principles and theories from the assigned papers and broader discipline. b. Understanding: Explain/Describe/Analyze complex concepts and methodologies presented in assigned papers. c. Analyzing: Analyze peer-reviewed articles to extract and summarize key points effectively.
- 2\. Articulate multiple objective evaluations of selected articles in class discussion, supported by facts found in the selected articles, scientifically grounded perspectives, and perspectives found in other competing studies. a. Analyzing: Analyze multiple perspectives presented in peer-reviewed articles and other competing studies. b. Evaluating: Evaluate the strengths and weaknesses of selected articles from various perspectives. c. Creating: Create comprehensive written critiques of articles that articulate multiple objective evaluations supported by facts and diverse perspectives.
- 3\. Effectively present peer-reviewed papers and facilitate discussion with others in the class with an eye toward critical review of selected articles. a. Applying: Apply advanced communication skills to present complex peer-reviewed papers to peers. b. Analyzing: Analyze peer-reviewed papers to identify nuanced points for discussion and critical review. c. Creating: Create effective presentations and summaries to effectively convey advanced topics to peers through effectively stimulating thought-provoking discussions.
- 4\. Demonstrate the ability to evaluate a peer-reviewed study based on aspects such as its quality and applicability, especially in context of potentially conflicting papers, and defend their review to an audience of their peers to teach them how to synthesis arguments. a. Analyzing: Analyze peer-reviewed studies in depth to evaluate their quality, relevance, and applicability. b. Evaluating: Evaluate conflicting perspectives within peer-reviewed studies and defend their reviews with comprehensive arguments. c. Creating: Create sophisticated arguments to defend their reviews of peer-reviewed studies and guide peers in synthesizing arguments effectively.
- 5\. Be able to describe the differences in peer-reviewed article structure, format, journal type, and impact factor to evaluate article and journal quality and identify potentially important peer-reviewed articles. a. Understanding: Describe and explain clear and nuanced differences in peer-reviewed article structure, format, journal type, and impact factor. b. Applying: Apply advanced knowledge of article characteristics to critically evaluate article and journal quality.

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes Assessment Measures:

Students in both 4500 and 650 will be assessed by participation in class discussion, completing written reviews, and presenting/leading class discussion at least once. Students in the 4500 course will complete written reviews of at least two peer-reviewed papers. Students in the 6500 course will complete written reviews of at least four peer-reviewed papers.

After each review, students receive feedback on their assessment of the work. Reviews will be graded by the instructor. For their presentation and leading discussion, students will choose papers in consultation with the course instructor. After each presentation students will receive feedback on their performance. Presentations will be graded by the instructor. Participation in class discussion will utilize both oral contributions during class time as well as summaries of articles on LMS due the night before class. A grading rubric for presenting and leading discussion and description for how participation will be evaluated is provided below.

No grades will be dropped, and no form of “resurrection” is built into the grading system, but expectations for written reviews and in-class discussions will be lower at the beginning of the semester and increase as students gain experience and better understand expectations.

This class is intended to be a highly interactive learning course. Attendance will be taken and participation is essential for successfully completing the course. Each missed class after the first two will result in the loss of half a letter grade. All students are expected to read the assigned papers before class and be prepared to discuss them in detail.

## Grading Criteria

### Criteria Details:

All students will be evaluated based on (1) class participation (40%), written review of peer-reviewed papers (30%) and presenting/leading discussion (30%). Discussion presentations will be evaluated as described below. After each discussion presentation, the student discussion leader will meet with the instructor to obtain feedback on their performance. Presentations will be graded by the instructor.

For students in 4500:

- A: The presentation/paper review provided a general background on the scientific question or hypothesis that is raised by the paper. Figures are assessed appropriately (e.g. reviewing individual panels important to the findings and removing panels that are not central to the paper). The presenter/reviewer has an informed understanding of the motivation, methods, interpretation for each experiment, and how they relate to the over-arching scientific question.
- A-: The presentation/paper reviews or discussion leading fell short in one of the elements described above.
- B+: There are important but not major or critical flaws in one of the described areas, but others are achieved well.
- B: There are multiple problems or flaws with the presentation/paper reviews.
- B-: The presentation/paper reviews had major important flaws in at least half of the above-described categories.
- C: The presentation/paper reviews had major and important flaws in most the above-described categories.
- D: The presentation/paper reviews had major and critical flaws in all or nearly all of the above-described categories.
- F: The student did not present/review papers.

For students in 6500:

- A: The presentation/paper review provided a thorough and detailed background on the scientific question or hypothesis that is raised by the paper. Figures are assessed appropriately and completely (e.g. reviewing individual panels important to the findings and removing panels that are not central to the paper). The presenter/reviewer has an in-depth and advanced analysis of the motivation, methods, interpretation for each experiment, and how they relate to the over-arching scientific question and broader literature on the topic.
- A-: The presentation/paper reviews or discussion leading fell just short in one of the elements described above.
- B+: There are important but not major or critical flaws in one of the described areas, but others are achieved well.
- B: There are multiple problems with the presentation/paper reviews.
- B-: The presentation/paper reviews had major and important flaws in many of the above-described categories.
- C: The presentation/paper reviews had major flaws in most or all of the above-described categories.
- F: The student did not present/review papers.

Note courses at the 6000 levels may not receive +D/D/-D grades.

In addition to the requirement of each student leading/presenting discussion on at least one peer-reviewed paper, students will be evaluated each class based on their contribution to discussion. These evaluations will be graded for each class and averaged across all classes in the semester to generate a participation grade toward the final grade. Evaluation will be based on the following contributions during class (i.e., oral contributions to discussion) and submission of written summaries the night before class. All students are required to submit written summaries via LMS. Evaluation of oral and written contributions will be based on the following:

1. Has the student demonstrated that they have read and understood the assigned paper, such as through asking questions, answering a question, or providing an insight into the paper topic? (50%)
2. Has the student demonstrated their comprehension of the topic covered in the assigned reading, such as through the content for their question or a perspective they shared on the assigned reading? (50%)

Students may appeal grades by emailing the course instructor to discuss each grade. Students may only appeal any grades received to the instructor within one week of receipt of the grade. Questions about grades or scientific issues must be submitted in writing with the assignment in question to the instructor. A missed or late assignment will be docked one letter grade for every week late. Final decisions about grading rest with the instructor. Students may monitor their course progress by checking their grades on RPILMS and at a time to be announced during the semester.

### Academic Policies:

- ### Academic Integrity

  Student-teacher relationships are built on trust. For example, students must trust that teachers have made appropriate decisions about the structure and content of the courses they teach, and teachers must trust that the assignments that students turn in are their own. Acts that violate this trust undermine the educational process. The Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities and The Graduate Student Supplement define various forms of Academic Dishonesty and you should make yourself familiar with these. In this class, all assignments that are turned in for a grade must represent the student’s own work. In cases where help was received, or teamwork was allowed, a notation on the assignment should indicate your collaboration.

  Violations of academic integrity may also be reported to the appropriate Dean (Dean of Students for undergraduate students or the Dean of Graduate Education for graduate students, respectively).

  If you have any question concerning this policy before submitting an assignment, please ask for clarification. In addition, you can visit the following site for more information on our Academic Integrity Policy: [Students Rights, Responsibilities, and Judicial Affairs](https://info.rpi.edu/dean-students/student-rights-responsibilities-and-judicial-affairs).
