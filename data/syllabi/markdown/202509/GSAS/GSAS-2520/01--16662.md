## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

Introduction to Game Storytelling provides an overview of the art of effective story development. This course explores foundational narrative techniques and principles in games and other media, providing hands-on exercises for students to develop their ideas into story-driven games using beginner-friendly tools. Students will begin by writing short stories and creating text-based games, progressively advancing to projects that require a broader, interdisciplinary skill set.

## Course Text(s)

### Text Details:

Into the Woods. (2014). John Yorke.

Interactive Storytelling for Video Games. (2011). Josiah Lebowitz and Chris Klug.

Story: Substance, Structure, Style and the Principles of Screenwriting. (1997). Robert McKee.

## Course Goals

### Goals:

- Learn the basic principles, techniques, and methods of game storytelling, including story structure, worldbuilding, environmental design, branching narratives, and more.
- Develop short narrative games using beginner-friendly tools that do not require prior programming experience.
- Analyze and critique narrative strategies used in video games, with the ability to apply these techniques to your own projects.
- Gain insight into the role of a narrative designer in game development, including how writers, artists, and programmers collaborate to create narrative-driven games.

## Learning Outcomes

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes (CLOs):

- Develop narrative games using tools like Twine and Unity.
- Understand plot models and write short stories with these models.
- Understand and implement narrative techniques and methods in game design.
- Explore storytelling techniques from other media and adapt them for games.

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes Assessment Measures:

Students will be assessed by writing and game projects.

- ### Academic Integrity

  Student-teacher relationships are built on trust. For example, students must trust that teachers have made appropriate decisions about the structure and content of the courses they teach, and teachers must trust that the assignments that students turn in are their own. Acts that violate this trust undermine the educational process. The Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities and The Graduate Student Supplement define various forms of Academic Dishonesty and you should make yourself familiar with these.
