## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

This course provides a set of genealogies, discourses, and trajectories of a collection of major concepts in game studies, as a way of helping students to form a conceptual basis for comprehending current configurations and past practices in the field of game studies. The word “concept” is commonly used across the humanities and social sciences, yet is also difficult to define in its entirety. We can think of “concepts” as terms, phrases, and vocabulary that provide games scholars with tools for identifying and interpreting empirical and philosophical elements in game texts, and in clarifying and comparing social processes in play and production across space, time, and culture. Concepts are core words that allow scholarly communities to communicate with one another.

However, concepts are also impossible to totally disentangle from popular and scholarly history and politics. When we a student to discuss the “meaning” of a game text in one of our intro classes, for example, we are almost certainly asking them something different than if one of their friends asked them what they thought a game “meant.” Not only can colloquial-scholarly linguistic overlap muddle conceptual communication, the need for specialized scholarly vocabulary in game studies, like “biopolitical,” or “ergodic,” or “assemblage,” may at first appear to new scholars as opaque, or as jargon.

However, in the cases of both colloquial and specialized terminology, concepts contain within them deep strata and genealogies of thought. Deploying specific conceptual language entangles the author and the reader with the prior arguments, findings, and theories from which the concept emerged. Further, game studies has been pursued from myriad disciplinary and adisciplinary perspectives; our concepts are drawn from such fields as literary studies, anthropology, cultural studies, media studies, history, psychology, art history, sociology, and STS. Such hybridity can result in overlapping-but-sometimes-contested conceptual frameworks used in games research. The term “agency,” for example, so often used in game studies, implies different analytical meaning in some of the constituent disciplines that contribute to our field. Literary studies and sociology both employ the term, and share some common intellectual heritage on the origins of its use. But “agency” as a concept has developed on very distinct—and in some cases, incompatible—trajectories in literature and in sociology over the past 60 years. When a scholar at a game studies conference, or in one of our journals, uses the term, how do we know how to parse it? When a concept bounces back and forth between disciplines that use it differently, how do we track—and perhaps even critique—that messiness?

Part of the work we will do together is in tracing how particular concepts put to use in game studies emerge, are deployed, change, and are critiqued. At the same time, this course steers clear of naming “core” concepts or “canonical” texts in game studies. While many of the concepts and texts discussed will be well known in the field, the class’s disciplinary agnosticism is part of a commitment to ensuring that you acquire a comprehensive grasp of our interdisciplinary field, which works in synergistic relationship with other knowledge formations. My attempt is always to clarify the genealogies, configurations, discourses, situations, and trajectories of Game Studies without prescribing yours. We read “foundational”/“classic” selections, and “postfoundational” texts. The practical goal is intense immersion in a variety of game studies concepts that you might encounter in our field, and a deeper understanding of how concepts form, what concepts in game studies “do,” and how they are borrowed across disciplinary and interdisciplinary boundaries.

This class will thus require you to engage in deep interpretive scholarly work. In interpretive work, reading and writing operate together and recursively—the act and sharing of writing becomes a key part of how we sharpen our conceptual skills. Writing is not the end result of research—i.e., “thinking a lot and then submitting a final paper”—but rather a community effort of constant pushes, pulls, and tweaks. This class will embody this method, and will feature a consistent communal writing, sharing, and co-lecturing process for you to develop your conceptual deftness throughout the semester.

## Learning Outcomes

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes (CLOs):

- • Students will demonstrate comprehensive grasp of key conceptual developments in the field of Game Studies through reflexive reading and discussion of key texts reviewing classic and recent literature in the field and its constitutive disciplines.
- • Students will demonstrate fluency by facilitating discussion to contextualize, compare, contrast, and think critically about approaches in game studies.
- • Students will demonstrate the ability to synthesize and reflect upon assigned readings, lectures, and classroom discussion in written formats.
- • Students will be able to independently research, assess, contextualize, and historicize conceptual tools in game studies in written and oral form.

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes Assessment Measures:

**Weekly Prompts (each due twice throughout the semester):**

Throughout the semester, students will rotate in leading four weekly distinct student discussion prompts, which will combine with the work of other students to develop a weekly student-led class discussion. The four prompts each week require a different kind of engagement across the week’s assigned texts, and each prompt requires a different amount of work, including varied amounts of writing. The prompts are:

Summary: The summary is a mid-level overview of the arguments and structures of each of the assigned readings for the week. The student running the summary should prepare a 15-20 minute presentation that helps the class set a baseline of understanding of the content of each paper, as well as helping to clarify terms and approaches used in the reading. When preparing the summary of each reading, the student should be sure to cover:

- The author of the reading, and their institutional, disciplinary, and research context
  - o Who is/are the author(s)? What formal and informal trainings do they have? If they have a Ph.D., from what program at what university? Who was their advisor? Are they associated with any particular schools of thought or approaches?
- The major argument(s) of the reading
- Key terms, concepts, theories, and prior authors drawn upon in the text
- The intervention the paper attempts to make in the field or in multiple fields (note, depending on the reading, the “field” may not be game studies—for some papers, game studies as we think of it today may not have existed yet!)

Synthesis **(10 points each)****:** The synthesis is a critical, 1500 word written reflection on the readings, from which you will present in the class where those readings are discussed. Rather than summarizing the articles, synthesizers read across the articles to trace themes, arguments, connections, and dis-connections. In working across the readings, the synthesizer should reflect upon how each paper advances, challenges, re-positions, or re-reads the conceptual theme of the week. How, if at all, the readings directly or indirectly engage with one another, and/or are they drawing any core theoretical, empirical, textual, or scholarly resources in common? Are they drawing upon those sources in the same way? The synthesizer should also attempt to cross-contextualize the readings—are there disciplinary differences, or time differences, or field development differences that seem to be *making a difference* in the arguments the papers are making, and the kinds of evidence they are using to make that argument?

You must turn in the 1500 word paper on which your 15-20-minute presentation should be based. All of your written work for this class should be polished—i.e. I expect not a draft but a finished piece of academic writing, on the basis of which you present your reflections to class. You may use presentation software, but if so, that too should be turned in. Your paper should be emailed as a PDF to the professor and to the rest of the class before 11:59 PM Eastern time Wednesday nights before class, to give the rest of the class the opportunity to read your synthesis before the beginning of the class session.

Provocation **(10 points each)****:** On the date for which you sign up, you are responsible for introducing materials to provoke thinking and facilitate discussion for half of the 3 hour class period (factoring in about an hour for professor introductory remarks, the summary and synthesis presentation, and leaving about half an hour for the resolution discussion at the end of class). You should draw upon the main points of our readings for that day. You may choose to contextualize the texts for that day; to apply the concepts raised therein to a specific analytic situation of your choosing; to provide background or genealogy by drawing upon additional material; or to problematize and critique the chapters by drawing on other texts and contexts. What I mean by provocation is the integration of texts with different sources, intentions, or characteristics than those assigned in class that week. Provocations may include substantial video, performance, installation, or digital projects that provide substantive examples of the work of games in the world.

Resolution: The ability to improvise and synthesize by “thinking on your feet” is essential to success in graduate school. The resolver will draw connections across the components of each of your classes, and provide an oral synthesis in the last 20 minutes of class that draws our streams of discussion together and raises questions for us to consider during the week we are apart. The resolver should be thinking both within the context of the weekly class session, but also across the entirety of the course as our discussions continue and as we add more conceptual depth to our analytical toolkits.

Each student will be responsible for each prompt twice, once in the first half of the semester, and once in the second. Students will sign up for prompts the first day of class. Depending on course enrollment, there may be some days where more than one student is assigned to a single prompt. In these cases, students are encouraged to work together in the week prior to build their prompts, though for Synthesis prompts, each student should still prepare and submit their own individual 1500 word essay.

**Final Paper: Independent Conceptual Trace**

**5000 Word Final Paper Due December 15 (60 Points)**

At the end of the course, each student will prepare a 5000 word paper and 20 minute presentation based upon a concept in game studies that mimics a complete version of the weekly unit readings and prompts. Students will select four readings through which develop, deploy, and/or critique a unified conceptual theme in game studies, summarize those readings, provide a synthesis of them, introduce provocations using material outside of those core texts, and offer a resolution/takeaway for other students in the class. Each of these sections should follow the prompt formats that we have been using throughout the course. On the last day of class, each student will present a 20 minute talk based upon their final paper, followed by 5 minutes of Q&A and discussion from the class.

Conceptual and thematic suggestions for the final paper can be found after the Unit summaries in this document. By November 1, each student should email the professor with a list of three concepts they may be interested in tracing for their final essay. Each concept should also be accompanied by a game studies article or chapter-length reading that the student identifies as a strong exemplar of the concept being developed or deployed. Before class on November 6, the professor will email each student back assigning them their concept. Students are not obligated to use a concept from among the list below, but they should be sure when doing their initial research that the concept has enough use or development in game studies to make possible a rigorous paper and presentation.

### Additional Academic Integrity Course Policy and Penalty Information:

**Academic Integrity**

Trust: Student-Teacher relationships are built on trust. Students must trust that teachers have made appropriate decisions about the structure and content of the courses they teach. And, teachers must trust that the assignments that students turn in are their own. Acts, which violate this trust, undermine the educational process. The Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities defines various forms of Academic Dishonesty and you should make yourself familiar with these. In this class, all assignments that are turned in for a grade must represent the student’s own work. In cases where help was received, or teamwork allowed, a notation on the assignment should indicate your collaboration.

Plagiarism: All work produced in this course must be original and created by the student. Incidents of academic dishonesty or attempted academic dishonesty will result in grade penalties and may be referred to the Dean of Students. While the creation of digital artwork may utilize prior works for inspiration, reference, and source material, assignments in this class are expected to represent the student’s originality and technical ability through the creation of substantially new works. The use of AI generated content is forbidden except in cases where the project centers the use or critique of AI tools in its design. In such cases, plans for appropriate use and crediting of AI tools will be explicitly planned in consultation with the course instructors. When in doubt, ask your instructor.
