## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

This course is intended to provide graduate students with the intellectual and peer support to develop qualitative research writing based on contemporary approaches in Science and Technology Studies. Each week, students will read and analyze award-winning writing (as determined by the Society for the Social Study of Science); the aim of this is to determine generic conventions across disciplinary approaches to Science and Technology Studies, particularly as they relate to the collection and presentation of data and the role that theory plays in data collection and writing. In addition, students will regularly complete writing assignments of varying complexity, conduct peer assessments, and lead class discussions; the aim of these activities is to demystify these processes as well as to provide ongoing support for the development of iterative writing projects.

Science and Technology Studies—as a field and as a department at RPI—is disciplinarily diverse, drawing from history, cultural anthropology, sociology, political theory, and geography. The texts in this course reflect this disciplinary diversity, demonstrating the many models that students might employ in conceptualizing projects and their execution as writing projects. Across the materials that we read, students will be asked to consider the relationship between content and form, how theory and evidence motivate each other, and what forms of evidence and argumentation are convincing and compelling.

## Learning Outcomes

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes (CLOs):

- Students will become fluent in the formal analysis of published scholarship;
- Students will develop confidence in producing discipline-appropriate writing;
- Students will gain skills in analyzing academic writing through peer review.

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes Assessment Measures:

*Four waypoint assignments*, no more than two of which can be Stage One (40%; 10% each):

Stage One Assignments: an extended abstract (up to 600 words); a conference paper (no more than 2400 words); a literature review (no more than (1800 words); a methodology section (no more than 1500 words); an individual empirical case (no more than 2100 words); other options may be discussed.

Stage Two Assignments: a grant proposal (no more than 3500 words); a draft chapter or article manuscript (no more than 10000 words); a dissertation proposal (no more than 12000 words); a finalized chapter or article manuscript (no more than 10000 words) + an editorial cover letter; other options may be discussed.

*Two peer assessment assignments* (10%; 5% each): At two points, you will conduct a peer review of assignments turned in by your fellow students. The number of assignments that you are assigned will be determined by the length and variety of turned-in assignments. You will be provided these materials at Waypoint #2 and #4 and asked to return your peer assessments the following week (as marked on the syllabus). Read this for tips on peer review.

*Four leadership memos* (40%; 10% each): At four points in the term you will lead class discussion. In order to facilitate this, you should prepare notes based on the assigned reading that address the following questions: what theories structure the author’s overarching argument?; how does the overarching argument integrate arguments in each chapter of the book?; what kinds of evidence does the author employ?; how is this evidence produced, methodologically?; how does the evidence reflect theoretical commitments? You will be leading class discussion with one or more of your fellow students; it may be useful to plan how you will lead discussion. But your leadership memo should be your own and reflect your engagement with the text. Memos should be approximately 1500 words. Please deposit your leadership memos in this folder no later than the start of class. Please title your memos based on the text and your name, e.g., Sensory Futures_Wolf-Meyer.

*One integrative statement* (10%): Choose two texts from the course that use similar forms of evidence. These may include all texts listed on the syllabus. Compare the texts and their use of evidence as it relates to their argumentation (\~1200 words). How do these approaches inform how you will collect and analyze evidence? Provide an example of evidence from your work and consider how it relates to other uses of similar forms of evidence in published scholarship (\~900 words).

## Policies

### Academic Policies:

- ### Academic Integrity

  Student-teacher relationships are built on trust. For example, students must trust that teachers have made appropriate decisions about the structure and content of the courses they teach, and teachers must trust that the assignments that students turn in are their own. Acts that violate this trust undermine the educational process. The Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities and The Graduate Student Supplement define various forms of Academic Dishonesty and you should make yourself familiar with these. In this class, all assignments that are turned in for a grade must represent the student’s own work. In cases where help was received, or teamwork was allowed, a notation on the assignment should indicate your collaboration.

  Violations of academic integrity may also be reported to the appropriate Dean (Dean of Students for undergraduate students or the Dean of Graduate Education for graduate students, respectively).

  If you have any question concerning this policy before submitting an assignment, please ask for clarification. In addition, you can visit the following site for more information on our Academic Integrity Policy: [Students Rights, Responsibilities, and Judicial Affairs](https://info.rpi.edu/dean-students/student-rights-responsibilities-and-judicial-affairs).
