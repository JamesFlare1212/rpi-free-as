## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

This seminar is an investigation of creative, theoretical, and historical research methods in the interdisciplinary arts, humanities, and qualitative social sciences. Students will be introduced to traditional and practice-based approaches emphasizing qualitative research, and will gain competence in writing and research techniques, conventions, and methods. This course is open to graduate students across HASS, Architecture and ITWS, and is a requirement for Electronic Arts doctoral students.

## Learning Outcomes

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes (CLOs):

- Gain knowledge of interdisciplinary research methods, primarily in the creative arts, humanities, and social sciences.
- Demonstrate a capacity to apply the range of research methods studied to personal research and artistic practices.
- Acquire in-depth knowledge of the form and other technical components of the doctoral dissertation project.
- Develop a scholarly agenda that integrates creative practice and/or fieldwork or other appropriate methods
- Conduct literature search effectively and review the literature critically.

**Assignment Specifications**

Participation

- Active Discussion in class. Systematic note taking is an essential research skill, and students should find the best way to do this preparation, which is essential for active class discussion.
  - o Digital reference management systems will be introduced by the instructor as one way do this systematic note taking.
- Lead Discussion, five times throughout the course. As lead discussant you will artfully steer an in-depth critical group reading of one of the assigned articles. You will provide a thoughtful analysis of the central claims of the reading, the principal questions posed, and prompt class discussion with questions, counter-examples and counter-arguments.
- Work-in-Progress Presentation, Nov 11. This is an oral presentation of your intended research questions, introduced with a short background of how you arrived at these questions, contextualized in terms of key intellectual and creative influences, along with the disciplinary frameworks and scholarly/creative communities you want to speak to in posing these questions.
  - o Outline the possible methods you could use to answer each question. You don’t need to be certain about the questions or methods proposed at this stage, but it is essential that you are able to articulately frame the criteria you could use to refine them into a coherent and unified research plan.
  - o Support your talk visually, or with artifacts, to help the class along. Plan on 10-12 minutes presentation, 18 minutes discussion.
  - o You are also required to provide one background reading for the class to read the week before the presentation date, i.e. on Nov 4 (Use the discussion board provided to upload your pre-presentation reading). Assume all will have read this item before hearing your presentation.

Methods Exercises

- September 9. Problem Space and Mind Maps. Draw a concept map or diagram of the fields of scholarship or creative/social practice involved with your research interest.
  - o Be imaginative and feel free to use any approach that works for you to visualize your research interests as a “web” or “constellation.”
  - o Create a Problem Space map that visualizes the tensions, stakeholders, disciplines and methods surrounding your research question.
- October 1. Keywords. Using the models provided in the syllabus, select three keywords that are foundational to your research interests.
  - o Prepare detailed talking points for each keyword for oral presentation during class. Go beyond definitions and etymology to trace the history of the term’s usage and explicate current controversies in its meaning.
  - o You will then have a week to submit a short essay on each of the three selected keywords. (300 words each, 900 words total).
- CITI certification, October 1. Basic and Human Subject Modules (upload screenshot of certificate of completion).
- Bibliographic Essay, 1500 words, due October 29. Select a key article or book chapter that is at the core of your current research/creative interests. Be sure in your selection that the item has been published long enough to have spawned a substantial secondary literature.
  - o Then select at least three works of scholarship (or creative practice) cited in the focal article, locate and analyze each one; similarly, then locate at least three works of scholarship that cite the focal article in a formative way, and analyze them too. Use a professional scholarly reference database like Scopus or Google Scholar to carry out the search for “before” and “after” articles.
  - o Figure on about 250 words for the six before and after items, and 500 words for introducing the focal article and describing the “web” of past and future connections.
  - o The whole should read smoothly as an essay with a narrative, not just as a list of annotations.
- Fieldwork Report, 500 words November 5. Taking a field site you are already engaged with write a “field report” as a sample of one of the field methods discussed in the course:
  - o interview (with questions and transcribed actual interview)
  - o ethnography or autoethnography (a short vignette)
  - o or if you wish to pursue a more experimental method (arts-practice based or otherwise) feel free to bring this up with the instructor as another option.

Research Proposal Dec 9

The final assignment for this course is a finely crafted scholarly statement that addresses the student’s plan for their doctoral research/creative practice. The required structure is based on the Fellowship Proposal format used in the School of Humanities, Arts, and Social Sciences. The document will include:

- Background summarizing your field of study and the focus of your planned dissertation research (plus creative practice) at this stage. This section includes a summary of your trajectory, prior experience, and preparation for this inquiry. Situate your work relative to current and historical research in the field, providing citations for relevant publications and sources.
- Research questions (and creative objectives) that will inform your research/practice, along with a rationale for why these particular questions are relevant now (i.e. timely) and to/for whom (i.e., which scholarly communities or interest groups your work will feed into and impact).
- A discussion of the method(s) and approach(es) you plan to use to answer the research questions through scholarship and creative practice.
- Impact. Describe the prospective significance of your research/practice. How will your work impact critical issues within the field, and across disciplinary boundaries?
- Timeline. Provide an approximate timeline for the completion of your dissertation and creative portfolio, listing deadlines you hope to accomplish major goals.
- 7 pages single spaced using 12-point Times font and one inch margins. (3500 words)

## Grading Criteria

### Criteria Details:

A Range. Conceptual: thorough and coherent, original thinking, moves beyond assignment bounds. Writing: Excellent writing and grammar skills. Expresses ideas clearly and effectively; spends time on the work; attention to detail. Participates fully, taking a leadership role in discussion in class and online community outside of class. Brings new ideas to class consistently. Listens to others’ views with respect. Attends class on time and turns in work on time.

B Range. Conceptual: Complete assignments with some originality. Writing: Good writing and grammar skills. Writing is clear and ideas are organized. Participates fully in discussion. Attends class on time and turns in work on time. Participates in online comments and engages with others’ ideas.

C Range. Conceptual: Unoriginal or common-sense thinking; doing only what is required; Writing: Unclear writing, grammatical errors, lack of thoroughness. Inconsistencies in presentation of work and participation or does not frequently participate. Attends class but minimally engages with others’ work.

F. Fails to meet requirements of Assignment/Not turned in on time.

- ### Academic Integrity

  Student-teacher relationships are built on trust. For example, students must trust that teachers have made appropriate decisions about the structure and content of the courses they teach, and teachers must trust that the assignments that students turn in are their own. The Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities and The Graduate Student Supplement define various forms of Academic Dishonesty and you should make yourself familiar with these. ### Additional Academic Integrity Course Policy and Penalty Information:

**Academic Integrity.** Collaboration between students in this course is strongly encouraged. Likewise, students are encouraged—indeed, to some extent required—to exchange ideas, opinions, and information constantly. You are encouraged to help each other with performance, production, and presentation of projects.

Plagiarism of any kind is in direct violation of the University Policy on Academic Dishonesty as defined in the *Rensselaer Handbook*, and penalties for plagiarism can be severe. In this class you will be expected to attribute due credit to the originator of any ideas, words, sounds, or work which you incorporate substantially into your own work. This applies particularly to citation of sources for quotes and ideas included in your compositions. Copying and pasting from published sources or the internet is considered plagiarism and is not acceptable. Plagiarized work will receive an automatic grade of zero for the assignment in addition to other possible penalties. The Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities and The Rensselaer Graduate Student Supplement define various forms of Academic Dishonesty and you should make yourself familiar with these. In this class, all assignments that are turned in for a grade must represent the student’s own work. In cases where help was received, or teamwork was allowed, a notation on the assignment should indicate your collaboration. Submission of any assignment that is in violation of this policy will result in a penalty. If found in violation of the academic honesty policy, students may be subject to two types of penalty. The instructor administers an academic [grade] penalty and the student is reported to the Dean of Students or the Dean of Graduate Education as appropriate. The first violation results in 0 grade for that assignment. The second violation results in failure of the course. If you have any questions concerning this policy before submitting an assignment, please ask for clarification.
