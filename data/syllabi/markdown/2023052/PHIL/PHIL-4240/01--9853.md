## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

As a philosophical discipline, the subject matter of ethics is broadly framed around how we ought to live. Herein, some of the questions explored in the process of doing ethics include: (a) Are values objective or subjective? (b) Is moral knowledge possible? (c) Does self-interest (egoism) underly all moral motivations? (d) How does one cultivate virtue and good character? (e) What makes actions right? (f) What kinds of beings are deserving moral consideration? (g) What is the nature of justice? As we explore these questions, students will be come familiar with both classical and contemporary thinkers who have contributed to the three subdivisions of this philosophical discipline, mainly: (i) metaethics; (ii) ethical theories; and (iii) applied ethics (e.g., medical ethics, animal welfare and economic justice).

## Course Goals

### Goals:

After completing this course, students will be able to:

1\) think critically about a range of beliefs, theories and arguments that are salient to metaethics, ethical theories and a selection of applied ethical issues.

2\) demonstrate their ability to evaluate, discuss and debate arguments concerning: (a) the status of moral knowledge; (b) cultural relativism; (c) ethical egoism; (d) virtue theory; (e) deontology; (f) consequentialism; (g) social contract theory; and (h) particularlism.

3\) identify and explain key concepts, theories and thinkers that have shaped our understanding of classical and contemporary ethical theories, metaethical positions and applied ethical stances.

4\) effectively communicate their perspectives through essays, seminar discussions and panel presentations.

5\) conduct and present their research in The Chicago Manual of Style format.

## Learning Outcomes

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes (CLOs):

- After completing this course, students will be able to: 1) think critically about a range of beliefs, theories and arguments that are salient to metaethics, ethical theories and a selection of applied ethical issues. 2) demonstrate their ability to evaluate, discuss and debate arguments concerning: (a) the status of moral knowledge; (b) cultural relativism; (c) ethical egoism; (d) virtue theory; (e) deontology; (f) consequentialism; (g) social contract theory; and (h) particularlism. 3) identify and explain key concepts, theories and thinkers that have shaped our understanding of classical and contemporary ethical theories, metaethical positions and applied ethical stances. 4) effectively communicate their perspectives through essays, seminar discussions and panel presentations. 5) conduct and present their research in The Chicago Manual of Style format.

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes Assessment Measures:

Short Essays (1000 words): For each short essay assignment, students will be given constructive feedback and comments. After submitting their essays for a preliminary review, each student will be given two weeks to edit and make changes prior to submitting their work for a final grade. Essay prompts will be centered around specific theorists/arguments/cases that define the field of Ethics. Essays will be evaluated according to one’s ability to: (1) write clearly; (2) apply philosophical theories to specific issues; (3) employ the basic skills of critical thinking; and (4) evaluate and create arguments in defense of a normative position.

Seminar Discussion Participation: This class is a communication intensive course, which means there will be regular seminar discussions that address specific theories, thinkers and issues that have defined the field of Ethics. Discussion topics will be provided prior to class meetings so that students will have time to reflect upon their perspective and normative position. Participation will be evaluated based upon one’s ability to dialogue with students and articulate arguments that are relevant to the course readings. Student will be evaluated based upon their ability to contribute to class discussions by: (a) addressing specific philosophers; (b) appealing to specific theories; (c) treating specific problems/issues; and/or (d) referencing specific course readings.

**Grading Policy:**

All assignments in this course – discussions, presentations and essays – will be evaluated in light of the following criterion: (1) ability to write clearly by following grammatical rules and conforming to style guidelines of The Chicago Manual of Style; (2) ability to demonstrate critical thinking by formulating and assessing arguments; (3) ability to effectively engage and reference key articles and philosophical perspectives included in the syllabus. Late assignments will not be accepted for a grade unless: (i) a student requests an extension in advance (48 hrs. prior to the deadline); and (ii) the request concerns a legitimate issue warranting an extension.

Rensselaer Polytechnic Institute’s commitment to thoroughness and knowledge includes the values associated with intellectual honesty.
