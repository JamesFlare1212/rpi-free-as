## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

This course combines demand, derived from consumer preferences, and supply, based on firms’ production functions, to establish market prices for goods and services. Calculus-based techniques are used to minimize costs and maximize utility and profits across differing industry structures. Product pricing strategies are examined. The course also introduces topics such as the pricing of stocks and bonds, game theory, positive and negative externalities, asymmetric information, and behavioral economics.

The course applies the microeconomic theory of the firm to price, cost, and output decisions of business enterprises under different market structures. Regression analysis of demand and cost, linear programming of production and simulation analysis of risk, and capital budgeting are also presented.

The grading scale is 90-100 A, 80-89.9 B, 70-79.9 C, 60-69.9 D, <60 F. Plus/minus grading will be used in this course with plus grades will be assigned for scores in the top 3 points of a grade range while minus grades will be assigned for scores in the bottom 3 points of a grade range. For example, 82.6 points out of 100 is a “B minus” while 89.6 is a “B plus.” Your overall score is determined by the summation of your performance on exams, assignments, and participation.

## Learning Outcomes

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes (CLOs):

- To be able to explain to others why economists use mathematics and formal modeling to analyze situations of constrained choice.
- To be able to use the mathematical tools of algebra and calculus to determine the optimal allocation of resources for a given economic agent’s preferences and constraints (where the agent is either a consumer or producer).
- To be prepared, in subsequent classes, to approach academic articles in economics and explain the article’s formal model or implied theoretical ideas to others.
- To be able to document the limits of economic reasoning and modeling, and clearly explain to others the choices that might separate the model from the real-world choice situation that it supposedly represents.
