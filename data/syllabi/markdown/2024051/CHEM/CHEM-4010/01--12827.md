The slides on LMS also constitute a framework of material that you are responsible for (unless indicated otherwise) and *some* material covered in class may not be found in the text.

## Course Goals

### Goals:

The objectives of this course are to extend the material of Inorganic Chemistry I to more advanced topics, primarily molecular symmetry properties and group theoretical applications, solid state chemistry and some applications, nomenclature, electronic and spectral properties of transition metal complexes, magnetism, bonding and reaction chemistry of classical coordination compounds and organometallic systems, and inorganic nanoparticle synthesis and applications.

## Course Content

### Content Details:

*Topics to be covered:* **Molecular Bonding, Symmetry, and Polyatomic MOs (Chapters 2 & 3)**  
Review of basic molecular orbital ideas for diatomic and triatomic molecules  
Symmetry elements and operations and molecular point groups  
Character tables and symmetry labels  
Connection to vibrational spectra, chirality  
Polyatomic (Ligand) molecular orbitals from group theory concepts  
**Solid State (Chapter 4)**  
Unit cells, Bravais lattice, crystal systems, space groups, Miller indices,  
Bragg equation and X-ray diffraction. *See* *http://www.chem.ccu.edu.tw/~kmc/98materials%20crystal%20structure.pdf* Crystal defects  
Semiconductors and solid electrolytes, etc.  
**Transition Metal Chemistry (Tentative) (Chapter 19)**  
A brief survey of the transition elements and a few aspects of their chemistry.  
**Coordination Chemistry. (Chapters 7 & 20)**  
General chemistry of transition elements  
Review nomenclature, stability considerations, chelation, isomerism, coordination numbers, *d*-orbital splitting etc.  
Ligand field concepts, stabilization energies, regular and distorted geometries, Jahn-Teller distortion  
Molecular orbitals and bonding in coordination complexes - s and p  
Polynuclear complexes and metal-metal bonds  
**Spectra and Related Properties (Chapter 20)**  
Spectroscopic term symbols and energy states for free atoms; Racah parameters; selection rules  
Ligand field spectra ground and excited state *d*1 and *d*9 configurations; ditto for *d*2 configurations; Energy level diagrams; Tanabe-Sugano diagrams, assigning spectra; evaluation of Do and B; spectrochemical series  
Charge transfer spectra  
Magnetism  
**Reactions of Complexes. (Chapter 21)**  
Rates and general mechanisms of ligand exchange reactions  
Substitution mechanisms in octahedral complexes  
Substitution mechanisms in square planar complexes and *trans* effect  
Redox reactions - inner and outer sphere  
**Organometallic Chemistry (Chapter 22)**  
Organometallic compounds - carbonyls, examples of stoichiometry and structures; terminal and bridging CO; 18- and 16-electron rules; bonding in metal carbonyls; p acceptor ligands  
Alkene ligands; bonding in ethylenic compounds; other p systems; hapticity  
Cyclopentadiene and other aromatic p ligands  
Alkyl, carbene, carbyne, phosphine, and hydride systems  
Clusters  
Reaction Types  
Catalytic Cycles  
**Inorganic Nanoparticles (relevant sections of Chapter 24)**  
Terminology and approaches to nanoparticle synthesis  
Solution-phase synthesis of metallic and metal-oxide nanoparticles  
Templated synthesis of nanoparticles and surface functionalization  
Applications in diagnostic imaging and generally metals in medicine  
**Biological/Medicinal Inorganic Chem (.*..if time permits*) (Chapters 26 & 27 relevant sections)**  
General roles and functions of metal ions in biological system  
Iron in biological systems  
Zinc and Cobalt enzymes  
Copper systems  
Some experimental techniques for structure determination

## Learning Outcomes

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes (CLOs):

- Expected Student Outcomes: At the end of the course, students completing the course successfully will demonstrate the following knowledge or competence: Symmetry – Students will be able to identify the symmetry elements and point group of a molecule and be able to interpret and apply the basic concepts of group theory to molecular orbitals in simple cases, e.g., diatomics and polyatomics including octahedral and tetrahedral coordination complexes.
- Expected Student Outcomes: At the end of the course, students completing the course successfully will demonstrate the following knowledge or competence: Solid state –Students will be able to recognize and interpret the various unit cell types and associated concepts, and to describe the relationship of structural characteristics of crystalline solids to important properties. Coordination chemistry – Students will be able to interpret the spectra and magnetic properties of transition metal compounds according to their electronic configurations; they will be able to describe the essential features of controlling the stability and reactions of transition metal coordination complexes.
- Expected Student Outcomes: At the end of the course, students completing the course successfully will demonstrate the following knowledge or competence: Organometallic chemistry – Students will be able to explain the orbital interactions involved in the bonding of metals in organometallic compounds and with pi-acceptor ligands generally; they will be able to identify the basic types of reactions found in organometallic systems and how these are involved in catalytic behavior.

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes Assessment Measures:

**Expected Student Outcomes:** At the end of the course, students completing the course successfully will demonstrate the following knowledge or competence:

### Additional Academic Integrity Course Policy and Penalty Information:

**Academic Integrity:**

**You are expected to work independently and without collaboration** on Exams, Homework assignments, and Quizzes. Failure to follow this policy is regarded as cheating and will result in a grade of “F”. Collaboration will be evidenced and revealed as you must SHOW ALL YOUR WORK to receive credit for answers.

The relationship between students and faculty is based upon trust and the continued maintenance of this trust is necessary for education to be successful. Students need to trust faculty to make appropriate judgments about the content and structure of the course. Faculty members need to trust that the work turned in by students represents their own effort. Violation of this trust undermines the educational process. In addition, as professionals, you may be designing products upon which lives depend, as a result, there is no tolerance for breach of academic integrity such as cheating or plagiarizing. Since homework assignments and Exams are completed and taken at home/remotely, you are allowed to consult (but should not copy from) your book and class notes/slides. Although Dr. B cannot monitor your consultation with internet sites such as Chegg.com, these sites will disclose information to universities in official investigations of cheating. See Chegg.com honor code: https://www.chegg.com/honorcode

The Rensselaer Student Handbook and the Dean of Students Office Webpage (http://doso.rpi.edu/update.do?artcenterkey=676) defines various forms of academic dishonesty. Please familiarize yourself with these and ask us if you have questions about what constitutes academic dishonesty.
