## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

It’s hot out there! The water is rising, there’s plastic in the fish, the pipes keep breaking, and the storms are getting stronger. Our communities face a growing number of challenges, from the extreme (surviving an oil spill, a hurricane, a factory closure, or a heat wave) to the mundane (repairing a road, turning on the air conditioning, finding an apartment, taking a shower). For sustainability minded people - or for anyone paying attention - it’s hard to ignore the distressing daily signals of ecological, social, and political crises that shape our lives and futures. Why is it like this, what should we do, and who decides?

No matter our role in the organization of resources and power for sustaining and nurturing life, we are members of complex and interdependent human political ecologies. We reproduce these ecologies all the time, as we make choices about what to buy, to do, or to advocate for. We reproduce them daily just by virtue of reproducing our own bodies: as we eat, drink, and excrete, globally scaled and intimately experienced flows of resources and capital weave through our lives unevenly.This course provides students with a wide-ranging understanding of the relationships between humans, nature, power and difference. Drawing primarily on concepts and research from critical geography, political ecology, critical urban studies, post-colonial studies, and more, the class will use land, labor, and history as tools for examining structures of power, difference, and inequality across the economic and ecological scales of our lives.

## Learning Outcomes

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes (CLOs):

- Recognize and apply key social science concepts for analysis of social & environmental issues such as: race, class, gender, globalization, neocolonialism, neoliberalism, etc.
- Use concepts from critical geography to describe environmental issues.
- Articulate alternative theoretical interpretations of environmental issues.
- Reflect on their own position with regards to social and environmental issues.
- Identify perspectives on environmental and social issues from multiple stakeholders.
- Communicate stories about the environment and society to a broader audience.

## Policies

### Other Course Policies:

- **Class attendance is important and required.** I ask each of you to stay present in class, limit distractions, and participate actively in the discussion. I recognize that participation may look different for each of us, so I want to remind you that listening actively is a form of participation. Likewise, contributing to the notes, responding to open questions, and fully engaging the assigned materials is a form of participation.
- **We will maintain collective notes**, to notice and track key themes, questions, articulations, assumptions, and other ideas.
- **Let’s be on time.** It’s disruptive for both of us if we are consistently late. At the same time, managing our time and our lives is a tough skill to develop. I will excuse reasonable absences and latenesses, especially if you communicate in advance. If I start to notice a pattern or correlation between attendance, understanding, and engagement, I may invite a conversation and consider an impact to your participation grade.
- **Make space / Take space.** If you tend to talk a lot, raise your hand first, or feel comfortable speaking out loud in front of others - that’s wonderful, but make sure to make space for others to speak. Likewise, if you hang back, make sure to speak up a few times.
- **Expect iteration:** it takes practice to build literacy and dexterity in complex social, ecological, and economic concepts. Revision and editing are essential skills. Expect to revisit topics and ideas, build on small changes, and articulate related ideas in new, familiar, or overlapping ways.

- ### Academic Integrity

  Student-teacher relationships are built on trust. For example, students must trust that teachers have made appropriate decisions about the structure and content of the courses they teach, and teachers must trust that the assignments that students turn in are their own. Acts that violate this trust undermine the educational process. The Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities and The Graduate Student Supplement define various forms of Academic Dishonesty and you should make yourself familiar with these.
