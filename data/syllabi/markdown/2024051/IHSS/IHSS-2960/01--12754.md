Stage 1 Assignments

Dialogue vs. Discussion vs. Debate Assignment - due Friday 5/31

An important part of the first stage of dialogue is being able to discern between dialogue, discussion, and debate. These are all different forms of communication styles that have specific purposes and use different methods and approaches to inquiry, information sharing, and conflict. In order to attempt to achieve the goals of intergroup dialogue, we are going to aim to engage mostly in dialogue with one another, and observe and name when we may be drifting into discussion or debate.

To further explore these communication types, please identify separate examples of debate, discussion, and dialogue. These can be examples from your own life, or they can be from a reading, video, or other form of media. Please discuss the following questions for each example:

- Please provide a summary of each example of debate, discussion, and dialogue
- How does this example illustrate the particular communication method (debate, discussion, or dialogue)?
- What main characteristics of the communication method are present in the example? Please be sure to connect the characteristics to specifics found within the example.
- What was the result/conclusion of the example? What were the benefits and drawbacks to utilizing the particular communication method in the example?
- How would the example be different if another communication method was utilized?

After reflecting on your examples of debate, discussion, and dialogue, please provide a brief overall conclusion that discusses your experiences with these communication methods and how being able to recognize and discern between these different forms of communication may assist you in the future.

*Out of Comfort Zone Experience Assignment - Required, due Friday 6/7

When engaging in dialogue, we go beyond our comfort zones and lean into our learning edges to experience some discomfort in learning about ourselves and those who are different from us. We aim to bring our whole selves to the dialogue and lead with vulnerability and authenticity when sharing our own experiences, and responding with empathy and affirming inquiry when listening to others.

The comfort zone is exactly what it sounds like, a comfortable state of being in which you’re not feeling challenged or threatened. Although it may feel nice to be in your comfort zone, there are minimal opportunities for learning and growth to occur here. The danger zone is also characterized by a lack of learning and growth, but it’s because someone in this zone has been pushed too far beyond their comfort zone. Being in the danger zone feels unsafe and this may manifest in you shutting down or withdrawing from an interaction or staying in the interaction but being unable to actively listen or take in any new information.

The goal in dialogue is to learn from conflict and different perspectives, and this cannot occur in either the comfort zone or danger zone. Instead we encourage ourselves to explore the space between these two extremes, occupying a cognitive and emotional area we call the learning edge or stretch zone. Here it is possible to challenge yourself to embrace the discomfort that may come with choosing to engage in and learn from conflict.

To better understand your comfort zone, learning edge, and danger zone, as well as grow your capacity to be in discomfort, we want you to engage in an out of comfort zone experience of your choosing. The experience should be aligned with goals and topics of the course, related to social justice and/or social identity. The parameters and specifics of the experience are for you to decide based on what would be outside of your comfort zone. Some examples for an experience that you could do include:

- Attending and participating in a program or event focused on a culture and/or identity that is different from your own
- Having a conversation or dialogue with a family member or friend about a topic related to social identities, privilege, and oppression
- Having a conversation with a classmate or peer about a topic related to social identities, privilege, and oppression
- Watching a film related to social justice and/or systemic marginalization
- Reading a book related to social justice and/or systemic marginalization
- Engaging in an in-class dialogue by asking questions and sharing of yourself

There are a multitude of other out of comfort zone experiences you could engage in as well - as we all have our own personal levels of comfort, it is up to you to decide what kinds of experiences would stretch you into your learning edge.

After you complete the out of comfort zone experience, please write a reflection about the experience that answers to the following prompts:

- What about the experience was outside of your comfort zone?
- What emotions came up as you engaged in the experience?
- In what ways did this experience propel you to confront your beliefs?
- What was the most important thing you learned from the experience?
- How can you engage in your learning edge in future class dialogues?

Stage 2 Assignments

*Autoethnography Reflection My Story Assignment – Required, due Friday 6/14 (will be shared in class Monday 6/17)

In order to address diversity and equity issues, we must reflect on and understand our own social identities. Contemplating different ways in which we are privileged, the different facets of marginalization within our lives, and our beliefs about oppression are critical to engage in meaningful dialogue and further the goals of social justice. In addition, reflecting on our challenges and limitations with diversity and equity can enable us to develop empathy for those we ask to step outside their comfort zones. We cannot effectively wrestle with various “isms” (e.g. racism, heterosexism, classism, sexism, ableism etc.) without knowledge of our own social identities and the ways we inadvertently contribute to oppressive systems.

The purpose of this assignment is to share your own story. Focus specifically on diversity, equity, power, privilege, oppression, social identities, and your engagement in dialogues about these issues in your past and current experiences. You will be able to choose the format you would like to share your story in. Some possible options include:

- Written reflection essay
- Poetry (Where I am From poem)
- Visual essay
- Creative video
- In-class performance/presentation

We will be sharing more about these options in class. If there is another format you would like to tell your story in, please share your idea with instructors before proceeding. We are open to additional possibilities!

When putting together your story, consider the following guiding questions. You do not need to answer all of the questions in the order in which they are presented, and indeed you will not have space to do so in a meaningful manner. Consider them as general guides for writing your story:

- Who am I?
- What are the multiple social identities that make me who I am?
- What do these social identities mean to me?
- What role do these social identities play in my life? Which of these social identities are more or less important to how I understand who I am as a person?
- In what ways, if any, do I experience relationships among these social identities?
- How do other people’s perceptions of me influence how I understand and experience my social identities?
- What do I see as the role of social identities in STEM?
- What matters to me and why in relation to diversity?
- What kinds of experiences have I had in engaging in dialogues about my social identities and the social identities of others?
- What are my biggest fears in engaging in dialogues about diversity and equity in my own life and in the lives of others?

Developing your My Story is your opportunity to practice some risk taking and be bold. Try a different writing or presentation style, stretch yourself, reach deep within, and focus deeply on your narratives about who you are.

Dialogue Partner Practice & Reflections - due Friday 6/28

Engaging in dialogue often leads to rich communication and meaningful relationships with others. Being able to engage in effective dialogue requires practice and skill building over time. In order to practice dialogue skills and build relationships with those in our class community, we would like you to meet with a dialogue partner from our class 2-3 times outside of class meeting times. Plan to meet at least an hour to engage in dialogue with one another.

The topics of your dialogues can be of your choosing - reactions and reflections about things we have done in class, deeper dives on specific class topics, additional social justice topics we have not dialogued about yet in class, personal sharing about your own lived experiences etc.

After each dialogue partner meeting, please write a reflection that answers the following prompts:

- What was it like to engage in one-on-one dialogue outside of class?
- What was challenging about the dialogue? What was easy?
- How did you choose your dialogue topic(s)?
- What are some things you learned about your dialogue partner? What are some things you learned about yourself?
- What dialogue skills did you use or try out? How has your capacity for engaging in dialogue changed after your partner dialogue?
- For your second and third meetings, how has your relationship with your dialogue partner grown and/or changed?

Please submit your series of reflections as one document.

Game/Activity Design: Social Identity Related Game - due Friday 7/19

Design and create a game or group activity rooted in concepts of social identities, social justice, and/or intergroup dialogue. The game or activity can be a board game, card game, digital game, playground game, simulation, or educational activity. If you have a different type of game or activity you would like to create, check in with the instructors so we can further explore your idea. Your game or activity should aim to teach something, share an experience, or seek to persuade the participants of a point of view for a social justice issue you care about.

Use a variety of materials that you may have on hand to create a prototype that is playable by members of the class. Please also create a set of instructions for your game or activity that outline the background and purpose, rules for playing, game mechanics, and how your game is connected to social identities and/or a social justice issue.

Please submit the instructions sheet, photos of your finished game (if physical), and a designer’s statement discussing the following points:

- How did you choose what social identities and/or social justice issues to focus on in your game or activity? How does the game convey information about these identities and/or issues?
- What is the game or activity experience intended to be? What will the participant get out of playing your game? Who is the audience?
- How is the game played?
- What materials did you use?
- How does the game embody the values it expresses?

We will hopefully have a chance to play prototypes of folks’ games in class if we have individuals complete this assignment.

*Dialogue Facilitation - Required, completed during Weeks 9 & 10 during specific dates assigned to partners and small groups

One of the goals of this course is to gain skills in engaging in open, honest, and vulnerable dialogues about diversity and equity issues. By pushing ourselves to participate in such dialogues, we develop our comfort with addressing these issues. Beyond our own engagement in these dialogues, it is crucial we learn how to facilitate dialogues with others in and outside the RPI community.

The purpose of this assignment is to utilize knowledge of dialogues in facilitating a classroom dialogue about a particular topic and/or activity related to diversity, equity, and social justice. Your topic can also connect to STEM in some way, but is not required to. Modeling the co-facilitation practice of dialogue, you will work in groups of 2-3 for this assignment. In forming these groups, you must work with others who are different from yourself in some way. We will form these groups and determine the facilitation dates during class in Weeks 4 & 5

Accomplish the following tasks in your facilitation experience:

Identify a topic and readings and/or materials you would like the class to review before the dialogue one week in advance of your facilitation. When selecting your readings and materials, keep the following parameters in mind:

1. Choose 2-3 readings that have similar themes and/or relate to one another
2. Have a variety of different types of readings to engage in that perhaps share multiple points of view. Scholarly peer-reviewed readings are encouraged.

Prepare a facilitation plan one week in advance of your facilitation (2-3 pages). Your facilitation plan should include the following elements:

1. List of readings or materials
2. Takeaways of your dialogue - What might we get from the dialogue?
3. Structure of the facilitation (guiding questions, activities, topics etc.)
4. How the structure of the facilitation will help class members meet the takeaways of your dialogue - How does the plan align with the goals?

*Please submit your facilitation plan on LMS before your dialogue!

Facilitate a dialogue on the date of your facilitation. Below are the guidelines for your facilitation:

1. The facilitation should last around 60-75 minutes. We will then take a break and spend the remaining class time processing your facilitation.
2. All members of the group must contribute actively and in notable ways to the facilitation.

This is a chance for you to practice dialogue facilitation with your peers and engage in topics that are particularly interesting to you. Remember, there is no perfect way to facilitate dialogue and we all make mistakes and missteps in our learning! Feel free to take risks and be messy in your learning, while also abiding by our community agreements and course outline. You are encouraged to ask instructors questions as you are planning your dialogue facilitation and bring up any concerns you have throughout the process.

Student Facilitated Activities & Simulations

As a part of practicing your dialogue facilitation skills, you and your facilitation partner will be asked to design and facilitate a brief 15-20 minute “warm up” activity at the start of class before we proceed into further class activities and/or a class dialogue with one another. Your activity can be in any format of your choosing and can include things such as small group discussions, think-pair-shares, role plays, games, brief simulations, large group dialogue/discussion etc. The goals of these activities are to give you and your partner an opportunity to practice some facilitation skills while also preparing our class community to engage in the dialogue or class topics with one another.

While these student facilitated activities will be informal and not graded, they should be prepared in advance with your dialogue partner. Develop a plan together that includes what type of activity you would like to facilitate, how you plan on facilitating the activity as a pair, the layout of the activity and timing, any needed background information, and important discussion/dialogue questions to be included in the activity. Feel free to consult with the course instructors to help develop your activity and any important facilitation skill sets you may want to utilize.

We will schedule these in advance so pairs have plenty of time to brainstorm and plan their activity or simulation. These activities will begin Monday 6/24 and will go through Thursday 7/18.

We want you to be active in your own assessment. We welcome your feedback on the system we’re using; you’ll have plenty of opportunities to give it.

## Policies

### Attendance Policy:

Active participation in class is expected and essential to learning. Recognizing that some people are naturally more extraverted than others, active participation does not mean always talking a lot and also includes active listening. More specifically, active participation includes:

- Demonstrating that you have completed the readings by incorporating aspects of the readings in our class conversation in meaningful ways
- Contributing thoughtful comments about and examples of concepts being discussed; this includes comments about ideas you are still trying to understand or may have questions about
- Raising thoughtful questions, including asking for clarification when you do not understand something
- Carefully listening to your classmates’ comments and respectfully responding
- Monitoring your own participation and being sensitive to allowing all people the opportunity to participate in ways that are comfortable for them, sometimes referred to as the community agreement “make space, take space”

As a dialogue-based course, in-person attendance is essential in building our classroom community and ensuring you are able to meet the learning outcomes for the class. If you must miss class for any reason, please let the instructors know before class if possible. ### Academic Policies:

- ### Academic Integrity

  Student-teacher relationships are built on trust. For example, students must trust that teachers have made appropriate decisions about the structure and content of the courses they teach, and teachers must trust that the assignments that students turn in are their own. Acts that violate this trust undermine the educational process. The Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities and The Graduate Student Supplement define various forms of Academic Dishonesty and you should make yourself familiar with these. In this class, all assignments that are turned in for a grade must represent the student’s own work. In cases where help was received, or teamwork was allowed, a notation on the assignment should indicate your collaboration.

  Violations of academic integrity may also be reported to the appropriate Dean (Dean of Students for undergraduate students or the Dean of Graduate Education for graduate students, respectively).

### Additional Academic Integrity Course Policy and Penalty Information:

Academic Integrity

For information about the RPI institute policies on academic dishonesty, please consult: https://rpi.app.box.com/s/bfzzwdsrqxzrm3jikr2uv6gn7zu6dhlt Please note in particular the sections regarding plagiarism. Plagiarizing is defined by Webster’s as: “to steal and pass off (the ideas or words of another) as one’s own: use (another’s production) without crediting the source.” If caught plagiarizing, you will be dealt with according to RPI institute policies. Plagiarized work will be submitted to the Dean of Students, and the grade on the assignment will be a zero. A second instance of plagiarism will result in failing the course. All assignments outside of class must be completed individually and without aid from Artificial Intelligence (AI) software/programs/websites unless prior approval is granted by the instructor. If it is found that AI was used without instructor permission to complete any assignment, the student will receive a grade of zero (0) for that assignment. If you have any questions, talk to the instructor.
