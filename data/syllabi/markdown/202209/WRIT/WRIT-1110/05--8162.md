## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

This course explores different genres of written communication employed in a variety of situations. Emphasis is placed on utilizing communication strategies that are tailored to both the audience and the context in which the communication takes place. The ethical implications of communication within a specific environment are also considered. This course develops a process approach to writing, which moves from planning through revision and final editing.

## Course Text(s)

### Text Details:

There is no textbook for this class. Instead, I will ask you to download and print articles from a variety of sources—e.g. *Scientific American, NPR, Popular Mechanics, The New York Times, The New Yorker, etc*. This notebook will also double as your “archive” to use for entries in your final portfolio project.

I will also note when it will be absolutely necessary for you to have your computer with you during class meetings.

## Course Goals

### Goals:

The primary goal of this course is to help you communicate in ways that actually make something happen through using narrative as a way to influence people’s actions, beliefs, and understanding. More specifically, the course is designed to improve your ability to:

- Communicate with a variety of *real audiences* in different written and spoken generic forms.
- Achieve a *variety of purposes* (provide perspective on an experience, analyze and review something, and persuasively argue your position on an issue).
- Incorporate visuals (graphs, visual images, page/screen design) effectively into your written and oral presentations.
- Practice creating and making effective oral presentations and being attentive and aware listeners.
- Assess and provide feedback on your own writing and the writing of others.
- Practice writing, speaking, and listening regularly and develop self-confidence in these skills as a life-long communicator.
- Become aware of the choices you’re making as a user of both Standard Academic English and English for everyday use.

## Learning Outcomes

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes (CLOs):

- • Analyze your audience. Identify specific knowledge, needs, emotions, values, and experiences that are characteristic of your audience, and how those experiences are both individual and social-cultural in origin.
- • Engage your audience. Show your audience how your topic matters to them, especially by showing how your topic relates to what the audience knows, cares about, wonders about, etc. (i.e. to consider things your audience takes as “givens”)
- • Explain your ideas in ways that reflect a thorough understanding of the subject and your audience’s needs, knowledge, values, etc. by anticipating and answering the audience’s likely questions and elaborating appropriately
- • Organize texts effectively, especially by using specific “moves” in most formal writing: getting directly to the point in answering audience’s likely questions, creating and fulfilling expectations, moving from given to new information.
- • Create an appropriate voice, not only in the words you choose but also in the kinds of questions you answer or avoid, and the kinds of information you choose to include and exclude.
- • Incorporate visual information effectively.
- • Practice and hone your speaking and listening communication skills in both one-on-one, small group, and large group settings.
- • Reflect on your communication practices and their growth/development over the course of the semester.
- • Begin to establish a toolkit of “practices” that you can take with you for your reading, writing, speaking, and listening practices.

## **Guided In-Class Writing Practices**

At the beginning of each class period, we’re going to engage in a writing exercise/practice that encourages us to engage in different sensory experiences. These in-class writings will serve as entry points/sparks/challenges to help prepare you for and potentially help polish your formal assignments, as well as stretch your creative process. **While I will not be assessing these writing practices on a scheduled basis (or even collecting them), *they* *WILL be a major part of your final portfolio assignment.***

## **Informal Assignments** (5% of final grade)

Informal assignments will take various forms. They may include submitting a short piece of writing (1 page-ish typed, single spaced), bringing material that you will use in completing a formal assignment, completing an improvisational activity via email, or short in-class writing assignments. These informal writing assignments will be graded out of three points, depending on the thoughtfulness and quality of what you turn in. If you have room for improvement on one or two of these assignments, your final grade will not be hurt too much. **Please take note, though:** A string of 3/3s will significantly help your final grade, whereas a string of 1/3s may negatively impact your final grade. **You will be well-informed of the assignment format and mode for turning in each informal assignment via the course schedule.**

**1) Telling a Story** (20% of final grade)

*“No one else sees the world the way you do, so no one else can tell the stories that you have to tell.” – Charles de Lint*

This assignment requires that you tell a story that you could submit to The Moth Radio Hour storytelling program [https://themoth.org]. It should be a story that gives your audience an *insider’s perspective* on an experience that you’ve had. You’ll tell your story for an audience of people who could benefit from or learn from hearing your story. *You will share the “kernel” of your story with me and your peer writing group before you begin to write to receive feedback. In the end, though, you will be the one to decide what story you want to tell depending on your level of comfort, courage, and vulnerability.*

The assignment is also shaped on The Moth’s criteria and expectations. You can learn more about the organization/show on their website, and they offer storytelling tips [https://themoth.org/share-your-story/storytelling-tips-tricks] as well.

However, you should still practice enough in order to be able to just need to reference your text. For this assignment, I’m asking that you first ***write*** the story (but write it as you want to **tell it aloud**) and *then* tell the story during a 2-day Listening Party in class. *“Don’t raise your voice, improve your argument.” – Desmond Tutu*

## Grading Criteria

### Criteria Details:

To help you achieve the goals and outcomes for this course, I will introduce you to research and theory about narrative structures, metacognition, mindful attention/Deep Listening, audience, persuasion, visual rhetoric. Based on the understanding of the theoretical background that supports this course, you’ll learn strategies you can use to communicate effectively with your audience for any communication practice—oral, written, or visual—in any situation, both in classrooms and in your career. These strategies are not algorithms or recipes that can be followed blindly: They are actions and choices people routinely take and make—sometimes consciously, sometimes intuitively—in communicating effectively. In your assignments, therefore, you’re expected to:

- Demonstrate generic conventions and audience-centered communication
- Organize communication coherently and cohesively
- Produce grammatically and syntactically appropriate written and oral communication (including using Standard Academic English when appropriate and warranted)
- Incorporate visual information effectively
- Appraise the communication of peers
- Revise texts as part of the writing process and in response to peer and instructor feedback
- Construct claims in a variety of communication formats
- Select appropriate arguments, evidence, formats, and language to support those claims
- Maintain academic honesty and citate your sources fairly and correctly
- Reflect on your own work in terms of the learning outcomes

In grading all of your work, I’ll ask three basic questions:

- How do you identify an audience that might have a real reason to read what you have written?
- How do you describe that audience clearly and specifically, and take into account the context in which you are writing?
- How does everything in your work (narrative, details, language, visuals) create a clear, cohesive piece given the nature of your subject and given the audience and context of the communication?

For each formal assignment, I’ll provide a detailed rubric for more specific criteria that will help you understand expectations and how to meet them.

### Additional Academic Integrity Course Policy and Penalty Information:

*The* *Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities* and *The Rensselaer Graduate Student Supplement* define various forms of academic dishonesty and you should make yourself familiar with these. In this class, all assignments that are turned in for a grade must represent your own work or designated group’s work. Note: only the project is collaborative. **If you plagiarize once,** you will receive an **F on the assignment.** If you plagiarize again, you will **fail the entire course** and may be reported to the *Dean of Students* as appropriate. If you have any questions concerning this policy before submitting an assignment, please ask me for clarification. The basics: when using information from another source **(including anonymous online sources**) always cite the original text or URL and distinguish it from your own commentary. If you directly cut and paste another person’s language, you need to use it as a **quotation**. *We will talk specifically about citation styles and how to make sure that you are giving “credit” when it is required of you in spirit of scholarly collaboration.*

## **Center for Communication+Design (Comm+D)**

The Center for Global Communication+Design (Comm+D) is a FREE resource for all members of the Rensselaer community: students, faculty, and staff. You can make appointments or utilize their online resources by visiting the Comm+D website. Comm+D is staffed by experienced graduate and undergraduate tutors who will work with you, one-on-one to discuss:

- ANY type of communication project – written texts, oral presentations, websites, visual elements
- For ANY purpose – classwork, research publications, personal projects, etc.
- From ANY discipline
- At ANY stage in the process – from brainstorming and hatching plans, to revising after receiving feedback (or before submitting for feedback!), to asking questions about citation styles, to polishing your final product.

I was a graduate tutor in Comm+D during my doctoral program at Rensselear. It REALLY is an incredible resource that should be utilized more. *If you visit Comm+D to receive extra feedback on your formal assignments and bring back “proof” that you were there, you can receive 3 extra points on EACH formal assignment (except your Final Portfolio).*
