## Course Content

### Content Details:

*This old debate [about the appropriateness of Art Nouveau ‘total design’] takes on a new resonance today, when the aesthetic and utilitarian are not only conflated but all but subsumed in the commercial, and everything – not only architectural projects and art exhibitions but everything from jeans to genes – seems to be regarded as so much **design.***

\- Hal Foster

*Design and Crime, 2002*

*When the term **museum** was first used in the Renaissance in reference to private collections, it evoked a different experience from what we now know. In one of the museum’s earliest incarnations, the **Cabinet of Curiosities**, natural and art objects were jumbled together on the walls and ceilings, cupboards and drawers of one or two rooms. Their purpose was to surprise and delight: viewers had to find the special objects that attracted them and then make their own connections…*

\- Victoria Newhouse

*Towards a New Museum, 1998*

As aesthetic and cultural enterprises art and architecture are closely related. But while art generally maintains purposeful uselessness to emphasize its aesthetic capacities, architecture is always at some level utilitarian. Relationships between the aesthetics of art and the utilitarianism of architecture are highlighted when designing museums, galleries, and other spaces for art. In his essay *Design and Crime*, the art critic Hal Foster implies this question in a general sense – how much is one willing to let the aesthetic and utilitarian concerns of architecture become conflated?

It could be argued that the most visible contemporary museum projects, Ateliers Jean Nouvel’s National Museum of Qatar for example, are quite extreme conflations of aesthetics and utility, and that in many high-profile contemporary museums architecture becomes at least as much of an aesthetic statement as the art it contains. In Architectural Design 3 students will design a new gallery building on the campus of the Thomas Cole Historic Society in Catskill NY, an arts institute dedicated to the work of Thomas Cole, who was a key figure among the Hudson River School painters. In designing this building students will investigate relationships between art and architecture. Over the course of the semester students must formulate a position regarding how and how much a building to house art should be aestheticized in relation to the art it contains.

Ideas of what a building dedicated to showing art should be and how it should present art to visitors are many and have shifted over time. Today different museums and galleries present art and engage culture in different ways. In the book Towards A New Museum, Victoria Newhouse describes several categories of the contemporary museum including: *cabinets of curiosities* that surprise, delight and ask visitors to make connections between objects; *sacred spaces* that invoke qualities of ecclesiastical architecture in order to raise the experience of viewing art to religious ritual; *monographic museums* that educate visitors about a particular artist’s development; *museums as entertainment* that attempt to engage touristic rather than academic culture; and *museums as environmental art* that become active rather than passive players in how art is viewed and experienced. In designing an addition to the Thomas Cole Historic Society, students must consider how the existing institution and the new proposal falls into one or more of these categories or seeks to define a new category.

As becomes clear through reading Newhouse a museum or gallery space is never neutral in terms of how it contextualizes the art exhibited within it. A museum always has an aesthetic, social and/or political agenda. Art also has aesthetic, social and/or political agendas. For example, art may have an explicitly didactic agenda, as in Bill McDowell’s re-presentation of rejected or ‘killed’ negatives from the Farm Securities Administration (FSA) sponsored depression-era photography, which shows not only living conditions on farms during the depression, but also how some of these photographs were deemed inappropriate for public consumption by punching a hole in the original negative. On the other hand, art may have a more commercial agenda, as in the collaboration between KAWS and Uniqlo, which leverages an aesthetic based on corrupted Sesame Street characters to sell t-shirts and tote bags. In designing an addition to the Thomas Cole Society, students must consider the content of exhibitions and how architecture and exhibit design relates to the mission of the institution.

**COURSE COMPONENTS, SEQUENCE AND DELIVERABLES**

1. **Precedent Analysis and Generative Drawings**

The semester will begin with an analysis of a small built museum or gallery project in order to gain an understanding of how other architects on other sites have approached and resolved this type of project. Each section critic will curate a collection of small (approx. 5-20,000 sf) built museums and/or galleries. Students will select one project from the collection to analyze in a series of diagrams that demonstrate comprehensive understanding of elements and organization of the design. Particular attention must be paid to how programmatic and site requirements relate to the architect’s design agenda. Diagrams will be constructed as overlays on drawings collected from print publications and the internet. Existing floor plans, sections, and site plans do not need to be re-drafted.

This analysis will lead into a generative exercise that will become the basis of a formal and conceptual language for the museum project. Specifics of this generative exercise will be defined by each section critic individually.

*AD3 & GAD1 Deliverables - Precedent Analysis:*

A series of notated analytical diagrams that describe the following:

- \- architectural concept
- \- orientation to north and solar exposure
- \- gross square footage
- \- net square footage
- \- program organization and distribution
- \- horizontal and vertical circulation
- \- site circulation
- \- lighting (approach to natural/artificial light in galleries)
- \- basic structural diagram (bearing wall, post and beam, etc).

Scale and format of diagrams to be determined with section critic’s guidance.

*AD3 & GAD1 Deliverables – Generative Drawings:*

A set of speculative drawings/models based on the precedent analysis that form the basis of a formal/conceptual design language for the museum design project. Scale and format of drawings to be determined with section critic’s guidance.

2. **Assessment of the Existing Institution**

The Thomas Cole Historic Society is a regional arts institution located in Catskill, New York. The stated mission of the institution is to preserve and interpret the home and studio of Thomas Cole, an English-born American painter who is generally considered the founder of the Hudson River School movement in painting. The Hudson River School was a loose group of painters interested in the American landscape in the mid-19th century. They worked extensively in the Hudson Valley region of New York State, as well as in other parts of the country. They were primarily interested in an idea of the American landscape as a vast wilderness untouched by humans. It is inevitable that culture evolves and artists from historical periods may have held views which are outdated when considered from a contemporary perspective. In addition to preserving and interpreting Cole’s work, the institution acknowledges culture’s evolution in exhibits of contemporary artists dealing with contemporary ideas of nature, landscape, and its inhabitants. These exhibits are often inherently critical of Cole’s thinking, and the institution embraces a critical re-interpretation of Cole’s ideas from a contemporary perspective. The grounds include Cole’s historic house, other buildings, and kept and unkept landscapes. The institution has plans to expand, more details of these plans will be presented in a class field trip to the site.

Based on visiting the site, learning about the institution from its website, researching work by Cole and his Hudson River School colleagues, and becoming familiar with contemporary artist who have be exhibited at the institution, students must make their own assessment of how the existing buildings and site work and/or do not work in terms of the museum’s stated mission.

*AD3 Deliverables – Assessment of the Existing Institution:*

200-word text and 5+ annotated sketches by hand, which describe and assess functioning of the existing museum and site in relation to its stated mission.

*GAD1 Deliverables – Assessment of the Existing Institution:*

500-word text and 7+ annotated sketches by hand, which describe and assess functioning of the existing museum and site in relation to its stated mission. This text must make specific reference to the Bennington Museum masterplan and readings assigned by the section critic.

3. **Site Plan**

Understanding and representing all aspects of an architectural project’s site through drawing is a crucial step in the design process. In this case, the site includes existing buildings and landscape. Draw the Thomas Cole Historic Society site, show all relevant site information including:

- \- building footprints
- \- building entrances (service and public)
- \- parking
- \- roads
- \- sidewalks
- \- curbs
- \- curb cuts (if applicable)
- \- vegetation
- \- stairs
- \- contour lines of topography

This drawing must be presentation quality, it may be in color or black and white, it may include rendered elements and/or shadows. This drawing of existing elements will be considered a final presentation quality drawing and it will subsequently be updated to represent the design proposal.

*AD3 & GAD1 Deliverables – Site Plan:*

Drawing of existing Bennington Museum site at 1:100 scale. Clearly indicate all site elements listed above using carefully calibrated drawing graphics.

4. **Program, Building Massing, and Response to Context**

The primary goal of this semester is to design a small freestanding gallery building on the Thomas Cole Historic Society Campus with a precise tectonic language, highly considered and resolved interior spaces, and a clear daylighting strategy. Begin the design process with an exploration of program.

In architecture ‘program’ refers to activities occurring in a building and on its site. At its most fundamental level an architectural program specifies an activity and size for a building (i.e. museum), a zone within a building (i.e. exhibit spaces), and an individual space within a building (i.e. gallery). Decisions regarding design of a building are made in direct response to program. Program for this project is a 5000 square foot (sf) freestanding building containing two main programmatic zones: exhibit space, spaces where art is shown, and public space, non-exhibit spaces which all museum goers can access. Individual spaces that must be included are as follows:

- \- gallery space, 2000sf
- \- main entrance and lobby, 1000sf
- \- café, 1000sf
- \- vertical circulation, 500sf
- \- horizontal circulation, 500sf

Develop a physical massing model that explores and resolves distribution and organization of program. Note that a massing model works with simplified spatial volumes rather than detailed form, and that this massing model must represent the new building in relation to the existing building and site.

Concurrent with the massing model, develop plan and section diagrams that demonstrate how program and massing relate to context of existing building and site. Consider the following:

- \- how the new building is placed in relation to existing buildings and site
- \- how alignments of grids, axes, nodes, or heights (datums) between the new and existing buildings are created or purposefully broken
- \- how new circulation elements join with and/or alter existing circulation elements
- \- how visitors approach and enter the building
- \- how staff, maintenance and deliveries approach and enter the building
- \- how views from site to building, building to site, and within the building are maintained, created and/or altered
- \- solar orientation of the new addition

*AD3 & GAD1 Deliverables – Massing Model:*

Physical model showing how program of the new addition is organized and situated in relation to the existing building. 1/16” scale, model materials to be specified by section critic.

*AD3 Deliverables – Context diagrams:*

Annotated plan and section diagrams demonstrating how the new addition relates to specific grids, axes, nodes, datums and views in relation to existing buildings and landscape. 1/16” scale.

*GAD1 Deliverables – Context diagrams:*

Annotated plan and section diagrams demonstrating how the new building relates to specific grids, axes, nodes, datums and views in the existing building and site. 1/16” scale. 300-word text describing how proposed contextual relationships between existing and new architecture relate to the current mission and architecture of the Thomas Cole Historic Society, as well as how its mission and architecture is proposed to be supported, altered and/or critiqued through the addition. This text must make specific reference to the mission of the Thomas Cole Historic Society in relation to the Hudson River School movement and readings assigned by the section critic.

5. **Building Floorplans, Sections, and Elevations**

While it is assumed that three-dimensional computer modeling will occur through all phases of project development, two-dimensional orthographically projected floor plans, sections, and elevations will also be used instrumentally in design. These drawings MUST:

- \- be built up from scratch – a three-dimensional model may be used as a reference underlay to help produce the drawing, however it must not be included in the drawing
- \- be used to explore and evaluate design decisions
- \- bring together aspects of the precedent analysis and generative drawings with design decisions made through explorations of massing, programming, and context
- \- be used to explore how natural light is brought into exhibit and public spaces
- \- describe all components of architecture at accurate scale
- \- be used to describe accurate floor, wall, and roof thicknesses
- \- be used to describe simple floor, wall, and roof assemblies
- \- be graphically clear through effective use of line weight and line type

Refer to Architectural Graphic Standards as well as the reference file provided to the studio for information regarding architectural drawing standards.

*AD3 & GAD1 Deliverables – Building Floorplans, Sections, and Elevations:*

Two floor plans minimum (ground level, upper level), two sections minimum (longitudinal and cross), two elevations at 1/4” scale.

6. **Tectonic Section Model**

‘Tectonics’ refers to how construction is approached as an art rather than a prosaic technical endeavor. A tectonic approach to architecture implies that assembly, structure, space, and aesthetics are all intimately related. Explore a portion of the building in a tectonic section model. This model must cut through the new building, at least one existing building, and the surrounding site. It must explore and show the following:

- \- detailed design of interior space in relation to exterior of building
- \- detailed exterior envelope development of the new addition
- \- design of at least one daylighting element
- \- accurate floor, wall, and roof thicknesses
- \- simple floor, wall, and roof assemblies

This model will also be used to study daylighting with a heliosphere using the robot in the architecture shop. The robot will move a light around the model simulating daylight at different times and day and year. The model must include daylighting feature and be constructed in such a way as to facilitate this study.

*AD3 & GAD1 Deliverables – Tectonic Detail Model:*

Physical model of a section through new building, existing building, and site. 1/4” scale. Model materials to be determined with section leader’s guidance. A series of photograph documenting the daylighting study of the new building.

7. **Wall Section Detail**

A wall section detail of the project will be developed in Construction Systems. This must be presented as an integrated part of the project at the final review.

*AD3 & GAD1 Deliverables – Tectonic Detail Model:*

Wall section of the project showing full height of wall from foundation to roof. Specific requirements for the drawing will be given in Construction Systems. This drawing must be presented at the final review and will count toward the final grade in this class.

8. **Three-Dimensional Imagery**

Three rendered views of the project are required: one aerial view, one eye level view of building exterior, and interior view within the new building. Test and save multiple camera options for each view. Once views have been selected, add detail to the three-dimensional model in areas visible in the views. Images must be rendered as photo realistically as possible in Rhino/VRay. Develop a material and texture palette, a technique for handling context, and an overall aesthetic that complements and reinforces conceptual ideas of the project. Do not default to a conventional sunny day, blue sky, green vegetation aesthetic. Images must be started at least three weeks before the final review and be developed iteratively until the final review.

*AD3 & GAD1 Deliverables – Three-Dimensional Imagery:*

Three rendered views of the new addition in relation to existing building and site. Size and format of images to be determined with section leader’s guidance

9. **Project Manifesto**

A manifesto is a concise, incisive declaration of intent; it is an argument for a position taken. Write a manifesto for the project describing the following:

- \- an attitude toward aestheticization of the museum building in relation to art it contains
- \- how the new addition complements, enhances and/or alters functioning of the existing museum
- \- how program is organized
- \- how the new addition responds to context of the exiting building
- \- any other aspects of the project deemed most important
- \-

*AD3 Deliverables – Project manifesto:*

300-word text graphically incorporated into final presentation boards.

*GAD1 Deliverables – Project manifesto:*

1000-word text, supported by bibliographic citations, that argues for the project from socio-political and disciplinary perspectives. The text must address specifics of the design approach and how they position the final work in relation to historical and/or contemporary museum and/or gallery projects.

## Learning Outcomes

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes (CLOs):

- 1\. Analytical Skills – Students will demonstrate ability to read an existing building and distill an architectural idea from a case study project. Students will demonstrate ability to communicate reading of an existing building in a series of clear graphically annotated diagrams.
- 2\. Site Drawing and Planning – Students will demonstrate ability to read, understand, and draw all aspects of an architectural site plan, and demonstrate ability to effective design a simple architectural site intervention.
- 3\. Programming and Massing – Students will demonstrate ability to understand and organize a simple architectural program and show how it informs building massing.
- 4\. Context – Students will demonstrate ability to understand context in the form of existing buildings, site, and solar orientation as well as demonstrate ability to design in response to context.
- 5\. Structure – Students will demonstrate ability to understand and design realistic structural thicknesses of wall, floor, and roof assemblies.
- 6\. Two-dimensional Orthographic Drawing – Through iteratively developing a series of floor plan, section, and elevation, students will demonstrate ability clearly represent architecture and accurately design a building at a range of scales from site to preliminary floor and wall assemblies.
- 7\. Large-scale Physical Modeling – Students will demonstrate ability to construct a large-scale physical model that represents a portion of a building in detail. Students must demonstrate ability to bring together multiple materials and ability to skillfully craft a physical model.
- 8\. Three-dimensional Imagery – Students must demonstrate ability to compellingly communicate conceptual and aesthetic aspects of an architectural project.
- 9\. Writing – Students must demonstrate ability to clearly and concisely communicate an architectural agenda through using writing as a complement to graphic and physical production.

### Attendance Policy:

Attendance is required at all class meetings during the semester. Two (2) unexcused absences from class will result in the lowering of the grade by one (1) letter. Four (4) unexcused absences from class during the term result in an automatic F. Whether an absence (health, immediate family death, officially approved field / sport trip, etc.) is excused or unexcused is determined by the Students Experience Office (4th Floor Academy Hall, x8022, se@rpi.edu), upon presentation of required documentation, according to established policies and procedures (see http:// http://se.rpi.edu/policies/ea/).

Students are required to work in studio during studio hours, and have the required materials and basic tools to develop the assignments. Work includes participation in site visits, design and development, pinups, presentations, discussions and field trips. Failure to work in class with undivided attention and disruptive behavior (tardiness, leaving early, lack of participation, general socializing, etc.), as well working on assignments from other classes during class, will count as absences.

### Other Course Policies:

**Faculty Lectures**

AD3 faculty will give a series of lectures covering major architectural topics being newly introduced this semester including:

- \- Site drawing, planning, and design
- \- Architectural program and context
- \- Orthographic drawing, floorplans, sections, elevations
- \- Tectonics and architectural assemblies
- \- Three-dimensional imagery

**Digital Constructs 3**

AD3 will be accompanied by Digital Constructs 3 taught by Professors Hower, Imaeda, and Lopez on Wednesdays during studio time. Half of each studio will attend the digital session 2-4pm, and the other half will attend 4-6pm. Digital sessions will introduce new, and reinforce known, tools and techniques that support development of studio projects.

**Pin-ups**

Periodically students will be required to present their work publicly in a formal review process. These presentations are a critical part of the architectural education. It is an opportunity for the student and the faculty to judge the project and critically evaluate the process of design. It may be appropriate at points in the semester to evaluate only selected projects in order to convey information relevant to the entire class. In this situation it is important for each student to witness and participate in the review process. Even if your project is not being reviewed, the criticism and discussion should inform the evaluation of your own progress and design.

**Desk Critiques**

The design studio environment involves two-way communication. The role of the studio critic is to evolve and strengthen the student’s design process. Every time the student meets with the faculty, he or she must have new iterations of work based on previous conversations. If the student has not produced new work, the faculty reserves the right to skip the critique and continue with other students who have produced new material.

**SoA Lectures**

Attendance is MANDATORY at all School of Architecture’s lectures regardless of whether lectures occur in-person or on-line. Unexcused absences from lectures will result in one letter grade dropped from the final studio grade.

**Archiving**

At the conclusion of the course, all students will be required to submit all final digital works to Publications. Publications will assign a liaison to the second-year studio; all students will be responsible for submitting work to the liaison. In addition, selected physical material will be collected and stored between semesters so that it may be photographed or scanned by Publications. Grades for the semester will not be released until this material is received.

### Additional Academic Integrity Course Policy and Penalty Information:

**Academic Integrity**

The Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities defines various forms of Academic Dishonesty and procedures for responding to them. All forms are violations of the trust between students and teachers. Student-teacher relationships are built on trust. For example, students must trust that teachers have made appropriate decisions about the structure and content of the courses they teach, and teachers must trust that the assignments that students turn in are their own performance. Acts that violate this trust undermine the educational process.

**Policy on collaboration and cheating/plagiarism**

Interdisciplinary collaboration is a critical component of this course. All assignments that are turned in for a grade must accurately represent the student’s own work, and properly cite authorship of work that was not solely completed by the student. Any miss-representation of work shall be evaluated and could result in a failing grade for the assignment. A subsequent infraction by the student will result in a failing grade for the course.
