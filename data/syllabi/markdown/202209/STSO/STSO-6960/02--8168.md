## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

Decolonizing Social Theory was designed to unfold conceptual frameworks and methodologies useful for comprehending current and chronic crises. The course aspires to enable students to enter into conversations with and about the past 50-60 years of anticolonial, postcolonial, and decolonial thought in intersection with feminist and antiracist work in STS, and the interpretivist or post-positivist humanities and social sciences broadly construed. We will trace the multiple genealogies and trajectories that have contributed to the emergence and enactment of this form of situated knowledge. This process we will refer to as unfolding.

Postcolonial, feminist, post-positivist, and antiracist STS are critical interdisciplinary formations that have cross-fertilized with cultural studies, studies of colonial discourse and postcolonial studies, Critical Race Theory and Critical Race Feminism, queer theory and sexuality studies, and the many disciplinary and adisciplinary formations that playfully and critically inform each. We will explore these multiplicitous and pluricentric positions to the greatest extent possible given constraints of time and space.

Professor Campbell apologizes in advance for the sheer amount of reading, listening, presenting, and/or viewing required. We will divide the labor wherever possible so that you are reading, listening, and viewing with and for one another. Please prepare to summarize, synthesize, and otherwise record your impressions via voice memo, reading, listening, or viewing notes, so as to recall and recast your impressions. The conversation will be structured, but these structures will not—indeed cannot—contain the full extent of your learning this semester.

Engagement will be assessed through six (6) arcs (below). Graduate courses are designed to enable you to broaden and deepen committed engagement with STS as a field, including as-yet unnamed STSs enacted through your own research and conceptual contributions. This course steers clear of “core” concepts, “foundational” theories, “canonical” texts but hopes that you will fold these into your own thinking. To ensure you acquire a comprehensive grasp of STS, which works in synergistic relationship with other knowledge formations, we will clarify genealogies, configurations, discourses, situations, and trajectories of feminist and antiracist STS without prescribing yours. The practical goal is intense immersion.

***Week 4* Situating Science Studies I: Place Matters**

**Friday, September 24**

The feminist transformation of science studies came early enough that it may be thought of as foundational, despite the ongoing marginalization implied by the label. Feminist scientists and historians and philosophers of science, the latter schooled in logical positivism, began a critique of science studies and feminist movements even as they produced both. Their critical endeavor emphasized that the places where science is done were largely masculine domains dominated by modes of expertise and objectivity that foreclosed accounts founded in tacit knowledge, proximity and respect between researchers and researched, and situationally specific elements. They became targets during the “science wars.”

**Readings:**

**ARCS OF ASSESSMENT**

**Arc 1: Structured Provocation (PREPARE BEFORE CLASS)**

Provocations “trouble” the texts we read for the day by introducing critical projects that are orthogonal or oppositional, occluded or excluded, in relation to the days’ readings. This is your chance to engage the texts under collective examination. Provocations are typically short, pointed, written up formally in 5-7 pages and sent to the professor the night before class is to meet. You are responsible to introduce the provocation whenever the discussion facilitator turns the conversation to the texts you examined. Provocations may draw upon **additional** material, texts, and contexts, relating the texts for the day with *different* sources, intentions, or characteristics from those under “official” discussion. However, they should be directly (not tangentially) related to the texts on the syllabus. Provocations may include video, performance, installation art, or writings that provide substantive examples of STS work in art worlds, digital media, or data science.

**Arc 2: Discussion Facilitation (IN CLASS)**

On the date for which you sign up, you are responsible for introducing materials that will help frame and facilitate discussion for 1 hour. Draw upon the main points of our readings for that day and choose one of the following options: 1. Add context; 2. “Apply” concepts to a specific analytic situation or text of your own choosing; 3. Draw upon additional material to provide genealogy. Above all, keep the conversation moving, and ensure fairness in terms of who gets to speak, how long they do so, etc.

**Arc 3: Rumination (BEFORE AND DURING CLASS)**

Ruminations are critical, written reflections **on the readings** (required and/or optional) presented in the class where those readings are first scheduled for discussion facilitation. You must turn in the 5-7-page rumination paper on the day you present—you do not need to read the paper (indeed will not have time to do so). All written work for this class should be polished—i.e. I expect finished pieces of formal academic writing, on the basis of which you present your reflections to class. You may use presentation software, but if so, that too should be shared with the class either as an emailed link or PPT presentation.

**Arc 4: Meditation**

A “meditation” paper enables you to begin exploring the area or artifact that you will write about in your final paper, “Decolonizing X.” Use the meditation to start exploring the theoretical concepts you will integrate from social theory and postcolonial and decolonial thought with cultural artifacts in your own arena of interest and study.

**Arc 5: Synthesis (AT THE BEGINNING OF EACH CLASS)**

Capacity to improvise and synthesize-- to “think on your feet” is essential to success in graduate school and elsewhere. This arc requires that you connect across the components of each of your classes, and provide an oral synthesis of the previous seminar in the first **10 minutes** of the next class. You will be opening the next class, framing continuities, and synthesizing the previous class by drawing together the streams of our discussion, reminding us where we have been, and raising questions that help build continuities in times of discontinuities. The synthesis is not to exceed 10 minutes. Practice.

**Arc 6: Decolonizing X (AFTER CLASS)**

Your final written assignment for this course is a position paper summarizing and applying your current understanding of Decolonizing as a verb to a topic within the fields of STS, which by now you realize is comprised of interlocking “science studies.” Choose a focus around which to arrange your characterizations; add texts and contexts to those discussed in the course (you may also include your provocation); but reflect as fully as possible upon the body of knowledge presented in this course. This reflection should be approximately 20 pages, typed, double-spaced, polished, and deeply documented. It should be written with your portfolio in mind.

***Week 6*** **Postcolonial and Orientalist Challenges to Western Thought**

**Required reading:**

This special issue of a journal that found itself enacting studies of “colonial discourse,” “minority discourse,” and “postcolonial theory,” based upon the 1982 essay by Said titled “Travelling Theory” (1982). Said indicated that for theory to be alive, it must be grounded in specific historical context, to travel (for which he uses the example of “reification,” which emerged from Georg Lukacs’ Budapest, Hungary, in the 1920s). The patterned movement of theory requires:

- A point or points of origin, political configuration or social formation, place and time;
- A distance travelled as ideas move to other times and places along different trajectories and through various contexts;
- A set of *material conditions* of acceptance or resistance that confront the transplanted theory;
- A transformation of theory to new uses, new positions.

**Week 11 Subaltern Standpoints**

**Tuesday, November 8**

Feminist transformation of science studies grew out of and alongside colonial and postcolonial and decolonial studies. Feminist scientists, historians, and philosophers of science, the latter schooled in logical positivism, began to critique science studies even as they began to read and produce it. They aimed to change feminist movements as well as labor, pro-democracy, civil rights, Black Power, Third World, women’s health, reproductive rights, and other movements in the late 1960s and early 1970s. Theirs was a critical endeavor emphasizing that science has been a largely masculine domain dominated by modes of expertise, value neutrality, and objectivity that foreclosed tacit knowledge, relational proximity between researchers and researched, and situationally specific elements.

**Required Reading:**

1. Julian Go, Ch. 4, “The Subaltern Standpoint,” and “Conclusion: for a Third Wave,” pp. 143-200.

**Rules of engagement for seminar**

***With gratitude for the work of the Harm Reduction Coalition***

- Make Space / Take Space. Before speaking twice, make space for others to share first.
- Be mindful of power and how it manifests: Speaking over, cutting people off, raising your voice, monopolizing space and time, speaking about someone else’s experience without having direct knowledge.
- Brave space: Take a positive risk, lean into discomfort. If you are uncomfortable, ask yourself why. *Is it your privilege or vulnerability?* View discomfort as space for growth.
- Disclosure and confidentiality: Respect peoples’ agency and stories as their own. Don’t disclose people or their stories without their consent. When someone tells you their experience, don’t argue with them.
- Do not colonize people’s stories or expect people to share or disclose their trauma.
- Become familiar with the concept of emotional labor and don’t expect it from people around you. When freely given, emotional labor holds our communities together, so we ask that you carefully consider both giving and receiving of this most important but easily exploited kind of work.
- Support nonviolent communication, take threats of violence and harassment seriously.
- We do not tolerate hate speech and micro-aggressions, sexual harassment, or misogyny.

**Our Commitment to Our Collective Safety**

We work to resolve issues that arise as a community, choosing to avoid engaging with law enforcement.

**Self-Care & Joy**

Graduate school can be overwhelming. We encourage self-nurturing and self-care. We encourage you to explore local resources including meditation, yoga, affinity meetings, and silent, contemplative space for stepping away from the vibrant energy of grad school. The campus has a pool and gym that provides free access to students, and wellness classes including, at times, goat yoga.

Thank you for all that you do to make the world a better place – Harm Reduction STS.

### Academic Policies:

- ### Academic Integrity

  Student-teacher relationships are built on trust. For example, students must trust that teachers have made appropriate decisions about the structure and content of the courses they teach, and teachers must trust that the assignments that students turn in are their own. Acts that violate this trust undermine the educational process. The Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities and The Graduate Student Supplement define various forms of Academic Dishonesty and you should make yourself familiar with these. In this class, all assignments that are turned in for a grade must represent the student’s own work. In cases where help was received, or teamwork was allowed, a notation on the assignment should indicate your collaboration.

  Violations of academic integrity may also be reported to the appropriate Dean (Dean of Students for undergraduate students or the Dean of Graduate Education for graduate students, respectively).

  If you have any question concerning this policy before submitting an assignment, please ask for clarification. In addition, you can visit the following site for more information on our Academic Integrity Policy: [Students Rights, Responsibilities, and Judicial Affairs](https://info.rpi.edu/dean-students/student-rights-responsibilities-and-judicial-affairs).

### Additional Academic Integrity Course Policy and Penalty Information:

There are real consequences for acts of academic dishonesty. Please do not place either of us in the position of having to pursue them. *Submission of any assignment in violation of the Academic Integrity policy will result in an academic (grading) penalty. You cannot earn a grade for work that is not your own; you forfeit the entire percentage of your grade for that assignment if you submit work that is not your own. In addition to the academic penalty, there are judicial processes to determine further sanctions for academic dishonesty and misconduct that are beyond faculty control. A grade of zero will be given on the first assignment where a violation is detected. If there is a subsequent infraction the student will receive a grade of F for the course.*
