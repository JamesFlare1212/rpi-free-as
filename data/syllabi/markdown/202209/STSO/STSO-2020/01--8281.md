### Additional Information for Course Information Section:

***Course Overview*** As compared to the smaller design projects that you encountered in Studios I and II, Studio III is built around a semester-long project. Our course will operate as a simulated, professional design studio. In terms of content, the focus of this studio will be

- engineering design methods and their critical deconstruction;
- prototyping (for both function and form); and
- further extensions to the designer identity work that you began in Studio II.

Each year we choose a different theme for Studio III, one oriented towards our focus on societal systems and “complexity.” This year the theme is:

**“Displacement”**

Interpret this broadly to include such things as political access, access to education, access to different cultures, in addition to more traditional topics such as universal design and design for disability.

The course is divided into the following units:

- Problem identification and formulation
- Concept generation &rapid prototyping
- Evaluation
- Design audit and iteration
- Fabrication and presentation

Each unit will run approximately three weeks. See the separate document, “Course Schedule,” for detailed schedule information and deadlines.

***Studio Format*** The class will operate as a studio environment throughout, with hands-on exercises governing the class activities.

Classes will start with check-ins followed by discussions of assigned readings. You will need to schedule and spend time outside of class in order to complete all work required for this course; You are expected to manage the time available in and out of class to function as a design team.

## Learning Outcomes

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes (CLOs):

- \- Efficacy using engineering design methods and formal design processes, as well as their critical deconstruction in ways that are useful to your professional practice as a designer.
- \- Demonstrated skills in prototyping, making and fabrication, evaluation, and design aesthetics
- \- Developing your design identity and capacity to engage with STS
- \- Demonstration of effective group work and other professional skill sets

## Grading Criteria

### Criteria Details:

Major assignments:

Unit I: Problem Identification & Formulation

- Design proposal
- Proposal presentation
- Peer critique

Unit II: Concept Generation

- Proposal presentation
- Peer critique

Unit III: Evaluation, Mock-Ups & Communication

- Evaluation
- Prototype report
- Initial design report & presentation

Unit IV: Design Process Audit & Iteration

- Design process audit
- Peer critique
- Revised design process
- Improved prototypes

Unit V: Final Fabrication & Presentation

***Grades:*** The following serves as a uniform evaluation criterion for all assignments:

*Outstanding (A+):* Work that significantly exceeds instructor expectations. Work that demonstrates an understanding of and/or the mastery of a practice that is clearly beyond the expectations of this course.  
*Strong (A**):* Work that exceeds instructor expectations for student work and performance. Professional conduct in studio.  
*Solid (B):*Work that solidly meets instructor expectations regarding specified learning objectives. Also indicates solid attendance and good participation in class, as well as contributions to teamwork. Adequate time devoted to assignments outside of class.  
*Developing (C):*Some clear progress towards stated learning objectives, but current ability or knowledge falls short of learning outcomes. May include deficiencies related to attendance, professionalism, class participation, successful group work, and effort and/or contributions to the team.  
*Basic (D):*Demonstrates a basic understanding of the subject, or a rudimentary ability to follow a defined process, but has not achieved a level of mastery where it is advisable to continue to the next studio in DIS. May also include significant deficiencies related to attendance, professionalism, class participation, successful group work, and effort and/or contributions to the team.

- ### Academic Integrity

  Student-teacher relationships are built on trust. For example, students must trust that teachers have made appropriate decisions about the structure and content of the courses they teach, and teachers must trust that the assignments that students turn in are their own. Acts that violate this trust undermine the educational process. The Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities and The Graduate Student Supplement define various forms of Academic Dishonesty and you should make yourself familiar with these. In this class, all assignments that are turned in for a grade must represent the student’s own work. In cases where help was received, or teamwork was allowed, a notation on the assignment should indicate your collaboration.

  Violations of academic integrity may also be reported to the appropriate Dean (Dean of Students for undergraduate students or the Dean of Graduate Education for graduate students, respectively).

  If you have any question concerning this policy before submitting an assignment, please ask for clarification. In addition, you can visit the following site for more information on our Academic Integrity Policy: [Students Rights, Responsibilities, and Judicial Affairs](https://info.rpi.edu/dean-students/student-rights-responsibilities-and-judicial-affairs).
