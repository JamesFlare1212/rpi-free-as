## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

A detailed survey of important topics in the neurosciences. Some of the topics to be covered in this class are: basic mechanisms of neural signaling (neurophysiology, synaptic transmission, and molecular signaling); understanding of sensation/movement and in particular how it relates to neuroanatomy; neurodevelopment and how the mature brain can change (plasticity); complex brain functions and neurological disease. May be used to fulfill the Culminating Experience requirement in Biology. BIOL 4100 is an undergraduate course taught together with the BIOL 6100 graduate section.  
From Neuron to Behavior is a lecture course and assignments are comprised of major exams and article critique writing assignments. Students enrolled in the 6100 graduate section have an additional paper and presentation assignment.

## Course Goals

### Goals:

Upon completion of this course, students should have acquired a solid foundation of knowledge for the major topics relevant to the field of neuroscience.

## Learning Outcomes

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes (CLOs):

- 4100/6100 - Describe the basic electrophysiological and biochemical properties of brain cells by the conclusion of the course.
- 4100/6100 - Discuss the development of the brain by the conclusion of the course.
- 4100/6100 - Discuss how cells of the central nervous system communicate with other cells in the central nervous system and with the peripheral nervous system.
- 4100/6100 - Describe how the brain interprets its surroundings through the senses (sight, hearing, taste, touch, and smell).
- 4100/6100 - Describe the role of the brain in higher, complex behaviors (i.e., cognition).
- 4100/6100 - Describe the pathophysiology of neurological, neurodevelopmental, and psychiatric disorders.
- 4100/6100 - Apply basic concepts in neurobiology to challenging questions in the field of neuroscience by the conclusion of the course.
- 6100 - Apply basic concepts in neurobiology and be able to analyze and interpret neuroscience data in research papers by the conclusion of the course.

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes Assessment Measures:

Exams and article critiques will be use to assess content-based (i.e., neuroscience knowledge) student learning outcomes for all students enrolled in the 4100 or 6100 sections.  
A review paper and classroom presentation will be used to assess graduate student's (6100) ability to analyze and interpret neuroscience data.

***Other Course Policies:***

ELECTRONIC DEVICES - Students are requested to turn off all cellular phones or set them to an inaudible setting unless specific prior arrangements are made with Dr. Flint. If your phone is activated, please quietly leave the room to prevent further classroom disruption. Do not sit in the classroom and talk. If such interruptions occur repeatedly, you will be asked to leave the class or security will be contacted to confiscate the electronic device. Some devices have the ability to take pictures or video. Under no circumstances are students permitted to use these in this course. Students do not have permission to photograph or record the professor, other students, or material presented in this class.

TIME COMMITMENT - It is expected that students will read the assigned material prior to coming to class and that students will spend a MINIMUM of 3 hours studying outside of class for every 1 hour of class time. This means that students should spend at least 12 hours per week working on the material for this course outside of classroom contact time. This time should include reading the assigned portions of the textbook, reviewing/studying the textbook and class notes, and preparing for exams.

LECTURES - Lectures are not designed to cover everything in a textbook and no textbook is comprehensive. The majority of the lectures will be comprised of selected topics from the book. Not all of the material found in the text will be covered in class lectures, but students are still responsible for all of the material in the assigned readings. Some of the material covered in class will not be found in the textbook, so it is important that students attend class and take good notes. Students are encouraged to ask questions and will be held responsible for knowing all assigned readings and lecture information.

CRITICAL REACTION PAPERS – The course will be roughly divided into 5 sections, one for each of the 5 major exams, and students will be required to find and read a peer reviewed empirical journal article relevant to some aspect of the material covered in each of the 5 sections of the course. Students will then be required to write a critical reaction of the article they selected using MS-Word. Students may select an article relevant to any of the material that was covered in that particular section of the course.

A peer reviewed primary empirical journal article is a publication in a peer reviewed journal that represents the first reporting of results from one or more experiments. Such articles traditionally have an introduction, methods (including a clear description of the procedures utilized), results (normally involving statistical analyses and data presentations in tables and/or graphs), and a discussion or conclusion section. Students are encouraged to contact Dr. Flint with questions regarding the appropriateness of an article if they are at all in doubt.

Each Critical Reaction will be comprised of a 700-750 word analysis of the article, excluding any title or reference pages, and students must provide a reference citation of the article being critiqued at the end. Students should also be prepared to provide a copy of the article to the TA and Dr. Flint if requested. Since this assignment is restricted to a single research article, and because students must provide a reference citation, there will be no need to provide any in-text references/citations in the paper. Please note that this is an exception to the rule where paraphrased material should normally be cited in the tex. Students are prohibited from using direct quotes. Any information from the article should be paraphrased in the student’s own words.

Critical Reaction Papers must be submitted online in LMS/Blackboard by 11:59 pm the day before the scheduled exam. Papers submitted late, but within 24 hrs of the deadline will receive a 25% deduction (5 pts). Papers received between 24 and 48 hrs late will receive a 50% deduction, etc.

Critical Reaction Papers will be worth 20 points each. These papers are NOT summaries of the articles that were read, but instead should be critiques of the articles. In composing each critical reaction, students might consider one or more of the following:

1. How does the information in this article enhance or expand your knowledge of a topic covered in the class?
2. Is the hypothesis for each experiment clearly stated and do you think that it is an important issue to study? Why or why not?
3. What are the strengths of the study and why do you consider them strengths?
4. What are the weaknesses of the study and why do you consider them weaknesses?
5. Do the authors under or over generalize the impact of their results? Please explain.
6. Do the authors use appropriate statistical analyses of their data and/or appropriately present their data in tables and/or graphs?
7. Are the author’s findings correctly interpreted? Please explain.
8. Are the techniques, methods, and/or procedures utilized in the experiment(s) appropriate for the purpose of the study.

Critical Reach Papers will be graded using the following rubric:

- Writing = 5 points
  - 5 pts – Paper is well-written and organized in a meaningful manner with appropriate paragraphs. Paper is devoid of spelling, grammar, and punctuation errors.
  - 4 pts – Paper is moderately well-written and/or moderately well-organized and/or has 2-4 spelling, grammar, and/or punctuation errors.
  - 3 pts – Writing and/or organization is average and/or there are 5-7 spelling, grammar, and/or punctuation errors.
  - 2 pts – Paper is poorly written and/or poorly organized and/or the paper has 8-10 spelling, grammar, and/or punctuation errors.
  - 0-1 pt – Paper is poorly written and/or poorly organized and/or had more than 10 spelling, grammar, and/or punctuation errors.
- Content
- - 14-15 pts – Paper provides an excellent critique, examining the article thoroughly for at least 4 key issues. There is no unnecessary summary of material from the article.
  - 12-13 pts – Paper provides a good critique, thoroughly examining the article for 3 key issues and/or there is one instance where there was unnecessary summary of material from the article.
  - 8-11 pts – Paper provides an average critique, presenting shallow examination of 3 or more issues or a thorough examination of only 2 key issues and/or there are two instances where unnecessary summaries of material from the article were provided.
  - 0-7 pts – Paper provides a below average or poor critique, presenting a shallow examination of fewer than 3 issues or a thorough examination of only 1 key issue and/or the paper contains significant unnecessary summaries of portions of the article.
  - Content (paper clearly focuses on providing a critical reaction to the article and does not summarize the article beyond what is necessary to place the critical reaction into context) = 15 points

Note: Critiques that do not fall within the word limit for the assignment or that use quotations from the article will receive additional point deductions.

EXAMS - There will be a total of five major exams during the semester. Each exam will be comprised of multiple-choice questions covering the assigned readings, lectures, and presentations that have occurred since the previous exam, and each exam will be worth 100 points (50 questions worth 2 pts each). Students should anticipate that there will be questions from the text that were not discussed in class, so it is important that students have a firm understanding of all the assigned readings. Exams must be completed independently. Once the exam is distributed and students have begun, no one will be permitted to leave the classroom and return to work on the exam.

MAKE-UP EXAMS - If a student fails to attend an exam and they can provide a legitimate written and verifiable excuse, then a make-up exam may be scheduled. Valid excuses may include a serious illness, death in the family, uncontrollable emergency situation, or participation in a legitimate RPI-related activity. Students who have scheduled valid excuses should notify Dr. Flint immediately so that arrangements may be made to take the make-up exam. All excuses must be provided in writing and must be submitted prior to scheduling the make-up exam. If a student misses an exam and does not have an adequate written excuse, he/she may schedule and take a make-up exam, with the understanding that there will be an automatic deduction of 25% from the value of the exam. Make-up exams, regardless of the excuse, may be of a different format than the original exam (e.g., oral, fill-in-the-blank, essay, etc.). Make-up exams need to be completed within 1 week of returning to campus. Contact Dr. Flint immediately if you miss an exam for any reason.

GRADE APPEALS – Students will not be granted access to completed exams. In other words, graded exams will not be returned. However, upon request, Dr. Flint will provide a list of the topics associated with each question a student missed. Students with questions regarding the exams will have an opportunity to obtain clarification under specific circumstances. Any questions must be presented in writing by the end of the second class period following the day of the exam.

GRADING CURVES/ADJUSTMENTS – Exams may be subject to a curve. Whether or not a curve is implemented and the size of the curve will depend on the overall class performance and grade distribution. Curves will take the form of raw points added directly onto the student’s score on the test and will be applied equally to all students in the class. At no time will a student’s grade be lowered as part of a curve. Historically, curves have not be necessary in this class.

GRADE DISCLOSURE - Exams grades will be entered in LMS and will be made available within 48 hrs of taking the exam. Article critiques will be graded and posted in LMS within 5 business days of their due date.

Paper Formatting Requirements:

- Paper must be written and submitted in MS-Word
- Body of the paper must be 8-12 pages in length, excluding title and reference pages. Images, graphs, tables, etc., may be included in the paper, but do not count toward the paper length requirement.
- Size 12 of a standard font such as Times New Roman should be used
- Double spaced
- 1 inch margins
- Use of headings and subheadings to organize the paper
- Title page including paper title, student’s name, course, and date
- Reference pages that include all references cited in the paper. Do not provide references if they are not cited in the paper
- Reference style and in-text citation style – student may use an accepted formatting style for their disciple
- No quotations – all material referenced in the paper that comes from another source must be paraphrased into the student’s own words and the sources should be cited in the text appropriately as well as on the reference page

Paper Content Requirements:

- Review of an issue relevant to behavior and/or cognitive neuroscience
  - o Approximately 50% of the paper should be focused on behavior or cognitive aspects of the topic and 50% on the neuroscientific aspects of the topic (e.g., neuroanatomy, neurochemistry, neurophysiology, etc.)
- Minimum of 15 peer reviewed empirical research articles must be cited in the body of the paper. Additional articles are welcome, as are other sources (e.g., meta-analyses, review articles, books, book chapters) as long as there are at least 15 peer reviewed empirical research articles included. Please avoid referencing web sites, dictionaries, and other secondary sources.
