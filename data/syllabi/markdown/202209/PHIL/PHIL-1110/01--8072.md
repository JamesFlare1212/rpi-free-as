## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

This course invites you into the world of philosophical ideas and reasoning -- to join a great conversation that has unfolded over the past 2,500 years since philosophy began in Ancient Greece. We will explore such issues as the nature of Philosophy, whether reason can allow us to make better judgments about right and wrong and about better and worse ways of living, the relationship that exists between mind and body, and whether philosophy has anything to contribute to discussions about the existence and nature of God. This course will aim to help you develop your skills in reading, discussing and evaluating philosophical concepts.

## Learning Outcomes

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes (CLOs):

- 1\. Students will be able to explain and communicate the essential characteristics of Philosophy and the uses and value of Philosophy.
- 2\. Students will be able to identify the main types of arguments and explain how arguments are used in Philosophy.
- 3\. Students will be able to articulate and explain major approaches within philosophical ethics, including Utilitarianism, Deontology (duty-based ethics), Virtue Ethics, and Ethics of Care.
- 4\. In the context of ethics, students will be able to explain and utilize the distinction between intrinsic value and instrumental value.
- 5\. Students will be able to articulate such concepts and categories within Philosophy of Mind as Substance Dualism, Property Dualism, and Physicalism.
- 6\. Students will be able to identify and discuss some of the most influential issues and arguments within Philosophy of Religion having to do with religious experience, arguments for the existence of God, and the prevalence of evil and suffering in the world.

## Course Calendar

### Calendar Details:

The course calendar has been provided to you in a separate document which is also available on the LMS site for the course. However, a condensed version of the Calendar is as follows:  
Week of 08/29: Overview of course; Basics of arguments, necessary and sufficient conditions.  
Week of 09/05: Knowledge and how it might be acquired, difference between knowing and thinking, value and benefits of philosophy.  
Week of 09/12: Plato's Allegory of the Cave as classic image of the role of Philosophy. Intro to Ethics, intrinsic vs. instrumental value, Principle of Impartiality.  
Week of 09/19: Bentham's and Mill's versions of Utilitarianism, strengths and criticisms of Utilitarianism.  
Week of 09/26: Review for Exam 1, EXAM 1  
Week of 10/03: Kant and duty-based ethics, hypothetical vs. categorical imperatives, autonomous vs. heteronymous will, 1st and 2nd formulations of C.I.  
Week of 10/10: Trolley Problem and ethics settings for autonomous vehicles.  
Week of 10/17: Virtue ethics and ethics of care; Martin Luther King, Jr. on building social conscience through non-violent tension.  
Week of 10/24: Survey of positions on Mind-Body relationship; Descartes, knowledge, and interactive dualism.  
Week of 10/31: Review for Exam 2, EXAM 2.  
Week of 11/07: Neuroscience, Physicalism, and disorders of mind and perception; Nagel's "Knowledge Argument" and Jackson's "Qualia Argument" against Physicalism, and David Lewis' response.  
Week of 11/14: Overview of topics and positions in Philosophy of Religion.  
Week of 11/21: Hume and criticism of classical cosmological and teleological arguments; Problem of Evil.  
Week of 11/28: Buddhism as non-Western religious/philosophical tradition; Buddhism on removal of suffering in life.  
Week of 12/05: Review for Exam 3.

- ### Academic Integrity

  Student-teacher relationships are built on trust. For example, students must trust that teachers have made appropriate decisions about the structure and content of the courses they teach, and teachers must trust that the assignments that students turn in are their own. Acts that violate this trust undermine the educational process. The Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities and The Graduate Student Supplement define various forms of Academic Dishonesty and you should make yourself familiar with these. In this class, all assignments that are turned in for a grade must represent the student’s own work. In cases where help was received, or teamwork was allowed, a notation on the assignment should indicate your collaboration.

  Violations of academic integrity may also be reported to the appropriate Dean (Dean of Students for undergraduate students or the Dean of Graduate Education for graduate students, respectively).

  If you have any question concerning this policy before submitting an assignment, please ask for clarification. In addition, you can visit the following site for more information on our Academic Integrity Policy: [Students Rights, Responsibilities, and Judicial Affairs](https://info.rpi.edu/dean-students/student-rights-responsibilities-and-judicial-affairs).
