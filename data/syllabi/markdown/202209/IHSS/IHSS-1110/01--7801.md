### Additional Information for Course Information Section:

**What will we cover in this class? : Course Description**

Nature/Society is the core seminar for the Vasudha (Sustainability) Living & Learning Community. In this course we will explore sustainability through human/environment relationships. How did the concepts “nature” and “society” develop? How did they become categories of life conceived of as distinct and even oppositional? This semester we will situate our work within the theme, “Entangled Nature,” culminating in a project based on field research about human-extrahuman interaction you find beautiful, inspiring, useful, or hopeful. Science, technology, political and economic institutions transform the earth’s land, water, and atmosphere. We will probe these relationships through issues of resource access, pollution, and inequality tied to ideas of sustainability.

**How will we do this?** Through critical reading, discussion, fieldwork, and analysis.

We will engage critically with work from a range of approaches, including: ecologists, anthropologists, historians, activists, Marxist theorists, biologists, sociologists, philosophers, epidemiologists, journalists, and conservationists.

First we will explore the historical development of the distinct concepts, “nature” and “culture.” To explore contemporary human/nature entanglements we will read Anna Tsing’s ethnography, *The Mushroom at the End of the World*. Place-based experiential learning will be central to our work this semester. We will practice the ethnographic research method participant observation in our own backyards. Our work will include a Walk Log with guided walking exercises and reflections, investigating the conditions and context of the Anthropocene from different fields of expertise, and participating with local community organizations focused on food security and environmental justice. For our final project we will use Tsing’s “Arts of Noticing**”** as a guiding principle. To practice the arts of noticing means seeing patterns and big picture connections without letting go of vital detail and local context. We will describe sites of entangled nature that present useful guides for a sustainable present and imagined future.

- ### Academic Integrity

  Student-teacher relationships are built on trust. For example, students must trust that teachers have made appropriate decisions about the structure and content of the courses they teach, and teachers must trust that the assignments that students turn in are their own. Acts that violate this trust undermine the educational process. The Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities and The Graduate Student Supplement define various forms of Academic Dishonesty and you should make yourself familiar with these. In this class, all assignments that are turned in for a grade must represent the student’s own work. In cases where help was received, or teamwork was allowed, a notation on the assignment should indicate your collaboration.

  Violations of academic integrity may also be reported to the appropriate Dean (Dean of Students for undergraduate students or the Dean of Graduate Education for graduate students, respectively).

  If you have any question concerning this policy before submitting an assignment, please ask for clarification. In addition, you can visit the following site for more information on our Academic Integrity Policy: [Students Rights, Responsibilities, and Judicial Affairs](https://info.rpi.edu/dean-students/student-rights-responsibilities-and-judicial-affairs).
