## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

This course explores the role of religion in different cultures and the everyday lives of people around the world. Herein, this course will introduce students to key concepts, themes and debates when exploring religious traditions through different methodologies, including: (1) cultural anthropology and sociology; (2) psychology; (3) phenomenology; and (4) political science. Accordingly, our exploration will cover the myths, rituals and philosophical/theological perspectives that define the identities of world religions, including: (a) indigenous religions of America, Africa and Japan; (b) Hinduism; (c) Jainism; (d) Buddhism; (e) Confucianism; (f) Daoism; (g) Judaism; (h) Christianity; (i) Islam; and (j) Sikhism. Special attention will be devoted to discussing contemporary issues such as the relation between national politics and religion, violence and religion, climate change and religion, and magical thinking in technology and science.

## Course Goals

### Goals:

The goals of this course are to familiarize students with the different methodologies for studying religions so as to reveal the variegated beliefs and practices that define the identities of world religions. Students will learn how to: (1) analyze and synthesize both primary and secondary sources; (2) conduct a comparative inquiry of world religions; (3) examine and evaluate theories and arguments, specifically in regard to methodologies, that shape our understanding of religious traditions; and (4) engage in the general practice of hermeneutics so as to deepen their appreciation of the historical/cultural contexts by which religious beliefs and practices present themselves to both practitioners and scholars of religion.

## Learning Outcomes

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes (CLOs):

- After completing this course, students will be able to think critically about a range of beliefs, theories and arguments that are salient to religious traditions.
- After completing this course, students will be able to demonstrate their ability to evaluate world religions through different methodologies, including: (a) cultural anthropology and sociology; (b) psychology (Freud and Jung); (c) phenomenology; and (d) political science.
- After completing this course, students will be able to identify and explain key concepts, theories, thinkers and contemporary issues that have shaped our understanding of world religions.
- After completing this course, students will be able to engage in the practice of religious hermeneutics by comparative exploring world religions.
- After completing this course, students will be able to effectively communicate their perspectives through essays, seminar discussions and panel presentations.
- After completing this course, students will be able to conduct and present their research in The Chicago Manual of Style format.

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes Assessment Measures:

All assignments in this course - discussions, presentations and essays - will be evaluated in light of the following criteria: (1) ability to write clearly by following grammatical rules and conforming to the guidelines of The Chicago Manual of Style; (2) ability to demonstrate critical thinking by assessing theories, methods and arguments that shape our understanding of world religions; (3) ability to effectively reference and examine both primary and secondary sources included in the course reading list.  
**Assignments and Evaluation** *Two Essays (1000 words):* For each short essay assignment, students will be given constructive feedback and comments. After submitting their essays for a preliminary review, each student will be given two weeks to edit and make content changes prior to submitting their work for a final grade. Essay Prompts will be centered around specific theorists/arguments/methodologies/issues that help reveal the variegated practices and beliefs of religions from around the world. Essays will be evaluated according to one's ability to: (1) write clearly; (2) apply the methods of studying religions to specific cases and issues; (3) employ the basic skills of critical thinking; and (4) evaluate and create arguments in defense of a hermeneutical perspective.  
*Research Paper (1500-2000 words)*: Students will have the opportunity to conduct their own research upon a specific topic concerning the religions of the global world, and the methods by which we interpret them. Students will compose a short 100-word abstract detailing their research thesis; abstracts should be submitted no later than 10/14/2022. All papers will be submitted for a grade no later than 12/19/2022. Research papers will be evaluated according to one's ability to: (1) write clearly; (2) apply the tools of critical thinking to specific theories and issues; (3) identify and explain their research methodology; and (4) evaluate and create arguments in light of their references (no less than seven academic sources).  
*Research Panel Presentation*: Each student will participate in a panel discussion whereby they will have the opportunity to present their thesis, arguments/methodology and conclusions in regard to their research papers. They will also have the opportunity to engage in a dialogue with other panelists as well as field questions from the audience. Students will be evaluated individually based upon their ability to: (1) effectively and clearly present their research and arguments; (2) engage in a panel discussion with other panelists; and (3) respond to questions from the audience.  
*Weekly Seminar Discussions*: This class is a communication intensive course, which means there will be regular seminar discussions that address specific theories, thinker and issues that have defined the field of Religious Studies in general, the scientific methods of studying religions in particular. Discussion topics will be provided before class meetings so that students can prepare to share their perspective and thereby raise any critical questions they may have. Participation will be evaluated according to one's ability to dialogue with students and articulate arguments that are relevant to the course readings.  
**Evaluation Breakdown:**  
Two Essays: 20% each  
Research Paper: 35%  
Panel Presentation: 10%  
Weekly Seminar Discussions: 15%

### Additional Academic Integrity Course Policy and Penalty Information:

**Academic Integrity & Plagiarism Policy**:  
Rensselaer Polytechnic Institute's commitment to 'thoroughness' and 'knowledge' includes the values associated with intellectual honesty and humility.
