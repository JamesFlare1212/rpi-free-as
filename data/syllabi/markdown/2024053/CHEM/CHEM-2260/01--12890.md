## Course Goals

### Goals:

**Course Description**

This is the second semester of the Organic Chemistry Lecture series. Students enrolled in this course are required to have successfully completed Organic Chemistry I (or equivalent course) which covered the fundamentals of the subject matter. In Organic Chemistry II, students will learn additional spectroscopic methods (NMR) and how to apply them in coordination with other methods (IR, MS, UV/Vis) to identify molecules. Students will also learn about the chemistry of aromatic systems and carbonyl-based chemical reactions and derivatives. Catalysis, synthetic polymers and concepts of pericyclic reactions are also covered in this course. Toward the end of the course selected topics in the biological applications of organic chemistry will be covered.

## Learning Outcomes

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes (CLOs):

- Students will be able to interpret IR, MS, UV, NMR spectra and determine molecular structures from spectra and other information together. More details from the instructor's knowledge of spectroscopy that are not included in the text will also be covered.
- Students will demonstrate knowledge of the reactions of carboxylic acid derivatives and the mechanisms of those reactions. Students will demonstrate knowledge of the reactions of aldehydes, ketones, carboxylic acid derivatives (either saturated or unsaturated). Students will demonstrate knowledge of the reactivity of carbonyl alpha carbons and be able to demonstrate the use of enol/enolate chemistry in synthesis.
- Students will be able to explain why benzene has different reactivity patterns than non-aromatic olefins and write mechanisms for a variety of known reactions and conditions. Students will be able to predict the reactivity pattern (and necessary conditions) to add or remove functional groups from substituted benzene. Students will demonstrate knowledge of the reactivity and selectivity patterns of aromatic compounds.
- Students will demonstrate knowledge of the characteristics, reactivities and synthesis of amines and heterocycles.
- Students will demonstrate knowledge of catalysis in organic reactions and different types of catalysis.
- Students will demonstrate knowledge of synthetic polymers, including their synthesis, characterization and specialized properties.
- Students will demonstrate knowledge about the nomenclature, structures and reactivities of common carbohydrates.
- Students will demonstrate knowledge of the properties of amino acids, peptides and proteins, selected DNA/RNA topics as well as common methods for their synthesis.
- Over the course of this second semester of Organic Chemistry, students will be able to review and better understand the topics covered in Organic Chemistry I.

**Homework**

Homework will be assigned directly from your textbook post-chapter or tutorial practice problems, or in the form of an LMS assignment. Late assignments will be penalized at 10% per day late. It is well established that students learn much more through active learning than through passive learning. Attending and taking notes in lecture, and reading the textbook are passive learning, while working homework problems is active learning. Therefore, most of your learning will take place while you work homework problems. Some students may find that assigned homework is sufficient for them to master the concepts we study. Others will need more practice before they achieve full mastery. I strongly recommend that you try additional textbook exercises and ACS practice problems (posted on LMS) to supplement your homework assignments since the answers are available to you in the solution manual. It is up to you to assess your own understanding, and to work enough exercises to achieve your desired level of mastery.

**Quizzes**

Each quiz will be semi-cumulative. Approximately half (or more) of the points on each quiz will be for questions involving material that has not yet been quizzed. The other half of the points will be distributed amongst previous topics. The reason for this is to discourage cramming and ensure that you achieve long-term learning of Organic Chemistry. No subject can be retained long-term if you cram the material. Hopefully, this will substantially decrease your final exam studying needs, since you will have been reviewing and reinforcing knowledge all semester.

**Final Exam**

**The final exam will be cumulative and will include both Organic Chemistry I & II**. Attendance at the final exam is mandatory. A grade of 0% will be given for any unexcused absence and make-ups will not be allowed. Absence from the final exam due to travel is always unexcused.

**Semester Exams**

Each exam will be cumulative, with emphasis on material that has not yet appeared on an exam. The distribution of points will be more variable than on quizzes, since a single exam question can be more complicated than a quiz question and incorporate ideas from several chapters.

As with quizzes, this will lead to material covered early in the semester being assessed more often than material late in the semester. In order to provide some compensation for this over-emphasis on early material, the contribution of the later exams to your final grade is larger than that of the first exam.

## Policies

### Attendance Policy:

**Attendance and Make-up Opportunities**

Attendance is required at every lecture. Since it is true that active learning is generally preferable to passive learning, why is attendance important?

- I cannot cover all material completely during the lecture time we have. Therefore, I will naturally focus on and emphasize those aspects that are most crucial. It is not always clear which ideas or details are most important from reading a text, where almost all information is given equally thorough treatment. By coming to class, you will learn which ideas are most important, and then you can manage your study and active learning time accordingly.
- Some students will learn easily from reading the text. Others will not immediately connect with the text and will benefit from hearing an alternative treatment of the same material with different worked examples. Most students will find themselves falling in both of these categories at different times throughout the semester. Coming to class will help you clarify and reinforce the understanding you developed by reading.

**If you are absent for a quiz or semester exam:**

- If your absence is not excused, you will receive a grade of zero.
- The first time you miss a quiz and provide a valid excuse, no make-up will be given, and your other quizzes will hold more weight toward your final grade. In other words, the missed quiz will not be used toward the quiz portion of your final grade. For second and subsequent missed quizzes with valid excuses, a make-up must be scheduled not more than one week after your absence. In such a case, those students should bring an official document that proves one's legitimate excuse of absence to the Dean of Students who requests a recommendation to the instructor. After discussing with the Dean of Students, the instructor will make a decision whether a make-up should be given or not. In case a make-up is given, it must be scheduled not more than one week after your absence.
- Exams missed with a valid excuse for the date of the exam must be made up not more than one week after your absence.
- If your absence is due to a foreseeable circumstance (such as an athletic event) on the day of the quiz or exam, you must receive permission to be absent at least 48 hours in advance.
- If your absence is due to an unforeseeable event (such as illness or family emergency) on the day of quiz/exam, you must provide proof of the event within one week of returning to classes.
- All excuses will be handled by the Dean of Students. Take documentation of the reason of your absence to the Dean’s office, 4600 Academy Hall, not to me. The Dean’s Office will then contact me.

**If you miss a homework assignment:**

Homework will be penalized 10% for each day it late. Since you have many days to complete each assignment, no excuses will be accepted for late homework apart from excuses which would also result in an incomplete grade for the course (e.g. prolonged illness).

## Other Course-Specific Information

### Additional Course Information:

**Succeeding in This Course**

Organic Chemistry is a difficult course covering an enormous breadth of material at blinding speed. Since much material builds upon the preceding material, it is absolutely imperative that you do not fall behind. Disciplined, consistent study habits are absolutely necessary for success in this course.

We will not generally begin a new topic/chapter on any particular day of the week. It may therefore help you to structure study efforts based on progress through a chapter instead of a rigid weekly schedule. An outline of progress through each chapter and a sample study schedule are shown below.

The evening before beginning a new chapter: Skim the textbook. Look at section and subsection headings as well as figures. Read only enough of the text to give yourself a good idea of what major topics and key concepts to listen for the next day. Look up bold words you do not know in the glossary. **Suggested minimum time commitment: 1 hour.**

Lecture introducing a new chapter: Learn the basics and overarching ideas of the new chapter. Later that same day, review your lecture slides/notes, and consult your textbook or plan to attend office hours for answers to any in-class questions. **Suggested minimum time commitment: 2 hours.**

Before the next lecture: Read the chapter thoroughly. Stop frequently to consult the glossary or use the internet to clear up points you don’t understand. (Use the lecture slides to help you focus on the most important concepts, but don’t forget that you are responsible for all material, whether or not it is explicitly covered in lecture!) Then, complete the “easier” post-chapter practice or homework problems in order to gauge your understanding of the overarching ideas and topics of the chapter. **Suggested minimum time commitment: 4 hours.**

During remaining lectures: Deepen your understanding of chapter material. Later the same day, review your lecture slides, and consult your textbook or plan to attend office hours for answers to any in-class questions. Complete more post-chapter practice problems. **Suggested minimum time commitment: 5 hours.**

The suggested minimum time commitments above sum to 12 hours per chapter. Some chapters will demand more of your time some less. Additionally, this is the tentative amount of out-of-class time an **excellent** student will need to earn an **average** grade in the course. If you wish to earn a higher-than-average grade, you will need to spend more time studying and solving problems.

**Additional advice to students who are taking organic chemistry class**:

1. Organic chemistry is not terrible as the rumors say, but it is labor intensive.
2. Read the textbook. It is your best friend for the better part of the year.
3. You have to study a little at a time (as described above); you cannot cram everything in just before a test.
4. Come to class. Go to group help sessions (ALAC) even if they are not mandatory.
5. Work extra problems after each chapter from the textbook with a pencil. Use the solution manual to check your answers. Build models. Recopy your lecture notes. You may realize there are things you do not completely understand.
6. Model sets are a great resource. They are essential to understanding stereochemistry.
7. Working on syntheses ties together everything you have learned.
8. If you know the underlying reasons why certain reactions take place, you can figure out a reaction you have never seen before.
9. Organic chemistry is intense, requires a lot of work and it goes very fast, but it is doable.
