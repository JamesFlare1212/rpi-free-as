## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

This is the first semester of the Organic Chemistry Lecture series. Students enrolled in this course are required to have successfully completed General Chemistry (or equivalent courses) which cover the fundamentals of the subject matter. In Organic Chemistry I, students will learn nomenclature and structure of functional groups, common reaction mechanisms involving organic compounds, and synthesis of organic molecules.

### Goals:

Structure and chemical behavior of organic molecules with particular emphasis on reaction mechanisms as pathways for understanding their reactions. Stereochemistry, synthesis, reactions and mechanisms are among the topics included.

## Learning Outcomes

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes (CLOs):

- Students will be able to explain electronic structure and covalent bonding in organic molecules. Students will be able to explain why certain molecules are more acidic or more basic and how their structures affect acidity strength. Students will demonstrate knowledge of nomenclature and physical properties and how to represent molecules’ structures. (Ch 1-3)
- Students will demonstrate knowledge of arrangement of atoms of organic molecules in three dimensions. Students will demonstrate knowledge of structures, nomenclature, and reactivity of alkenes. Students will demonstrate knowledge of stereochemical outcomes of addition reactions of alkenes. (Ch 4-6)
- Students will demonstrate knowledge of reactions of alkynes and how to synthesize more complex molecules by multistep synthetic routes. Students will demonstrate knowledge of delocalization of electrons and their effect on stability and reactivity. Students will demonstrate knowledge about substitution reactions of alkyl halides. Students will demonstrate knowledge about elimination reactions of alkyl halides. (Ch 7-9)
- Students will demonstrate knowledge of the reactions of alcohols, ethers, epoxides, amines, and thiols. Students will demonstrate knowledge of the oxidation of alcohols. Students will demonstrate knowledge of concepts and reactions of organometallic compounds. Students will demonstrate knowledge of radical reactions of alkanes. (Ch 10-11)

**Recitation Activities**

These activities will be distributed, completed, and collected during the recitation period. Therefore, you will be graded on completion, not correctness. If you complete every question with a relevant, thoughtful answer you will receive full credit. If most students do not have enough time to finish the activity, grades will be adjusted to reflect that. Some of the recitation activities may require the use of a laptop.

**Homework**

Homework will be assigned for each chapter we complete and will be turned in using Achieve. Late assignments will be penalized at 10% per day late. It is well established that students learn much more through active learning than through passive learning. Attending lectures and taking notes in lecture, and reading the textbook are passive learning, while working homework problems is active learning. Therefore, most of your learning will take place while you work on homework problems. Some students may find that the assigned Achieve homework is sufficient for them to master the concepts we study. Others will need more practice before they achieve full mastery. I strongly recommend that you try textbook exercises to supplement the Achieve assignments since the answers are available to you in the solution manual. I will not assign specific problems from the textbook, and they will not be graded. It is up to you to assess your own understanding, and to work enough exercises to achieve your desired level of mastery.

**Quizzes**

Each quiz will be semi-cumulative. Approximately half (or more) of the points on each quiz will be for questions involving material that has not yet been quizzed. The other half of the points will be distributed amongst previous topics. The reason for this is to discourage cramming and ensure that you achieve long-term learning of Organic Chemistry. No subject can be retained long-term if you cram the material. To do well on quizzes, you will therefore need to briefly review as much as a month’s worth of material. Hopefully, this will substantially decrease your final exam studying needs, since you will have been reviewing and reinforcing knowledge all semester.

One consequence of this setup is that early in the semester, there will be no older material to cover, so the new material will account for the entirety of the quiz. Late in the semester, some topics will only be quizzed once or twice because we will run out of time. This means that early chapters will be somewhat over-assessed and later chapters will be somewhat under-assessed.

**Final Exam**

The final exam will be cumulative and will occur sometime between Dec. 14 and Dec. 20 (inclusive). The exact date and time will be set by the Registrar later this semester. Attendance at the final exam is mandatory. A grade of 0% will be given for any unexcused absence and make-ups will not be allowed. Absence from the final exam due to travel is always unexcused.

**Semester Exams**

Each exam will be cumulative, with emphasis on material that has not yet appeared on an exam. The distribution of points will be more variable than on quizzes since a single exam question can be more complicated than a quiz question and incorporate ideas from several chapters.

As with quizzes, this will lead to material being covered early in the semester being assessed more often than material late in the semester. To provide some compensation for this over-emphasis on early material, the contribution of each exam to your final grade increases throughout semester, so that the last exam is worth twice as much as the first.

### Attendance Policy:

Attendance is mandatory at all class meetings.  
**Attendance and Make-up Opportunities**

Attendance is required at every lecture and recitation. Since it is true that active learning is generally preferable to passive learning, why is attendance important?

The instructors cannot cover all material completely during the lecture time we have. Therefore, they will naturally focus on and emphasize those aspects that are most crucial. It is not always clear which ideas or details are most important from reading a text, where almost all information is given equally thorough treatment. By coming to class, you will learn which ideas are most important, and then you can manage your study and active learning time accordingly.

- Some students will learn easily from reading the text. Others will not immediately connect with the text and will benefit from hearing an alternative treatment of the same material with different worked examples. Most students will find themselves falling in both categories at different times throughout the semester. Coming to class will help you clarify and reinforce the understanding you developed by reading.

- If your absence is not excused, you will receive a grade of zero.
- The first time you miss a quiz and provide a valid excuse, no make-up will be given, and your other quizzes will hold more weight toward your final grade. In other words, the missed quiz will not be used toward the quiz portion of your final grade. ## Other Course-Specific Information

### Additional Course Information:

Organic Chemistry is a difficult course covering an enormous breadth of material at blinding speed. In this first semester of Organic Chemistry, we have 15 weeks to cover 12 chapters of the textbook. Due to holidays and breaks, these 15 weeks contain 14 weeks’ worth of class meetings. Our pace through the textbook will be correspondingly rapid. Since much material builds upon the preceding material, it is imperative that you do not fall behind. Disciplined, consistent study habits are necessary for success in this course.

We will not generally begin a new topic/chapter on any day of the week. It may therefore help you to structure study efforts based on progress through a chapter instead of a rigid weekly schedule. An outline of progress through each chapter and a sample study schedule are shown below.

The evening before beginning a new chapter: Skim the textbook. Look at section and subsection headings as well as figures. Read only enough of the text to give yourself a good idea of what major topics and key concepts to listen to for the next day. Look up bold words you do not know in the glossary. **Suggested minimum time commitment: 1 hour.**

Lecture introducing a new chapter: Learn the basics and overarching ideas of the new chapter. Later that same day, review your lecture slides/notes, and consult your textbook or plan to attend office hours for answers to any in-class questions. **Suggested minimum time commitment: 2 hours.**

Before the next lecture: Read the chapter thoroughly. Consult the glossary or use the internet to clear up points you don’t understand. (Use the lecture slides to help you focus on the most important concepts, but don’t forget that you are responsible for all material, whether it is explicitly covered in lecture!) Then, complete the “easier” homework problems to gauge your understanding of the overarching ideas and topics of the chapter. **Suggested minimum time commitment: 5 hours.**

During remaining lectures: Deepen your understanding of chapter material. Later the same day, review your lecture slides, and consult your textbook or plan to attend office hours and/or ALAC tutoring session for answers to any in-class questions. Complete the remaining “easier” homework problems as well as the “harder” homework problems. **Suggested minimum time commitment: 6 hours.**

The suggested minimum time commitments above sum to 14 hours per chapter. Some chapters will demand more of your time, some less. For example, Chapter 9 in the current 8th edition of the textbook combines two chapters from the previous 7th edition and will require twice the time commitment. Additionally, this is the tentative amount of out-of-class time an **excellent** student will need to earn an **average** grade in the course. If you wish to earn a higher-than-average grade, you will need to spend more time studying.

**Additional advice to students who are taking organic chemistry class**:

- Organic chemistry is not terrible as the rumors say, but it is labor intensive.
- Read the textbook. It is your best friend for the better part of the year.
- You must study a little at a time (as described above); you cannot cram everything in just before a test.
- Come to class. Go to group help sessions (ALAC) even if they are not mandatory.
- Work extra problems after each chapter from the textbook with a pencil. Use the solution manual to check your answers. Build models. Recopy your lecture notes. You may realize there are things you do not completely understand.
- Model sets are a great resource. They are essential to understanding stereochemistry.
- Working on syntheses ties together everything you have learned.
- If you know the underlying reasons why certain reactions take place, you can figure out a reaction you have never seen before.
- Organic chemistry is intense, requires a lot of work and it goes very fast, but it is doable.
