## Learning Outcomes

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes (CLOs):

- Understand theoretical basis for biomolecular interactions a. Calculate Kd from G and vice versa. b. Suggest an appropriate ligand titration concentration range for simple binding to a protein given a hypothetical Kd. c. Suggest an appropriate protein (target) concentration for simple binding to a protein given a hypothetical Kd. d. Know under what conditions the Kd can be measured and under what conditions the concentration can be measured. e. Calculate from a free energy diagram the coupling free energy between ligation and dimerization for a system where monomer can bind ligand and dimerize. f. Determine from a folding free energy diagram whether denaturant or stabilizer was used. g. Determine from a folding free energy diagram whether the transition state is late or early. h. Draw an approximate folding free energy diagram corresponding to a given chevron plot of ln vs [denaturant]. i. Discern from the stated concentrations in a kinetics experiment of ligand binding whether it is being carried out in the Pseudo First Order regime. j. Know how to extract k+1 and k-1 from kobs in a pseudo-first order kinetics experiment. k. Explain the steady-state approximation and the limiting cases of a: diffusion limited reaction or b: kobs=k2. l. Calculate how much faster reaction 2 proceeds compared to reaction 1 given their activation energies. m. Calculate at what temperature reaction 2 would be as fast as reaction 1. n. Calculate molecular weight from diffusion time or Diffusion coefficient and o. Calculate the diffusion coefficient or Diffusion time from molecular weight. p. Calculate how much faster (or slower) a protein of MW1 diffuses compared to a protein of MW2. q. Calculate the mean square distance a protein moves from point 0, at time, t given either its molecular weight or diffusion coefficient. r. Calculate the diffusion coefficient given the mean square displacement and time interval.
- Undestand the theory and oractical limits of Optical Spectroscopy to probe Biomolecular interactions A. Optical Spectroscopy CD and IR a. What is circular dichroism? b. Why is the unit of CD an angle, theta? c. What gives rise to a CD signal in proteins? d. Why does it change when the structure changes? e. What is it used for in biophysics? f. Know the origin of and difference between IR active vibrational modes and IR inactive vibrational modes. g. Know the two major physical properties that influence IR absorption frequencies. h. How is IR used in protein chemistry? i. Be able to discuss the advantages and disadvantages of CD and IR as concerns protein folding studies. B. Fluorescence Spectroscopy a. What is fluorescence? b. On what timescale does it occur? c. What are the golden rules of fluorescence? d. What is a fluorescence lifetime and how is it measured? e. What can you learn from it? f. What is solvent relaxation and how is it used to study biomolecules? g. What is anisotropy and how is it used to study biomolecular interactions? h. What is FRET and how is it used to study biomolecular interactions? C. Fluorescence Microscopy a. What is fluctuation microscopy? b. What can be measured and how? c. What is sN&B? d. What is fluorescence lifetime microscopy? e. What can be measured? f. What is a phasor? g. What is FLIM most useful for? Why? h. What is the PSF? i. What is PALM/STORM microscopy? j. How does it work?
- Understand the theory and practical limitations of other analysis technics for biomolecular interactions A. Calorimetry a. The difference between DSC and ITC b. How to choose the protein and ligand concentrations for an ITC experiment. c. Typical problems that occur in ITC experiments. d. What information can be obtained from a DSC experiment on a protein, a DNA oligonucleotide, lipid vesicles? e. How is a sloping Cp(F) baseline interpreted? B. Analytical Ultracentrifugation a. The experimental and theoretical differences between sedimentation velocity and equilibrium sedimentation experiments; what exactly is measured and what does it tell you? b. What is reduced or buoyant mass? How is it calculated? Why is it important? How big is it compared to true molecular weight? c. Sedimentation velocity – know how to recognize the shapes of the sedimentation velocity curves and understand how molecular size and density affect their shapes d. In sedimentation velocity what is a g(s) plot? How is it different from a c(s) plot? e. Also what do you need to know to go from an s-value to a molecular weight? f. Why would s increase as a function of protein concentration? g. Equilibrium Sedimentation – same thing, know how to recognize the shapes in an equilibrium sedimentation curve and how they change with rotor speed, MW, heterogeneous samples or association equilibria. h. What is density gradient sedimentation, how is it done and what is it used for? C. Small Angle X-ray Scattering a. How is the scattering pattern produced? b. What is Q? Why is it called the momentum transfer? How is it related to he scattering angle? The wavelength? c. How small is small in SAXS? Why do we do small angle scattering on proteins instead of wide angle scattering? d. What in the sample scatters x-rays? e. How does the scattering angle depend upon distance between scattering centers? f. What information can you obtain from the intercept in a plot of I(Q) vs Q? g. What is the Guinier approximation? What is it used for? Know how to recognize a Guinier plot and know what makes the slope change and how. h. What is a P(r) plot? What can you learn by visual inspection of a P(r) plot? i. What is a Kratky plot? What can you learn from visual inspection of a Kratky plot? How does it change with protein size and shape? D. Data Analysis a. What is least squares analysis? b. What is a Chisquare value? c. What is global analysis? What are its advantages? d. What are rigorous confidence limits? e. What are residuals? What can they tell you? f. What is a solver in the context of data analysis programs? g. What is the general purpose of Marquardt-Levenberg algorithm in a non-linear least squares data analysis program?

### Additional Academic Integrity Course Policy and Penalty Information:

**Basic principles:**

The basis of the relationship between the teacher and students is trust. The teacher has the obligation to prepare the lectures and work with the students. Students must work on the assignments and learn the material.

Students must trust that teachers have made appropriate decisions about the structure and content of the courses they teach, and teachers must trust that the assignments that students turn in are their own. Acts that violate this trust undermine the educational process.

Students and teachers will act in honesty. The Rensselaer Handbook defines various forms of academic dishonesty and procedures for responding to them. All forms are violations of the trust between students and teachers. Students should familiarize themselves with this portion of the Rensselaer Handbook and should note that the penalties for plagiarism and other forms of cheating can be quite harsh.

-Cheating on an exam. Anyone cheating on an exam will be given a zero for that exam. **Two cheating episodes will result in an F in the course and may be reported for academic discipline.** NO BOOKS, notes, crib sheets or other written materials are allowed during exams. Looking at other student’s work is not allowed.

-Re-grading: DO NOT WRITE ANYTHING ON AN EXAM PAPER returned to you after grading by the Instructor. If an exam paper has new markings, this will disqualify you from any consideration of re-grading. **Anyone submitting an altered exam for re-grading will be given an F in the corresponding exam. Two cheating episodes will result in an F in the course and may be reported for academic discipline.**
