## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

Undergraduate course, 4 credit hours.  
This course covers the design and implementation of computer-based systems to support the collection, organization, and analysis of data and information.  
Topics include theory and techniques for transforming raw data from various sources into structured and usable information; the role of information and data systems in the engineering enterprise; and approaches to interacting with computer-based information systems to support decision making.  
Information systems turn scattered data and records into organized information within easy reach of decision makers.  
The semester follows a single path: raw data, a well-kept database, queries that draw out answers, analysis in code, a model that weighs the options, and an application decision makers can use.  
Each step builds on the previous one, and every step serves a single purpose: supporting more confident decisions.  
The ideas introduced here return in fuller and more formal form in ISYE 2210, 4210, 4500, 4600, and 4960.  
Activities:  
Two weekly meetings combining lecture with hands-on build sessions.  
A posted problem set with full solutions after each lecture.  
A short, individual, handwritten problem set test most Fridays.  
A semester-long team project that ends in a working decision support system, demonstrated live in the final week.

## Course Goals

### Goals:

The course teaches students to carry raw data through every stage of an information system that supports a real decision: modeling the data relationally, building and loading a database, querying it with SQL, analyzing the results in code, applying a transparent decision model, and assembling a simple application for a clearly defined stakeholder.  
Along the way students learn to work in teams on a real dataset, to use generative AI as a working aid while retaining full understanding and ownership of what they build, and to explain their results plainly in writing and in live demonstrations.

## Course Content

### Content Details:

1\. From data to decisions: information systems, data versus information, structure, and missing values (NULL)  
2\. The relational model; keys and integrity constraints; relational algebra  
3\. Entity-relationship modeling and translating diagrams into relations  
4\. Functional dependencies and normalization  
5\. SQL: creating and populating tables; filtering and NULL logic; joins; subqueries and set operations; aggregation and views  
6\. Working with the database from code; analysis with data frames  
7\. Semi-structured data, and relational versus semi-structured representations  
8\. Building a user interface for decision support (Streamlit)  
9\. Decision models: inventory (EOQ and newsvendor), decision trees and what-if analysis, and a stretch menu including light optimization  
10\. Interaction design for decision support; responsible data systems and synthesis  
11\. Team project in four milestones, a live demo, and an individual reflection

## Learning Outcomes

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes (CLOs):

- Design entity-relationship diagrams and well-formed database schemas for real decision problems.
- Build, load, and safely query a relational database using SQL.
- Write queries, build a transparent decision model, and interpret the results.
- Assemble a database-backed application that supports a decision model for a defined stakeholder.
- Explain results plainly, in writing and in team demonstrations, and function effectively on a team.

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes Assessment Measures:

Problem Set Tests (PSTs): fourteen short, individual, handwritten tests taken in the first 10 to 15 minutes of class, most Fridays; closed book, closed notes, no devices.  
Each parallels the posted problem set for that material and asks students to apply the same skills with different numbers and entities.  
Best 12 of 14 count.  
These measure CLOs 1 to 3 unaided.  
Team project: four milestones plus a final demonstration.  
M1 Define the Problem (dataset, stakeholder, recurring decision, feasibility case.  
ends with instructor approval).  
M2 Prepare the Information (cleaning decisions justified, relational design loaded, data dictionary, SQL checks).  
M3 Support the Decision (analysis notebook of measures and comparisons.  
a transparent decision model with assumptions stated).  
M4 Build the User Experience (working application with summaries, exploration, evidence-backed recommendations, limitations.  
usability writeup).  
Final demo (live demonstration, walk-through questions, signed AI-use attestation and log).  
These measure CLOs 1 to 5.  
Individual accountability within the team: peer evaluations at M2 and the final, walk-through questions at the demo where any member may be asked to explain any part, a signed AI-use attestation with every milestone, and an individual reflection due Friday, December 11.  
Posted problem sets with full solutions after every lecture serve as ungraded practice and self-checking.

## Grading Criteria

### Criteria Details:

Weights  
\- Problem Set Tests (best 12 of 14): 60%  
\- Team Project: 40%  
Team project points (40 total, equal to the 40% weight): M1 Define the Problem 4; M2 Prepare the Information 6; M3 Support the Decision 6; M4 Build the User Experience 8; Final demo 12; Peer evaluation adjustment 4.  
Letter scale (percentage cutoffs are the scale for this course; letters and quality points follow RPI's 4.0 scale, and RPI has no A+):  
A 93% and above; A- 90 to 92.99%; B+ 88 to 89.99%; B 84 to 87.99%; B- 80 to 83.99%; C+ 78 to 79.99%; C 73 to 77.99%; C- 70 to 72.99%; D+ 67 to 69.99%; D 60 to 66.99%; F below 60%.  
Dropped scores: fourteen problem set tests run this term and the best 12 count.  
The two drops are the student's to use as they choose: skip up to two outright, or take all fourteen and let the best twelve stand.  
An illness, an interview trip, or an official varsity competition simply becomes a dropped score (up to twice).  
A natural consequence of this flexibility is that no makeup problem set tests are administered.  
For full credit on a test, all work must be shown; a correct answer with no work is not full credit.  
Appeals: if a student believes an item was graded in error, they should raise it with the instructor within one week of its return.  
Progress feedback: graded problem set tests come back the class after they are taken, so standing is visible every week from the first Friday of the term.  
Milestone 1 (due September 18) ends with written instructor approval or feedback, and Milestone 2 (due October 23) returns feedback and the first peer evaluation, so every student has both test and project assessment before mid-semester.

## Policies

### Attendance Policy:

Students are responsible for attending class and for all class material: slides, discussions, and announcements.  
Plan to arrive on time and stay through the end of the period; late arrivals and early exits disrupt the class, so when one is unavoidable, please tell the instructor before the meeting and take a seat near the door.  
On test days the test begins shortly after the start of the period; arriving late does not extend the time, and a missed test simply becomes one of the two dropped scores.  
Attendance is not separately graded; its consequences run through the tests and the team project.  
Foreseeable conflicts such as athletic travel or a job interview should be raised as soon as possible, preferably at least a week in advance.

### Other Course Policies:

Missed tests and assignments. No makeup problem set tests are administered; the best 12 of 14 scores count, and up to two missed tests (illness, interview travel, official varsity competition, or by choice) become dropped scores. Project milestones are team deliverables due Fridays at 11:59 PM in the team's GitHub repository; the version committed by the deadline is the version graded.  
Participation. Participation is not separately graded. Classes are interactive and many days require a laptop; the classroom is the least expensive place to make mistakes, so students are encouraged to speak up.  
Extra credit. Each problem set test carries a small optional bonus question worth up to one point beyond the test total. There is no other extra credit.  
Use of AI and generative tools. AI use is permitted under a three-tier policy. Encouraged, no disclosure needed: help with syntax, debugging, studying, and understanding error messages and starter code. Allowed, with disclosure: drafting or refactoring project code, boilerplate, and project queries; each team keeps a short log per milestone of what it asked, used, and changed. Disallowed: any AI use on a problem set test (which is why they are handwritten and device-free), inventing entries in the AI-use log, or presenting work no member of the team can explain. Any use carries full student responsibility for the submitted work.  
Understanding and responsibility. Students are responsible for everything their team submits: a signed AI-use attestation accompanies each milestone; at the final demonstration any member may be asked to walk through any part of the submission; and the instructor may follow up individually with any team member, with anything a member cannot explain treated as not submitted for that member.  
Religious observance. If a class meeting falls on a religious observance, please let the instructor know so arrangements can be made.  
Incomplete grades and withdrawal follow Institute policy and deadlines.  
Changes to the syllabus. A course is a work in progress; the instructor reserves the right to make changes to the course and schedule and will notify students as soon as possible when changes arise.
