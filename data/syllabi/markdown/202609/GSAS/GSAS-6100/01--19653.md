## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

As a foundational course in the practice of critical game design, this course introduces students to major theorists in the field of game studies and topics including theories of play, space, narratology, ludology, identity, representation, culture and society. Students make integrative connections between theory and practice with a semester-long iterative design project, including critical writing, paper prototyping and peer feedback. Students explore game studies concepts both in a traditional research paper format, and in design practice.

## Course Goals

### Goals:

- Cultivate a vocabulary for analyzing games through major theoretical lenses in game studies, including play, narrative, procedurality, and abstraction.

- Gain the experience of moving between theory and practice, using critical frameworks to inform design work and using design work to test the frameworks in turn.

- Explore the relationship between play, games, and players, recognizing how each shapes and is shaped by the others.

- Enhance writing and design-reflection skills by articulating theoretical arguments in both traditional research and design documentation formats.

## Learning Outcomes

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes (CLOs):

- Students will be able to analyze games using established theoretical frameworks.
- Students will be able to design and iterate on an original game concept through prototyping and incorporating feedback at each stage.
- Students will be able to translate a design concept across media, evaluating what is gained, lost, or transformed in the process.
- Students will be able to document and reflect on their own design process.

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes Assessment Measures:

ASSIGNMENTS

Short Essay

Due: 09/16

Find and play two games: one where the goal is the point of playing (e.g., the reward or win-state is what the player is playing for), and one where the goal isn’t clear, or any goal feels subordinate to the experience of playing.

Write a short informal paper (e.g., 400-500 words) comparing the two games, arguing whether having a goal is necessary for an activity to count as "play." Use Huizinga, Caillois, Sutton-Smith, or other game theorists/philosophers of play to support your argument. You don't need to follow strict academic formatting, but you must include a reference list at the end.

Note: if you can't find two games, you may pick one single game that is sometimes played for the reward and sometimes played for its own sake, and compare those two modes of play.

Prototype (3 Phases)

Phase 1 - Ideation Notes

Due: 09/23

In class, you will brainstorm multiple sets of keywords. Choose the set you are most drawn to as the basis for a game. Write a short paper explaining which keywords you picked and what game/experimental work/artwork you plan to build from them. Specify your aspiration, theme, genre, medium, and a potential game loop and mechanics, plus anything else you would like to include. All of this can, and likely will, change as development continues.

Phase 2 - Paper Prototype

Due: 10/07

Regardless of your intended medium, make a playable paper prototype of your game idea. Write a short reflection on your design process: which ideas from your Ideation Notes made it into the prototype, which didn’t, and what you plan to implement, cut, or revise moving forward.

Phase 3 - Remediation Prototype

Due: 11/04

Transfer your paper prototype into the medium you intended for the finished project. As before, write a similar reflection: what design elements carried over from the Paper Prototype, what didn’t, and what you intend to make for the Final Project.

Final Project

Due: 12/09

Polish and complete the project you have developed across previous phases. Write a paper thoroughly documenting your entire development process, from ideation through prototyping, iteration, and final execution. A template will be provided for this documentation. Present both your finished project and your paper during the last class.

### Additional Academic Integrity Course Policy and Penalty Information:

Plagiarism

Plagiarism is defined as representing the work or words of another as one's own through the omission of acknowledgement or reference. Examples include using sentences verbatim from a published source in a term paper without appropriate referencing, or presenting as one's own the detailed argument of a published source, or presenting as one's own electronically or digitally enhanced graphic representations from any form of media.
