## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

This course covers modeling, viewing, and rendering techniques in 3D computer graphics systems, with emphasis on modern shader pipeline programming in OpenGL using C++. Topics include mathematical foundations and matrix transformations relevant to 3D graphics; clipping and projection; hidden surface removal; managing 3D graphics data structures; hierarchical systems; rasterization; culling; 2D and 3D texture mapping; methods for building and using 3D models; lighting and material models and implementation; shadows, shadow mapping, and generating soft shadows; skyboxes, skydomes, and environment mapping; normal, bump, and height mapping; parametric surface representation; introduction to animation; tessellation and geometry shaders; compositing; terrain generation; atmospheric effects such as fog and clouds; generating and applying 3D noise; performance issues such as minimizing dynamic allocation and controlling level of detail; special effects.

We will be also covering “classic” graphics algorithms such as Bresenham’s line drawing and Bezier curves

## Course Goals

### Goals:

After successfully completing this course the student will be able to:

- Demonstrate proficiency in programming with the OpenGL API using the C++ programming language.
- Create Shader programs using OpenGL’s shader programming language to shade fragments, vertices, and geometry.
- Discuss the general application of computer graphics in the development of Video Games based on an accumulated knowledge of the history of the field.
- Apply their knowledge of Computer Graphics programming in the field of Game Development or other relevant fields.

## Course Content

### Content Details:

week 1:course overview, introduction to OpenGL, GLM, libraries

week 2:OpenGL pipeline, GLSL, error handling, animation

week 3:mathematical foundations, matrix transformations

week 4: buffers, vertex attributes, uniforms, instancing, matrix stacks

week 5: face culling, texture mapping

week 6: texture mapping (continued), anti-aliasing

week 7: procedural and external models, indexing

week 8:lighting and materials

midterm project due

week 9:shadow mapping, percentage closer filtering

week 10: cube maps, skydomes, environment mapping

week 11: bump, normal, and height mapping

week 12: parametric surfaces, tessellation

Near-final Exam

week 13: geometry shaders

week 14: 3D textures, noise maps and applications of noise, clouds

week 15: fog, compositing and transparency, user-defined clipping

final project due

Note that this does not break out lectures even though there are obviously 2 per week. Depending on the extent of the material for the week additional material will be included as general topics and background as well as inspiration for projects, including:

- History of Computer Graphics in Games
- “Classic” Graphical Algorithms (Drawing lines, flood fill, etc.)
- Color Science
- Quaternions and other advanced math used in Computer Graphics
- Cameras and Viewpoints
- Emerging technologies: Vulkan and Metal
- Lines, Splines and Curves
- Shader “Graphs” in Game Engines and other applications

## Grading Criteria

### Criteria Details:

Half of your grade will be based on project work as follows:

20% midterm project, demonstrate “common” openGL techniques

30% final project, demonstrate “advanced” open GL techniques in a novel application (1/6 of the project grade will be assigned based on a proposal presentation)

Final projects can be done in small groups (no more than 3) that should demonstrate more advanced features and it should be made clear what each student's contributions are to the project. Projects will be presented in class.

Half of your grade will be based on non-project work:

35% - up to 7 homeworks (5% each) offered on a near weekly basis

\- general topics, math, rendering, openGL basics, etc.

25% - a ¾ “final” exam approximately 3 weeks before the end of the semester(under consideration, 1-2 short essays on either a prominent figure in computer graphics, and/or a novel use of graphics in a video game, replacing up to 80% of the final grade (20% overall))

By submitting enough homeworks and optional papers, it is possible to “earn out” of the course exam.

Additional extra credit “power-ups” will also be offered throughout the semester. Those may contribute to projects or homeworks as indicated.

## Policies

### Other Course Policies:

Submitting Programming Assignments:

All programming projects submitted must include the following items:

- all C++ source (.cpp) files
- all C++ executable (.exe) files
- all shader source (GLSL) files
- all necessary supporting files such as models, textures, cube maps, normal/height maps
- a “readme” file describing your program, including the items listed below

It is not necessary to include the GLM, GLEW, GLFW, or SOIL2 libraries, as your instructor will be testing your program on a workstation in which they are installed.

Your “readme” file is an integral part of your submission, and must include at least:

- a screen capture of your running program
- a description of where in your output each of the requirements can be found
- a list of required items that you were not able to complete
- clear instructions on how to run and interact with your program

citation and copyright permission information for all elements used by your program  
extra credit opportunities will be offered via homeworks and "added challenges" to the project assignments

### Additional Academic Integrity Course Policy and Penalty Information:

Appropriate Programming Practices

Programming is rarely a solitary endeavor, but for the sake of academic integrity in this course, it is essential that you do your own work. There is a reasonable line between asking people for help and outright plagiarism. While you may ask your classmates for help or advice and show them problematic code, you may NOT view their working code in response. The reverse is true if you are asked for help; while you may offer advice and look at broken code, you may NOT share your code in response. Don’t try to guilt-trip people and don’t allow yourself to be guilt-tripped: friends don’t manipulate each other.

The following are examples of acceptable behavior:

- Whiteboarding solutions with others using diagrams or pseudocode
- Using the web or other external resources for additional instruction, references, or solutions to technical difficulties
- Incorporating snippets of code (up to about 2 lines) that you find online into your own code, provided those snippets are not themselves solutions to assigned problems and that you cite their origins
- Running someone else’s code on your computer to see how their game works

The following are examples of UNACCEPTABLE behavior:

- Asking to see a classmate’s solution to a problem before submitting your own
- Showing a classmate your solution to a problem before they submit their own
- Whiteboarding solutions with others using actual code
- Searching for outright solutions to projects or problems online or elsewhere
- Splitting a problem’s workload with another individual and combining your work
- Submitting the same or similar work as that for another class without permission

violations may result in a penalty to the assignment up to receiving a 0 for the assignment/ project
