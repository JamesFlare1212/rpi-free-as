## Learning Outcomes

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes (CLOs):

- Explain how science, technology, institutions, values, material conditions, and power shape one another, with clear examples.
- Use STS concepts and approaches to investigate a sociotechnical case instead of treating the scientific or technical part as self-contained.
- Decide what evidence, context, and perspectives matter for a question, and map out what remains unknown or outside your analysis.
- Build, communicate, and revise evidence-based arguments in writing, speaking, visual, and digital forms.
- Contribute to shared inquiry through listening, questions, feedback, collaboration, project work, and help-seeking.
- Investigate a possible change by studying current conditions, learning from real-world evidence, and testing or examining a small, ethical next move.

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes Assessment Measures:

# GRADING

Across major assignments, strong work uses relevant evidence, connects social and technical conditions, changes or sharpens claims when the evidence calls for it, and communicates clearly about limits. Each assignment guide has its own rubric building off these general criteria.

## 10% — READING READINESS + SKILLS PRACTICE

We use a small number of short Reading/Skills Checks so you do not have to submit a response to every reading. Any lecture with required preparation may include one; not every check date will be announced in advance.

Checks focus on useful reading skills rather than trivia: finding an argument, recognizing evidence, explaining a distinction, or naming a real question. They cover only required preparation, never BONUS / GO DEEPER material. Your lowest or one missed graded check is automatically dropped.

## 25% PORTFOLIO + 10% CONTRIBUTION

Save useful evidence of your work as the semester goes on. At three checkpoints, you will choose pieces that show what you are learning and how you contributed. You do not need to keep a journal to track and reflect on your contributions, lessons learned, insights generated, questions you have, etc. but it is recommended.

The portfolio answers: What can you now notice, investigate, judge, communicate, or do that you could not do as well before?

Useful evidence might include notes, maps, drafts, feedback, small activities, Encounter Traces, or Impossible Design work.

Contribution asks: How did you help shared inquiry or work happen? Participation can take many forms: speaking, listening, asking good questions, peer feedback, documentation, research, technical or logistical work, coordination, or helping a group revise. It is not a speaking-count or popularity grade.

- Checkpoint 1 — Sun. Oct. 4: Portfolio 7% + Contribution 3%
- Checkpoint 2 — Sun. Nov. 1: Portfolio 8% + Contribution 3%
- Checkpoint 3 — Sun. Dec. 6: Portfolio 10% + Contribution 4%.

The automatic 48-hour grace window applies.

See Learning Portfolio + Contribution — Student Guide for the full instructions.

## ASSIGNMENT 1 — 10%

How Is This Scientific or Technical World Made?

Individual field memo • 900–1,100 words • due Sun. Sept. 27, 11:59 p.m.

Choose a specific scientific or technical world, practice, infrastructure, or arrangement and explain how it is made. Use firsthand encounter evidence plus other relevant evidence to trace the people, labor, technologies, materials, rules, institutions, histories, values, categories, or uneven effects that matter. You will get feedback and revise. A1 does not have to be about your later Impossible Design project.

The automatic 48-hour grace window applies.

See Assignment 1 — How Is This Scientific or Technical World Made? for the full guide.

## ASSIGNMENT 2 — 15%

How Does It Hold—and Where Might It Move?

Individual analytical memo • about 1,200 words • due Sun. Oct. 25, 11:59 p.m.

By this point, you will normally connect A2 to your Impossible Design project. Choose one or two conditions helping reproduce the current arrangement, support your account with traceable evidence, examine what one plausible response changes and leaves intact, and identify what still needs to be learned before an intervention or innovation could be effective. You will get feedback and revise.

The automatic 48-hour grace window applies.

See Assignment 2 — How Does It Hold—and Where Might It Move? for the full guide.

## IMPOSSIBLE DESIGN — 30%

Impossible Design is our semester-long final project. You will begin with an Impossible Goal: a change you genuinely want to make more possible at RPI, in a technology, institution, profession, community, everyday practice, or another world you care about. Then you will investigate what is making that goal hard under current conditions, gather evidence, learn from course ideas and real-world encounters, and revise your strategy for change.

Most students will work in a small project team within their recitation; a structured individual-project route is also available. The Course FAQ explains the difference between Course Crews and project teams.

The 30% project grade is:

- 6% — Project process: evidence of the inquiry and decisions made across the semester.
- 6% — Team final project: a public-facing way to share what your team learned.
- 18% — Individual Final Analysis: your own approximately 1,800-word argument about what is making the goal hard, what evidence changed your thinking, and what could make the goal more possible now.

Major dates:

- Sept. 15–25 — explore possibilities and form provisional project teams.
- Fri. Dec. 4 — bring a near-final team project for audience feedback and make at least one meaningful revision.
- Before Tue. Dec. 8 lecture — final team project due and ready for the live class sharing session. Because everyone needs the project for that shared session, this deadline is NOT eligible for the automatic 48-hour grace window.
- Before Tue. Dec. 8 lecture — default deadline for the Individual Final Analysis. The automatic 48-hour grace window applies.
- Dec. 8–11 — sharing, synthesis, appreciation, and course close. No new major work is assigned after the Tuesday sharing session.

You are not graded on solving a huge problem in one semester. A strong project may narrow the goal, change scale, identify a smarter first move, or show that a different actor or strategy is needed. See Impossible Design — Student Project Guide for the full project map.

# COMMUNICATION INTENSIVE WORK

This course is designed to meet RPI’s Communication Intensive requirement.

You will practice writing, speaking, listening, feedback, revision, and communication for different audiences throughout the semester.

Your three largest individual pieces total about 4,000 words: about 1,000 for Assignment 1, 1,200 for Assignment 2, and 1,800 for the Impossible Design Individual Final Analysis. Assignments 1 and 2 include feedback and revision; the team final project adds practice communicating to an audience.

# ATTENDANCE

Attendance is required in lecture and recitation because much of the course learning happens through investigation, discussion, peer feedback, and in-room practice that cannot be fully reproduced by a worksheet.

Built-in allowance: two lecture absences and one recitation absence. You do not need documentation or an argument that the reason is “valid” for absences within that allowance.

After the allowance: Each additional lecture absence reduces the final course grade by one percentage point and each additional recitation absence reduces the final course grade by two percentage points.

Formal accommodations, Institute-approved adjustments, and prolonged emergencies use the appropriate institutional route and are not treated as ordinary absences.

Late arrival / early departure: occasional brief lateness or leaving a little early does not by itself count as an absence. If lateness patterns become repeated, your TA will talk with you before any further action.

If you know you will miss class, contact your assigned TA before the meeting whenever reasonably possible. If the absence is unexpected, contact your TA as soon as reasonably possible afterward. A brief explanation is helpful for communication and coordination; you do not need to share sensitive or unnecessary personal details.

After an absence, work with your TA to reconnect. Review the LMS materials for that meeting and use any summary notes the teaching team and/or classmates have built along the way. If you are unsure what matters most or what to do next, ask your TA.

Communicating with your TA does not automatically erase an absence; it helps you stay connected and helps the teaching team understand what is happening.

Missing class does not automatically lower an assignment grade. But some assignments ask you to show in-class feedback, collaboration, or project work, so an absence can leave you with less evidence for that part of the assignment.

# DEADLINES, LATE WORK, + THE 48-HOUR GRACE WINDOW

Deadlines do different jobs. Some exist because your classmates need your preparation in a live session; others are individual take-home final products.

Live preparation: If a draft, response, evidence artifact, or other preparation is due because you will use it with classmates, the posted deadline matters. Late work may still be useful, but it cannot recreate peer feedback, discussion, or collaboration that depended on arriving prepared.

Automatic 48-hour grace: Eligible individual take-home final products get an automatic 48-hour grace window with no permission or explanation needed. Work submitted during the window can earn full credit. This applies to:

- Assignment 1
- Assignment 2
- all three Portfolio + Contribution checkpoints
- the Impossible Design Individual Final Analysis

The final team Impossible Design project is the important exception: it must be ready for the shared Dec. 8 session, so the automatic grace window does not apply to that team deadline.

If you need more than 48 hours on an eligible individual assignment, contact your assigned TA as soon as you can and propose a realistic new submission date. You do not need to share unnecessary private details.

If an eligible individual graded assignment is more than 48 hours late and we have not heard from you, a one-time 10% late deduction applies. It does not grow each day. You should still submit the work and reconnect.

Reading/Skills Checks use the built-in dropped score rather than ordinary individualized makeups.

Team work follows the shared project timeline; individual disruptions are handled through communication and role adjustment rather than automatically moving the whole group deadline.

### Academic Policies:

- ### Academic Integrity

  Student-teacher relationships are built on trust. For example, students must trust that teachers have made appropriate decisions about the structure and content of the courses they teach, and teachers must trust that the assignments that students turn in are their own. Acts that violate this trust undermine the educational process. The Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities and The Graduate Student Supplement define various forms of Academic Dishonesty and you should make yourself familiar with these. In this class, all assignments that are turned in for a grade must represent the student’s own work. In cases where help was received, or teamwork was allowed, a notation on the assignment should indicate your collaboration.

  Violations of academic integrity may also be reported to the appropriate Dean (Dean of Students for undergraduate students or the Dean of Graduate Education for graduate students, respectively).

  If you have any question concerning this policy before submitting an assignment, please ask for clarification. In addition, you can visit the following site for more information on our Academic Integrity Policy: [Students Rights, Responsibilities, and Judicial Affairs](https://info.rpi.edu/dean-students/student-rights-responsibilities-and-judicial-affairs).

### Additional Academic Integrity Course Policy and Penalty Information:

Your work should make your own inquiry and judgment visible, give credit where credit is due, and follow the collaboration and AI boundaries stated for each assignment.
