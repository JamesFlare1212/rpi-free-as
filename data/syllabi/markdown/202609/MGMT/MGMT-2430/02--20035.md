## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

Marketing is a dynamic and interdisciplinary field that blends analytical rigor with creativity to shape

customer behavior and drive business success. This course introduces students to the fundamental principles

of marketing while emphasizing their practical application through lecture-based instruction, exercises, and a

semester-long marketing plan project. Students will learn how to critically analyze marketing problems,

develop strategies, and build a comprehensive marketing plan in stages.

The course balances foundational principles with an exploration of marketing’s impact on all stakeholders —

customers, firms, policymakers, society, and the environment. Students will develop a strong grasp of core

marketing concepts such as the marketing mix (Product, Price, Place, Promotion) and segmentation,

targeting, and positioning (STP), while also learning to apply strategic frameworks including SWOT, Porter’s

Five Forces, the BCG and Ansoff matrices, and perceptual mapping. Consumer behavior topics will be paired

with insights from consumer psychology and customer relationship management (CRM), and the marketing

mix will be examined from branding and packaging through pricing approaches, channel design, integrated

marketing communications, and digital marketing.

By integrating analytical tools with ethical, cultural, and environmental perspectives, this course equips

students to approach marketing as both a strategic discipline for business success and a force that shapes

society.

## Learning Outcomes

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes (CLOs):

- Demonstrate functional knowledge of marketing concepts, principles, and practices, including the 4Ps (Product, Price, Place, Promotion), segmentation-targeting-positioning (STP), consumer behavior, branding, and customer relationship management (CRM).
- Analyze and evaluate the marketing environment using strategic frameworks such as SWOT, Porter’s Five Forces, and perceptual mapping.
- Develop and present practical marketing solutions through case analyses and marketing plan projects, demonstrating clear communication in both written and oral forms.
- Apply marketing mix decisions—product, pricing, distribution, and integrated promotion—by using appropriate models and analytical tools to support decision-making.
- Critically assess consumer behavior, including decision-making processes, Maslow’s hierarchy of needs, and the role of perception, involvement, and brand loyalty.
- Deliver professional-quality oral presentations supported by structured arguments, data, and clear visualizations.
- Demonstrate an understanding of the marketing research process and the role of data in guiding marketing strategies.
- Evaluate the ethical, societal, and environmental implications of marketing decisions, including issues of diversity, sustainability, and social responsibility.

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes Assessment Measures:

I. Exams (45% total)

There will be three non-cumulative exams, worth 15% each. The course is organized into three teaching

blocks, and each exam covers exactly one block. No new material is introduced between the end of a block

and its exam, so nothing on the syllabus sits untested going into an exam.

The exams will be based on slides and any other material discussed during the class (uploaded on LMS).

Chapters in the book are for guidance, but anything beyond what is discussed in the class will not be a part of

the exam.

Exams are closed-book and closed-notes, written in class, with no electronic devices of any kind permitted.

There are no make-up exams without written documentation of a university-approved conflict.

II. Marketing Plan Project (35% total)

In teams of four to five, you will develop a complete marketing plan for a product, service, or venture of your

choosing. The plan is submitted in three graded parts. Each part is due roughly two weeks after the block it

draws on has been taught, so you write each section while the concepts are fresh and receive feedback

before the next part is due.• Part 1 — Situation Analysis (7%). Product idea and value proposition; environmental and competitive

analysis using at least two strategic frameworks; customer insight and buying behavior, including a

proposed research approach. Draws on Chapters 1–6.

• Part 2 — Strategy (12%). Segmentation scheme; target segment selection and a positioning statement

supported by a perceptual map; product and brand strategy including life-cycle stage; pricing objective

and strategy. Draws on Chapters 7–11.

• Part 3 — Go-to-Market (16%). Channel and distribution strategy; integrated marketing communications

plan spanning advertising, PR, digital, and sales promotion; budget, timeline, and success metrics; and

an executive summary covering the assembled plan.

The parts are deliberately weighted so that the first counts least. Part 1 exists so that you learn the standard

against which the plan is judged while the cost of learning it is low. Part 3 carries the most weight.

A detailed component-by-component rubric will be posted on LMS. Grades reflect both team performance

and individual contribution.

III. Marketing Plan Presentation (5%)

Each team delivers a professional pitch of its marketing plan in the final two weeks, followed by questions

from the class and the instructor. Evaluation is based on the clarity and structure of the argument, the quality

of the supporting evidence, the visual design of the slides, the handling of questions, and time management.

The entire class is expected to participate in the Q&A that follows each pitch. A sign-up sheet for

presentation slots is posted in November; teams with members who have regular Friday commitments

should request a Tuesday slot.

IV. Individual Ethics Assignment (5%)

Chapter 20 is the final topic of the course. Following that session you will write an individual analysis of

approximately three pages examining the ethical and sustainability implications of your own team’s

marketing plan. You will identify the stakeholders your plan affects, apply the sustainable marketing

frameworks from Chapter 20, name the decisions in your plan that are ethically contestable, and say what

you would change and why. Reaching a different conclusion from your teammates is entirely acceptable —

this is an individual submission and is graded as one.

V. Class Participation and Attendance (10%)

Participation includes attendance, punctuality, and active engagement in discussion and in-class exercises.

Attendance is mandatory. More than two unexcused absences will begin to cost participation points. Excused

absences — official university activities, documented medical reasons — do not count toward that limit, but

you remain responsible for the material. Students more than 10 minutes late may be marked absent. If the

instructor is more than 10 minutes late, everyone is marked present.

In line with Institute policy, all exams and assignments will be returned with feedback within 10 business days

of submission, so that you can use that feedback on the next piece of work.

### Other Course Policies:

**No Phones, No Earphones**

Phones, and earphones/headphones are not to be used at any point during class. Phones should be put away, not face-down on the desk.

This is a deliberate design choice, not an inconvenience. Every slide deck, handout, and worked example used in class is posted to LMS, so there is nothing you need a device to capture.

Exceptions: If asked for an activity, or there are accommodations.

If you have a genuine reason to need to be reachable during a class, like a family situation, an on-call obligation, come and tell me. I will not ask for details, and we will work something out. Otherwise, repeated device use will be treated as non-participation for that session.

**Use of Generative AI**

Generative AI tools may be used for background research, brainstorming, and improving the clarity of your own prose. They may not be used to generate the substance of your analysis. Any use of a generative AI tool in a submitted assignment must be disclosed in a short note at the end of the document stating which tool was used and for what.

Undisclosed use, or submission of AI-generated analysis as your own, is a violation of the academic integrity policy below. Note also that these tools routinely fabricate market data, case figures, and citations. You are responsible for the accuracy of everything you submit, and in the case submissions in particular, invented numbers are easy to detect against the source.

The three exams are written by hand in the room, with no devices. They exist in part so that a meaningful share of your grade reflects what you can do unaided.

**Late Submissions**

Any submission turned in up to 48 hours after the deadline are graded with an automatic 30% deduction. Outside of grave, extenuating circumstances, work is not accepted beyond 48 hours after the deadline. In-class work cannot be made up.

**ACADEMIC INTEGRITY**

Student-teacher relationships are built on trust. Students must trust that teachers have made appropriate decisions about the structure and content of the courses they teach, and teachers must trust that the assignments students turn in are their own. Acts that violate this trust undermine the educational process. The Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities defines the various forms of Academic Dishonesty and you should make yourself familiar with these. In this class, all assignments turned in for a grade must represent the student’s own work. In cases where help was received, or teamwork was allowed, a notation on the assignment should indicate your collaboration. Any and every offense will be reported to the Associate Dean for Academic Affairs. If you have any question concerning this policy before submitting an assignment, please ask for clarification.
