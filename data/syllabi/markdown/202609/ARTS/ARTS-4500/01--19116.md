## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

Many of the musics we encounter today are palimpsests—collages and assemblages pieced together from pre-existing musical texts. In this seminar, we explore how sampling, remix, audio collage and other forms of audio repurposing shape the production, reception, and understanding of music in the 20th and 21st centuries. Through readings, discussion, hands-on creative work, and analysis papers, students interrogate how various musical practices generate polyvocal dialogues and new systems of meaning. We examine big questions of technology, authorship, originality, aesthetic unity, intellectual property, and capitalism. How do musicians and listeners act as curators, manipulators, and interpreters of sound? What responsibilities accompany creative reuse? Drawing on interdisciplinary perspectives and case studies that challenge disciplinary boundaries, the course invites critical listening, collaborative experimentation, and reflective analysis. No prior musical training is required—just curiosity about how digital culture remixes the world around us.[1]

[1] N.B. – This course description has been adapted from one created by Kate Galloway.

## Learning Outcomes

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes (CLOs):

- Students will become well-versed in the historical context, terminology, and popular/theoretical discourses surrounding the digital musical practices of sampling, remix, and audio collage.
- Students will develop the technical skills necessary to make rudimentary audio collages, mashups, and/or sample-based creative works.
- Students will develop and apply the critical thinking skills necessary to situate musical texts and creative practices in a broader cultural and material framework.
- Students will develop the collaborative and communication skills necessary to discuss difficult and thorny ideas in class and to present their work to their peers.

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes Assessment Measures:

**Class Participation:** This course depends on engaged and active participation in all class discussions.

**Reading/Listening Responses**: Each unit will begin with a set of readings and listenings that will set the stage for our class discussions, exercises, and projects. These responses should consist of some combination of summary, analysis, and critical engagement with the texts provided.

**Analysis/Creative Exercises**: Each unit, students will be asked to apply the unit’s concepts and techniques through short exercises that will then be discussed in class. For some units, there will only be one exercise prompt provided (either analysis or creative). For others, students will be able to choose between an analysis exercise or a creative exercise.

**Analysis/Creative Projects**: Each unit ends with a medium-length project which will substantially engage with the relevant concepts and techniques. As with the exercises, for some units there will be only one project prompt provided while for others students will be able to choose between analysis and creative prompts.

**Final Project and Presentation:** At the end of the semester, students will propose, workshop, and present a final project based on a topic of their choosing. This final project can be an in-depth analysis paper, a substantial creative work, or something else altogether. Make note that the final project proposal is due before class time on Friday, November 20th. In the final two class meetings, students will present their work to their peers.

## Policies

### Attendance Policy:

**Attendance:** Students are expected to attend all class meetings. Over the course of a semester, many unexpected conflicts and distractions can arise. If a student must miss a class meeting for any reason, they should provide the instructor with advance notice and arrange for an opportunity to catch up on missed work and material. Students may miss two classes without any penalty. Beyond that, each unexcused absence will incur a penalty of 2.5% to the final course grade.

### Other Course Policies:

**Email:** During the workweek, I will answer all course-related email within 24 hours of receipt.

**Late Work:** This course moves quickly through material and each unit’s work builds upon previously introduced topics. In order to stay on top of the course topics, it is essential that students do their work and turn it in on time. Late work can be turned in up to two weeks after it is due with a penalty of 2% per day it is late. If a student is aware of extenuating circumstances that will require them to miss an assignment’s due date, they should communicate in advance to avoid the late penalty.

**Devices:** Electronic devices, including phones, laptops, tablets, and smart watches, are ubiquitous in contemporary society. In furtherance of the learning goals of this course and in tandem with a robust participation in classroom activities, their use is encouraged. However, if at any point a student’s use of electronic devices becomes counterproductive to their learning or distracting to their fellow students, they will be asked to discontinue use and could face penalties to their participation grade.

**Accessibility Statement:** Students with diverse learning styles and needs are welcome in this course. In particular, if you have a physical, psychological, medical, or learning disability or health consideration that may impact your coursework and/or require accommodations, please feel free to approach me and/or the Disability Services for Students (dss@rpi.edu). DSS will work with you to determine what accommodations are necessary and appropriate. All information and documentation are confidential.

**Generative AI (LLM) Use:** Generative AI provides a shortcut to an end product without any *process*. Learning to analyze and engage critically with ideas and texts is all about process: the process of excavating hidden meanings, the process of identifying connections between texts, the process of situating ideas amidst material conditions, the creative process, the collaborative process. Without partaking in these (sometimes difficult, but also sometimes intensely pleasurable) processes, we cannot change. We do not acquire skills, our understanding of the world does not evolve, and our connections with others do not spread and flourish. Evidence is also coming out that reliance on generative AI is bad for brain functioning[1] and antithetical to the skill sets—so-called “soft” skills—that are increasingly viewed as desirable by employers[2]. Developing the ability to think critically, work collaboratively, and communicate effectively will prepare you for the job market, for participation in civic life, and for a well-rounded and fulfilling life. As such, your work in this course should be created by you. Any work not made by you will be considered a breach of academic integrity. Similarly, all course materials, assignments, feedback, and grades will be made by me without the use of generative AI. I look forward to enjoying the various processes of learning and creativity with you this semester!

  Student-teacher relationships are built on trust. For example, students must trust that teachers have made appropriate decisions about the structure and content of the courses they teach, and teachers must trust that the assignments that students turn in are their own.
