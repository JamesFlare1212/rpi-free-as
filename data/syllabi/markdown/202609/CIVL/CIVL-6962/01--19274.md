## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

This course introduces machine learning (ML) methods and their application to problems in transportation engineering. As transportation systems grow larger and more instrumented, the ability to learn from traffic data — loop detectors, probe vehicles, aerial and roadside video, trip records — has become a core engineering skill. The course follows the textbook chapter by chapter: transportation data and sensing, learning fundamentals, fully connected, convolutional, recurrent and graph neural networks, reinforcement and transfer learning, generative models, and edge and federated AI. Each method is treated the same way: the assumption it makes about the data, one derivation worked by hand on a small example, and a measured comparison against the simplest baseline that could do the job on real transportation data (PeMS, UAV imagery, CDTA transit data, NGSIM trajectories, NYC taxi records). Students gain hands-on experience with real data, with reading and presenting research papers, and with formulating a transportation problem properly as a machine learning problem.

## Learning Outcomes

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes (CLOs):

- Explain the fundamentals of machine learning and their relevance to transportation problems.
- Derive by hand, on a small example, how feed-forward, convolutional, and recurrent neural networks are trained (forward pass, back-propagation, one gradient step).
- State the assumption each architecture makes about the data — locality and weight sharing in space, in time, or over a graph — and judge whether a given transportation dataset satisfies it.
- Design an evaluation that cannot leak: a data split that fits the data, a trivial and a linear baseline, and a metric chosen for the decision at hand.
- Read a transportation machine-learning research paper critically and present it: what the model assumes, what it learned, and what the data could not tell it.
- Carry a transportation machine-learning project from problem statement to a presented, reproducible result, and consider its ethical and societal implications.

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes Assessment Measures:

By the end of this course, students will be able to:

1. Explain the fundamentals of machine learning and their relevance to transportation problems.
2. Derive by hand, on a small example, how feed-forward, convolutional, and recurrent neural networks are trained (forward pass, back-propagation, one gradient step).
3. State the assumption each architecture makes about the data — locality and weight sharing in space, in time, or over a graph — and judge whether a given transportation dataset satisfies it.
4. Design an evaluation that cannot leak: a data split that fits the data, a trivial and a linear baseline, and a metric chosen for the decision at hand.
5. Read a transportation machine-learning research paper critically and present it: what the model assumes, what it learned, and what the data could not tell it.
6. Carry a transportation machine-learning project from problem statement to a presented, reproducible result, and consider its ethical and societal implications.

**Homework (7 assignments).** Jupyter notebooks with coding tasks and short questions, research presentations, and handwritten derivations in the style of the in-class exercises (scanned and uploaded). Roughly one every two weeks.  
**In-class exercises.** Worksheets and short group exercises completed during class — hand derivations, paper-reading grids, design exercises. Collected in class; the lowest score is dropped.  
**Exam.** One in-class exam, tentatively Friday November 20 (or Tuesday November 17), covering Chapters 3–9: multiple choice, short answers, and one hand derivation in the style of Exercises 4.1–6.1.  
**Final project.** Individual. A transportation problem of your choice, with the baselines, split and negative result reported as taught in class. Idea proposal, mid-semester check-in, final presentation, final report.
