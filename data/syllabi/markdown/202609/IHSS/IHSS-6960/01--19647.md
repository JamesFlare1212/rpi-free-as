## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

Decolonizing Social Theory was designed to unfold conceptual frameworks and methodologies useful for comprehending current and chronic crises. The course aspires to enable students to enter into conversations with and about the past 50-60 years of anticolonial, postcolonial, and decolonial thought in intersection with feminist and antiracist work in STS, and the interpretivist or post-positivist humanities and social sciences broadly construed. We will trace the multiple genealogies and trajectories that have contributed to the emergence and enactment of this form of situated knowledge. This process we will refer to as unfolding.

Postcolonial, feminist, post-positivist, and antiracist STS are critical interdisciplinary formations that have cross-fertilized with cultural studies, studies of colonial discourse and postcolonial studies, Critical Race Theory and Critical Race Feminism, queer theory and sexuality studies, and the many disciplinary and adisciplinary formations that playfully and critically inform each. We will explore these multiplicitous and pluricentric positions to the greatest extent possible given constraints of time and space.

Professor Campbell apologizes in advance for the sheer amount of reading, listening, presenting, and/or viewing required. We will divide the labor wherever possible so that you are reading, listening, and viewing with and for one another. Please prepare to summarize, synthesize, and otherwise record your impressions via voice memo, reading, listening, or viewing notes, so as to recall and recast your impressions. The conversation will be structured, but these structures will not—indeed cannot—contain the full extent of your learning this semester.

Engagement will be assessed through six (6) arcs (below). Graduate courses are designed to enable you to broaden and deepen committed engagement with STS as a field, including as-yet unnamed STSs enacted through your own research and conceptual contributions. This course steers clear of “core” concepts, “foundational” theories, “canonical” texts but hopes that you will fold these into your own thinking. To ensure you acquire a comprehensive grasp of STS, which works in synergistic relationship with other knowledge formations, we will clarify genealogies, configurations, discourses, situations, and trajectories of feminist and antiracist STS without prescribing yours. The practical goal is intense immersion.

***Week 2* Waves of Postcolonial Thought**

**Tuesday, September 8**

There are multiple origin stories for “postcolonial theory,” but the genealogy from Aime Cesaire through Frantz Fanon is central to most. Robert J.C. Young in *White Mythologies* goes so far as to situate poststructuralist theory as a product of the Algerian war for independence, an anticolonial movement that became a full-blown critique of western philosophy. As with all origin stories, these are interested and somewhat mythological accounts. For today we will read some of this work, particularly that of Aime Cesaire, Fanon’s teacher, and Fanon’s *The Wretched of the Earth* or *Black Skin, White Masks.*

**Required Reading:**

1. Julian Go, Intro and Ch. 1, “Waves of Postcolonial Thought,” *Postcolonial Thought and Social Theory* (Oxford 2016): pp. 1-63. **BOOK**
2. Aime Cesaire, “Between Colonizer and Colonized,” *Social Theory: The Multicultural and Classic Readings*, ed. Charles Lemert, pp. 370-372. **LMS**

***Week 3* Travelling with** **Challenges to Western Thought**

**Tuesday, September 15**

Contrapuntal thinking of the sort that Edward Said was doing in *Orientalism* (1978) involves figures whose writings travel across time, space, and ideology, who are “un-mired” and “de-contextualized,” only to be “re-territorialized” in the ways that Fanon used phenomenology; or how Edward Said used Foucault, Gramsci, and Lukacs.

**Required reading:**

This grad student journal found itself enacting studies of “colonial discourse,” “minority discourse,” and “postcolonial theory,” based on the 1982 essay by Said titled “Travelling Theory” (1982). Said indicated that for theory to be alive enough to travel, it must be grounded in specific historical context (his example is “reification,” which emerged from Georg Lukacs’ Budapest, Hungary, in the 1920s). The patterned movement of theory requires:

- Origin point(s) in specific political configuration or social formation;
- Distance travelled as ideas move to other times and places along different trajectories and through various contexts;
- Material conditions of acceptance or resistance that confront the transplanted theory;
- Transformation of theory to new uses, new positions.

***Week 12* Subaltern Standpoints**

**November 17**

Feminist transformation of science studies grew out of and alongside colonial and postcolonial and decolonial studies. Feminist scientists, historians, and philosophers of science, the latter schooled in logical positivism, began to critique science studies even as they began to read and produce it. They aimed to change feminist movements as well as labor, pro-democracy, civil rights, Black Power, Third World, women’s health, reproductive rights, and other movements in the late 1960s and early 1970s. Theirs was a critical endeavor emphasizing that science has been a largely masculine domain dominated by modes of expertise, value neutrality, and objectivity that foreclosed tacit knowledge, relational proximity between researchers and researched, and situationally specific elements.

**Required Reading:**

## Learning Outcomes

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes (CLOs):

- 1\. Students will demonstrate comprehensive grasp of key concepts and theorizing in social theory in relation to postcolonial and decolonial theory, including feminist and antiracist STS, through reflexive reading and writing.
- 2\. Students will demonstrate fluency by facilitating discussions to contextualize and think critically about social theory.
- 3\. Students will demonstrate ability to trouble or “provoke” discussion by presenting on and integrating textual and experiential knowledges in conversation with social theory.
- 4\. Students will demonstrate the ability to synthesize and reflect upon (ruminate upon) assigned readings, lectures, and classroom discussion in written formats.
- 5\. Students will supply “meditations” of their own in order to integrate concepts from social theory and postcolonial and decolonial thought with cultural artifacts in their own arenas of study.
- 6\. Students will individually author and present a seminar paper “Decolonizing X” in which they apply theory to practice or interpret a cultural artifact using postcolonial and decolonial approaches. For STS students who will be assembling a portfolio, this essay should be useful for documenting the breadth of STS in the portfolio exam, or for the literature review to appear in the dissertation proposal and grant proposals.

**ARCS OF ASSESSMENT**

**Arc 1: Where Are We Now? Synthesis (BEGINNING OF EACH CLASS)**

Capacity to improvise and synthesize-- to “think on your feet” is essential to success in graduate school and elsewhere. This arc requires you to provide a brief oral synthesis of the previous seminar in the first **20** **minutes** of the next class so we can all get onto the same page. Those opening the class should listen closely to the previous session, framing continuities and synthesizing all of our work to draw together the streams of discussion, reminding us where we have been, and giving some thoughts about where we are going by raising questions. We all need help to build continuities in times of discontinuities. The synthesis is not to exceed 20 minutes. Practice.

**Arc 2: Discussion Facilitation (IN CLASS)**

On the date for which you sign up, you are responsible for introducing materials that will help frame and facilitate discussion for 1 hour. Draw upon the main points of our readings for that day and choose one of the following options: 1. Add context; 2. “Apply” concepts to a specific analytic situation or text of your own choosing; 3. Draw upon additional material to provide genealogy. Above all, keep the conversation moving, and ensure fairness in terms of who gets to speak, how long they do so, etc.

**Arc 3: Meditation (OUT OF CLASS; SUMMARY PRESENTED IN CLASS)**

Meditate on the area or artifact that you plan to “decolonize” for your final paper, “Decolonizing X.” Use the meditation to start exploring the theoretical concepts you will integrate from our readings and discussions on anticolonial, postcolonial, and decolonial thought with cultural artifacts in your own arena of interest and study. The “mediation” is to think about how you will mediate between past and present in your future work. All should be ready to present a short (10 minute) account of your Meditation in class on October 13.

**Arc 4: Provocation (OUT OF CLASS; PRESENTED IN CLASS)**

Provocations “trouble” the readings by introducing critical projects that are orthogonal or oppositional, occluded or excluded, in relation to them. This is your chance to engage the texts under collective examination. Provocations should be formally written up (7-10) pages and sent to the professor the night before the class meeting. Provocations draw upon **additional** material, texts, and contexts, relating the texts for the day with *different* sources, intentions, or characteristics from those under “official” discussion. They should be directly (not tangentially) related to the texts on the syllabus, but add something new. Provocations may include video, performance, or other media that provide substantive examples of STS work in art worlds, digital media, or data science.

**Arc 5: Decolonizing X (OUT OF CLASS; PRESENTED IN CLASS)**

Your final assignment for this course is a position paper summarizing and applying your current understanding of Decolonizing as a verb to a topic within the fields of STS, which by now you realize is comprised of interlocking “science studies.” Choose a focus around which to arrange your characterizations; add texts and contexts to those discussed in the course (you may also include your provocation); but reflect as fully as possible upon the body of knowledge presented in this course. This reflection should be 15-20 pages, typed, double-spaced, polished, documented.

***Week 12* Subaltern Standpoints**

**November 17**

Feminist transformation of science studies grew out of and alongside colonial and postcolonial and decolonial studies. Feminist scientists, historians, and philosophers of science, the latter schooled in logical positivism, began to critique science studies even as they began to read and produce it. They aimed to change feminist movements as well as labor, pro-democracy, civil rights, Black Power, Third World, women’s health, reproductive rights, and other movements in the late 1960s and early 1970s. Theirs was a critical endeavor emphasizing that science has been a largely masculine domain dominated by modes of expertise, value neutrality, and objectivity that foreclosed tacit knowledge, relational proximity between researchers and researched, and situationally specific elements.
