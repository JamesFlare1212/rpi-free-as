## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

How do ethnographers know the world? What are their methods and objects? How does this influence their production of texts and claims to objectivity? In this class we seek answers to these questions through two interrelated tasks: The first is reading ethnographies in anthropology, sociology, and cultural studies to direct our attention to ethnographic objects and ways of knowing them. The second is practicing ethnographic practice through the development of an ethnographic research project. This will require students to develop a pilot project—or continue an ongoing one—and conducting a series of interviews and descriptions of processes. By juxtaposing these two practices—one based on student research and the other based on close readings and discussion—we can come to some preliminary understandings of how and why ethnographers collect the data they do, how they analyze it, and how they contribute to ongoing scholarly, policy, and public conversations.

Ethnographers believe—whether they admit it or not—that the data collected through ethnographic practice demands presentation in generic forms. Because of this, a key element in our discussion of texts will be how they are structured formally, how those structures motivate their content, and what ethnographic genres do, rhetorically. Reading ethnography is also vital to conceptualize how to rally ethnographic data in convincing ways. To these twin ends, we’ll read several foundational ethnographies from the 1980s, 1990s, and early 2000s, many of which set or catalyzed trends. We’ll also consider some more marginal ethnographies and consider why they might have been (and might continue to be) marginal to anthropology, sociology, and cultural studies.

## Learning Outcomes

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes (CLOs):

- Students will become fluent in the formal analysis of published ethnography;
- Students will discern the role of disciplinary norms in ethnographic research and writing;
- Students will develop confidence in producing discipline-appropriate ethnographic writing;
- Students will gain skills in analyzing academic writing through peer review.

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes Assessment Measures:

*Ethnographic Research Paper* (20 points; plus 10 components, each worth 4 points; 60 points total): You will write an original ethnographic research paper based on research that you conduct during the course of the semester. This will include Pitching a Project, Designing a Methodology, Assembling Resources (e.g., identifying supporting scholarly material), conducting no less than 4 interviews, describing two processes, and writing a Rough Draft. Your interviews and process descriptions may provide the basis for your paper, or you can select to use only some of them; you may also conduct more interviews or describe more processes, depending on your research project. Your paper should abide by generic conventions relative to your field, but must include an interpretive argument and a literature review (based on your Assembled Resources). Additional details about each of these components will be discussed in class.

*Two Peer Assessments* (8 points total; 4 points each): You will conduct a peer review of the rough drafts turned in by your fellow students. Read this for tips on peer review.

*Four leadership memos* (32 points total; 8 points each): At four points in the term you will lead class discussion. In order to facilitate this, you should prepare notes based on the assigned reading that address the following questions: what theories structure the author’s overarching argument?; if it is a book, how does the overarching argument integrate arguments in each chapter of the book?; what kinds of evidence does the author employ?; how is this evidence produced, methodologically?; how does the evidence reflect theoretical commitments? You will be leading class discussion with one or more of your fellow students; it may be useful to plan how you will lead discussion and to distribute readings to ensure that all of them are covered. Your leadership memo should be your own and reflect your engagement with the text. Memos should be approximately 1500 words. Please deposit your leadership memos in this folder no later than the start of class on the day that you are leading discussion. Please title your memos based on the text and your name, e.g., Total Confinement_Wolf-Meyer.

## Grading Criteria

### Criteria Details:

Pitching a Project—In a page (~300 words), address: what is the problem you’re seeking to understanding? Where is it located? Who are the people involved? What kinds of processes are central to the problem? This should comprise a paragraph (or more) and lay out the empirical foundation of the study. In another paragraph, address: How will you access the place? What do you need to know to answer the questions that you have? How will you proceed in collecting the answers to these questions? This should be a first draft of your methodology.

Designing a Methodology—In a page (~300 words), describe what you will do in order to answer the questions laid out in the Pitching a Project assignment. This should include a list of questions that you will ask people who are involved in the fieldsite (i.e., an interview schedule) as well as a list of processes you seek to observe and how you will set about observing them. What do you need to observe and how does it relate to the questions you have?

Assembling Resources (e.g., identifying supporting scholarly material)—Identify two literatures that motivate your project with 5-10 items associated with each. Check out this summary of writing literature reviews. You should write 1-2 paragraphs about each (~1500 words total) of the literatures that you’ve identified that discuss main themes in the literature and how they relate to your project. Seek to have a motivating question distilled from each of the literatures that your ethnographic project can answer.

Interviews (four times)—Develop your preliminary interview schedule (from Designing a Methodology) into a formal interview schedule. It should be ~15 questions long and organized in a logical way. You may diverge from the schedule, but it is important to have to ensure that you are getting answers to the questions that you have. Plan on your interviews to be 60 to 90 minutes long. You must record them (using Voice Memo on an iPhone is fine and you can also check out better recorders from Karen Osborne [osbork2@rpi.edu]) and prepare a transcript to turn in. (Voice Memo automatically generates a transcript.) You should highlight the important parts of the interview.

Describing a processes (twice)—Observe a process or conduct additional interviews about other people’s participation in a process. Describe it in as much detail as possible. Who are the actors involved? What objects are involved? Are there bureaucratic elements to the process? What is being moved from one state to another? What does the state change entail among the actors? The context? These descriptions should be no less than ~600 words long each.

Rough Draft—Assemble your pieces into an ethnographic article manuscript. You should follow the steps laid out in this series. Your final piece should include an introduction, your literature reviews, a map of the paper’s contents and your argument, at least two empirical sections that relate to your argument, and a conclusion. This should be 6000-8000 words. You should also include a bibliography. Your rough draft will be peer reviewed by two of your peers to provide you with instructions for your revision.
