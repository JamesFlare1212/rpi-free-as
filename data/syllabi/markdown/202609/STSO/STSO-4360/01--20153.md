## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

What will it take for our communities to survive & thrive as we weather global climate change? Some may first identify the need for durable and effective infrastructures like sea walls, storm drains, clean power plants, resilient grids, and efficient transportation systems. Decent and affordable housing, dignified jobs, and effective schools are also important, especially for those of us who want to live in democratic and free societies. Whatever and wherever we are building (or storing or wasting) things, there are people with a stake in the outcomes – neighbors, workers, future generations.

In this course students will volunteer off campus for approximately 40-50 hours a with ongoing civic, community, and government initiatives related to climate change in Troy and the Capital region. Students will explore a variety of “theories of change,” connecting these theories to approaches used by a wide variety of organizations, developing their own stances on how to effectively contribute to goals of community resilience, environmental justice, and social cohesion. Students will develop skills in project management, communication, community building, and civic engagement.

## Course Content

### Content Details:

This course is part theory, readings, and discussion / part in-person service learning with an off campus organization or initiative.

The first five weeks of class (through Thus Sept 25) will be for orientation, foundations, and preparing for work off campus. These weeks will introduce you to the key theories and practices we will be working with and through for the rest of the semester. After Sept 25, we will use the following structure:

- Mondays for more class discussions on assigned readings. Everyone is expected to be in class for the full class period, having read and analyzed the readings. You should be prepared to discuss the larger themes and their relationship to your own work.
- Thursdays for project work, cohort meetings, and progress meetings with Dr. Black. Unless otherwise specified, you will largely not need to be in class on these days.

See the schedule for deviations from this structure, especially toward the end of the semester.

### Academic Policies:

- ### Academic Integrity

  Student-teacher relationships are built on trust. For example, students must trust that teachers have made appropriate decisions about the structure and content of the courses they teach, and teachers must trust that the assignments that students turn in are their own. Acts that violate this trust undermine the educational process. The Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities and The Graduate Student Supplement define various forms of Academic Dishonesty and you should make yourself familiar with these. In this class, all assignments that are turned in for a grade must represent the student’s own work. In cases where help was received, or teamwork was allowed, a notation on the assignment should indicate your collaboration.

  Violations of academic integrity may also be reported to the appropriate Dean (Dean of Students for undergraduate students or the Dean of Graduate Education for graduate students, respectively).

  If you have any question concerning this policy before submitting an assignment, please ask for clarification. In addition, you can visit the following site for more information on our Academic Integrity Policy: [Students Rights, Responsibilities, and Judicial Affairs](https://info.rpi.edu/dean-students/student-rights-responsibilities-and-judicial-affairs).
