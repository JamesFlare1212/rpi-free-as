## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

Today's global health challenges extend beyond biological and technical concerns, encompassing social, economic, and political dimensions as well. This course seeks to explore the intricate relationships among the economic, political, social, ethical, behavioral, and biomedical aspects of health through interdisciplinary lenses. It offers an introduction to key concepts and methodologies of Medical Anthropology/Sociology, Science and Technology Studies, and Critical Public & Global Health. Students will gain a comprehensive understanding of the diverse health challenges faced by populations both locally and internationally, alongside the economic, political, and cultural factors that influence these issues. By examining a diverse range of health issues, from addiction and eating disorders to cancer and reproductive health, students will explore how health is affected by structural inequalities, stigma, and discrimination. Throughout the course, students will engage in critical readings, discussions, and assignments designed to enhance their understanding of global and public health issues and to develop their ability to conduct independent research. The course will also delve into issues of deservingness in health care, exploring who is deemed worthy of protection and care within different systems.

The course aims to equip students with the skills necessary to critically analyze health policies and to advocate for equitable health practices. Students will engage with critical scholarship that will cover not only the foundational principles of global health but also the contemporary debates on public health ethics and policy. Students will develop critical analytical skills by engaging with a broad array of readings, case studies, and discussions, enabling them to understand and address pressing health challenges in a global context. In-class discussions will explore the ethical implications of prioritizing profit over saving lives, scrutinizing who gains from medical advancements and who holds power over access to treatments, while also examining the impact of influential donors like the Gates Foundation on global health initiatives.

*This course is reading-intensive and designed to encourage students engage with complex theories, methods, and ethical considerations central to global and public health.* Students will delve into a wide array of challenging texts that will deepen their understanding of health disparities and global health dynamics. Active participation in discussions is essential, as it will enhance comprehension and critical thinking skills. This course will prepare students for the rigors of graduate study by fostering analytical skills and intellectual curiosity. Completing the readings and engaging in class discussions are crucial components of this course, providing a solid foundation for advanced academic and professional pursuits in the field of public and global health.

## Course Goals

### Goals:

By the end of the course, students will be able to:

- ♦ Learn the ethical challenges and obstacles essential in addressing global health problems.
- ♦ Critically assess the theoretical frameworks and paradigms within global public health.
- ♦ Analyze the tension between profit motives and the ethical imperative to save lives, evaluating the ownership and accessibility of medical treatments.
- ♦ Explore the intersections of gender, race, and disability in health, and their impact on access to care and treatment.
- ♦ Enhance written and verbal communication skills by engaging in written assignments, participating in class activities and discussions.
- ♦ Think and write critically about primary source material to analyze and assess the political, cultural, representational, interpersonal, and affective dimensions of disease.

- ♦ Understand the political, economic, social, cultural, and biological factors that contribute to regional epidemics across the globe.
- ♦ Evaluate the social construction of “risk groups,” including which groups suffer the highest burden of infection and why these groups are particularly vulnerable.
- ♦ Identify key contributions that anthropologists and social epidemiologists have made to an interdisciplinary understanding of stigma, discrimination, risk, and vulnerability.
- ♦ Employ multiple modes of analysis (historical, sociological, cultural, and literary) to explore how epidemic disease outbreaks have mobilized anxieties about race, ethnicity, sexuality, and national belonging.

**5) Futures of HIV**

***Objective***

This assignment asks you to think critically and creatively about the future of HIV. What might the world look like 20, 30, or 50 years from now? Will HIV still exist? Will advances in medicine make HIV preventable but not curable? Will scientists develop a functional or sterilizing cure? Or will HIV continue to exist because access to treatment, prevention, and healthcare remains deeply unequal?

This is a speculative and creative writing assignment, but your speculation must be informed by what we currently know about HIV, public health, technology, and society. Think outside the box. There is no single correct HIV future.

You may imagine a future in which HIV has been completely eliminated, one in which HIV remains part of everyday life, or something in between. You should also think beyond the virus itself. Over the past four decades, HIV has produced an enormous infrastructure of clinics, community organizations, activists, researchers, pharmaceutical companies, testing programs, prevention programs, surveillance systems, and funding institutions. What happens to this infrastructure if HIV can be cured? What should be preserved, dismantled, or transformed?

Similarly, the existence of a cure does not necessarily mean the end of HIV. Who will have access to a cure? Who might refuse it? Why might someone choose to continue living with HIV rather than undergo a cure? What happens to people who cannot access it? Could a cure create new forms of stigma or inequality between those who are "cured" and those who remain HIV-positive?

Your task is therefore not simply to predict whether HIV will be cured, but to imagine what different futures of HIV might look like and who gets to inhabit them.

***Instructions***

1. *Research the Present Before Imagining the Future*

Begin by researching the current state of HIV treatment, prevention, and cure research.

Your research might include:

- Current HIV treatment and the role of antiretroviral therapy (ART)
- PrEP, PEP, and other prevention technologies
- U=U (Undetectable = Untransmittable)
- HIV vaccine research
- HIV cure research, including approaches such as latency reversal ("shock and kill"), broadly neutralizing antibodies (bNAbs), gene editing/CRISPR, stem-cell therapies, or other emerging technologies
- Global inequalities in access to HIV treatment and prevention
- HIV stigma and discrimination
- HIV activism and community organizations
- The healthcare and public-health infrastructure that has developed around HIV
- Pozfuturism

You do not need to discuss all of these. Instead, identify the developments that are most important for the future you want to imagine.

2. *Imagine a Future of HIV*

Choose a point approximately 20–50 years in the future and imagine the status of HIV at that moment.

Your future might involve the complete elimination of HIV, the development of a cure that remains inaccessible to many people, continued reliance on ART, the emergence of new prevention technologies, or a world in which HIV remains present but has acquired very different social meanings.

You are encouraged to be creative. Your future can be optimistic, pessimistic, dystopian, utopian, or—perhaps most realistically—a complicated mixture of these possibilities.

However, your imagined future must develop from current scientific, political, and social conditions rather than appearing out of nowhere.

3. *Consider the Social Consequences*

Your essay should consider not only what happens to HIV, but what happens to the world that has developed around HIV.

Questions you might consider include:

- o Science and technology: What technological developments have occurred? Is there a cure? If so, what kind? How does it work? Is it safe, affordable, and widely available?
- o Access and inequality: Who receives new treatments or cures first? Are they available globally? What happens in countries or communities that cannot afford them?
- o Acceptance and refusal: Does everyone want to be cured? Why might some people refuse a cure? How would governments, healthcare providers, families, or communities respond to people who remain HIV-positive when a cure is available?
- o Infrastructure: What happens to HIV clinics, testing centers, NGOs, community organizations, research laboratories, surveillance systems, pharmaceutical companies, and international HIV programs if HIV becomes curable or disappears?
- o Activism and community: What happens to HIV activism after a cure? Do organizations disappear, or do they redirect their expertise and resources toward other health and social problems? What forms of knowledge or community might be lost if HIV infrastructure disappears?
- o Stigma and identity: Would a cure eliminate HIV stigma? Or might it create new forms of stigma toward people who remain HIV-positive? What happens to communities and identities that have been shaped through decades of living with and responding to HIV?
- o Public health: What would it actually mean to declare HIV "over"? Who gets to decide when an epidemic has ended?

You do not need to answer every question. Select the questions that are most important for the future you are imagining.

4. *Writing the Essay*

- o Introduction: Introduce the HIV future you are imagining. Briefly explain the current scientific and social conditions from which this future develops.
- o Body: Describe how HIV treatment, prevention, cure technologies, healthcare infrastructure, activism, and social attitudes have changed. Explain the scientific and social developments that made this future possible. Pay particular attention to inequality: different populations and different parts of the world may experience very different HIV futures.
- o Your essay should combine imagination with evidence. Use academic research, public-health reports, current cure research, and other credible sources to explain why your imagined future is plausible.
- o Conclusion: Reflect on what your imagined future teaches us about HIV today. What decisions would we need to make now to produce a more equitable future? What should public-health institutions, researchers, activists, and communities begin preparing for today?

***Formatting and Submission***

- o The essay should be 1,500–2,000 words in length (feel free to write longer).
- o Use a clear and coherent structure. You may use headings and subheadings if helpful.
- o Your essay should draw on credible academic and public-health sources while leaving room for informed speculation and creativity.
- o Submit the essay through the course's online portal by the specified deadline.

***Evaluation Criteria***

- o Research and Scientific Grounding (25%) Demonstrates an understanding of the current state of HIV treatment, prevention, cure research, and/or public-health infrastructure and uses credible sources to support the imagined future.
- o Creativity and Speculation (25%) Develops an original, thoughtful, and plausible vision of an HIV future rather than simply summarizing existing HIV research.
- o Critical Thinking (30%) Examines the social, political, ethical, and economic consequences of the imagined future, including questions of inequality, access, infrastructure, stigma, or cure refusal.
- o Writing Quality (20%) Presents ideas clearly and coherently, develops a compelling argument or narrative, and follows the assignment guidelines.

**6) Public Health Futures Lab**

For your final project, your group will imagine the future of a major public health issue approximately **30–50 years from now**. Each group will choose a major public-health issue—cancer, addiction, antimicrobial resistance, reproductive health, sexual health, climate-related disease, mental health, aging, pandemics, AI in healthcare, etc.—and design its world in 2050 or 2075.

You cannot simply give a presentation predicting the future. You must create artifacts from that future: a government announcement, advertisement for a new technology, patient testimony, news report, clinical guideline, activist poster, insurance policy, protest material, or something similar.

Then you give the class a tour of their future and explain:

What happened? Who benefited? Who was harmed? What technologies emerged? What inequalities persisted? What institutions disappeared or were created? What decisions made in 2026 produced this future?

Your future can be utopian, dystopian, or—more realistically—a complicated mixture of both. You may imagine a future in which the health problem has become worse, transformed into something different, or even been completely solved.

But remember: **solving a health problem does not necessarily mean that its story ends.**

- o *Step 1:* Choose a Public Health Issue and let your professor now (groups will not be allowed to present on the same topic)
- o *Step 2:* Research the Present
- o *Step 3:* Build Your Public Health Future
- o *Step 4:* Create a Museum of Your Future

Your group will create a small **museum exhibition from your imagined future**.

Imagine that visitors in 2050–2075 are learning about the transformation of your public health issue. Your exhibition should contain **at least five artifacts** that help visitors understand how the world changed.

Artifacts can be physical, digital, visual, or audio. You may create fictional artifacts, but they must be grounded in your research.

For example, your museum might contain:

- An obsolete medication bottle
- A public health poster
- A newspaper front page announcing a cure
- An advertisement for a future medical technology
- A government health warning
- A patient diary or testimony
- An activist poster
- A clinical guideline
- A fictional medical device
- A pharmaceutical advertisement
- A protest sign
- A social media post
- A map or epidemiological visualization
- A doctor's note or medical record
- An object from an organization that no longer exists
- An oral history recording
- A memorial to people affected by the health problem

Each artifact should have a short **museum label** explaining what it is, when it is from, and why it matters.

The artifacts should not simply decorate your presentation. Together, they should **tell the story of how we got from the present to your imagined future.**

*Step 5:* Final Reflection: Looking Back from the Future

Your exhibition should end with one final question:

What decisions made today helped create this future?

Imagine looking backward from 2050–2075 toward 2026.

What could people have done differently?

What opportunities did they recognize—or fail to recognize?

What warnings did they ignore?

What institutions did they preserve, dismantle, or transform?

Most importantly, what does your imagined future reveal about the choices public health faces **right now**?

***Evaluation:***

- o **Research and Plausibility — 25%** The future is grounded in credible research and develops logically from current scientific, social, political, and technological conditions.
- o **Critical Analysis — 25%** The project examines inequality, institutions, infrastructure, power, and unintended consequences rather than simply predicting technological change.
- o **Creativity and World-Building — 25%** The group creates an original and coherent future, and the museum artifacts effectively bring that world to life.
- o **Museum Exhibition and Presentation — 15%** The artifacts work together to tell a story, the exhibition is engaging and interactive, and the group can explain and defend its choices.
- o **Collaboration — 15%** The project demonstrates meaningful participation and contribution from all group members.

You may use personal technology at your discretion in the ways that work best for your learning. This is subject to change should technology begin to cause distraction to the instructor and/or your peers. Please, be comfortable, but respect that the classroom is a space for communal learning.

**Access & Accommodation:** We all learn in different ways, and we all work to enable our own learning process under various circumstances that may be beyond our control. If for any reason you become concerned about your ability to fully participate in our course due to the structure of the activities or assignments, please talk to me immediately. I am personally committed to providing all students equal access to learning opportunities in my courses. If you are registered with the Disability Resource Center, please notify me at the beginning of the semester and we will set up a meeting to discuss what is needed to ensure your success in the class. If you are not (yet) registered with Disability Services, but know that your learning style requires specific attention, please talk with me as soon as possible regarding your experience. If you are a non-native English speaker/writer and need extra assistance or accommodation with the reading and writing assignments please talk with me as soon as possible. If you are a parent, do not let struggles with childcare stop you from coming to class! Your child is welcome in the classroom as needed. Similarly, if you are working one or more full-time jobs and struggling with course deadlines, be in communication with me as soon as possible so we may find a way to keep you engaged in the class.

Rensselaer Polytechnic Institute strives to make all learning experiences as accessible as possible. If you anticipate or experience academic barriers based on a disability, please let me know immediately so that we can discuss your options. To establish reasonable accommodations, please register with The Office of Disability Services for Students. After registration, make arrangements with the Director of Disability Services as soon as possible to discuss your accommodations so that they may be implemented in a timely fashion. DSS contact information: dss@rpi.edu ; +1-518-276-8197; 4226 Academy Hall.

**Attendance:** Students are allowed two excused absences, no questions asked. Following University policy, any documented absence due to University sanctioned events, family emergencies, religious holidays, or illness will be excused. To have an absence excused for any of these reasons, simply email me prior to our class meetings. Staying in communication with me is key. Beyond excused absences, I will subtract 5 pts per additional absence out of your attendance grade. Students will be marked absent if they do not sign the sign in sheet.

If you miss a class, you are responsible for making up the material and should contact a classmate for discussion notes and class announcements. If absent, I encourage you to spend extra time on the class Moodle Forums for days that you are gone. As an access measure, I will post my class PPT each day after class so that you may see our class work. You are welcome to do in-class activities that you may have missed and turn that work in when you return for participation credit. I cannot go over class material via email, but I will happily answer questions and go over assignments and notes during my office hours.

**Use of AI and ChatGPT:** You can use artificial intelligence and language l models to (1) brainstorm your ideas and (2) get feedback on your writing. Don’t forget that ChatGPT is a language model, not a factual model. You *cannot* use such services to (1) fact check; (2) write a paper; (3) or replace critical thinking. Your instructors are experienced faculty, and they will notice when/if you submit an assignment created by AI. This will automatically result in 0 points for the assignment, and you will fail the course should you repeat the same mistake. If you have found other creative ways to use AI to assist with your learning, feel free to come to my office hours to discuss it with me.

**Communication:** I will be easily accessible for one-on-one meetings if you need any help. I expect you to check your University email for class updates and be responsible for the information provided to you. You may use email to ask for clarification or instructions on how to complete an assignment, but please check the syllabus and LMS first for answers to your questions. When sending an email, please be sure to include the course number, your name, and the topic of the email in the subject line. The body of your email should contain specific questions and information as well as professional communication etiquette. Out of respect for your instructor’s time and effort outside of class, allow up to 48 hours for email responses—**please** **no urgent emails.**
