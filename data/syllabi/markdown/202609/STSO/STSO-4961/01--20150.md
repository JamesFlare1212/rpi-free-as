## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

Energy in History  
The social history of energy illustrates how social, cultural, political, and environmental contexts shape technological change over time, and how science and technology shape social contexts in which energy is generated, transmitted, consumed, and used to generate value. This course centers on how public and private values are translated into energy policy and practice.

Multimodal coursework will allow students to analyze how energy landscapes have moved from animal and agricultural energy to mineral energy to nuclear energy to solar, geothermal, wave, and wind energy. Although regional and topical focus is on the role of coal, oil, and nuclear power in the history of electrification in the United States from the 1800s into the present, critical energy histories are emerging everywhere and comparative examples abound. Students may introduce global content in self-defined assignments.

Using case studies to show how capital and government intertwine to motivate or impede transition or symbiosis in energy regimes, the course investigates social impacts of war, violence, social conflicts, and economic recession; social movements to address public health, risk, and harm in energy generation, transmission, and consumption; technological innovation to explore power’s uneven distribution and effects in energy landscapes. Upper-level interdisciplinary humanities and social sciences courses stimulate critical thinking about public policy, the policymaking process, and documents through which knowledge about social and environmental outcomes is made and demonstrated.

By the end of this course, you will be able to discuss the historical, political, economic, social, and cultural factors that have enabled and constrained energy history in the past and in the present. You will be better prepared to participate in conversations about energy futures, as well as knowing something about energy pasts and presents.

**Course Assessments Reading Energy History Presentation (20%)**

We cannot assume that everyone will come to class prepared for discussion, so each student is responsible for brief (10-15-minute) reading summaries that get the class on the same page. 1. summarize key points or “arguments” and relevant supporting evidence;
2. make 3-5 connections to other works, including references cited in the text, mass media or popular culture;
3. present this material so that the entire class becomes familiar with it.

No one student will likely be able to read every source included on the syllabus. Our goal with this assignment is to pool our efforts and support one another’s learning.

Your task is to use Carol Bacchi's What's the Problem Represented to Be? (WPR) approach to examine how energy and environmental problems are framed and represented. Your presentation should answer these questions:

- What assumptions underlie this problem representation?

- What is left unproblematized or ignored?
- What policy “solutions” or futures are made imaginable or foreclosed?

- Critically evaluate relationships between knowledge, power, and problem definition.

- Facilitate thoughtful classroom discussion.
- Assess the usefulness of selected episodes for future versions of the course.

***Energy Archaeology Project (25%)*** DO NOT UPLOAD ASSIGNMENTS TO LMS. Bring them to class. If you can’t be in class on the day this is due, email it to campbn2@rpi.edu and campbell@rpi.edu. This semester-long, multimodal assignment invites students to explore the experiences, influences, and perspectives that shape their attitudes, beliefs, and values about energy, including its production, distribution, and consumption. Through reflection, research, and analysis, students will create their own **Energy Archaeology**—a history of how energy has influenced their lives as individuals and as participants in families, communities, states, and nations. Students will collect and analyze artifacts such as family photographs, oral histories, utility bills, transportation records, maps, newspaper articles, academic research, government reports, podcasts, documentaries, artwork, advertisements, and original photographs of local energy infrastructure.

**Suggested Steps for Constructing Your Energy Archaeology Project**

Step 1: Create an Energy Timeline: Develop a historical timeline of significant milestones, events, or memories that have shaped your relationship with energy, including energy disruptions and/or transitions.

Step 2: Conduct Energy Interviews: Interview at least one family or community member about their experiences with energy, including energy disruptions and/or transitions, or find oral history interviews online.

Step 3: Analyze Local Energy Systems: Investigate where your electricity and energy resources come from, who governs or regulate them, who profits from them, etc. Follow those transmission lines back to their source.

Step 4: Connect Personal Experiences to Larger Systems: Connect your story to climate change, environmental justice or resilience, technology, energy policy, sustainability, the “macro-level” contexts that your microcosmic history nests within. Don’t worry, Professor Campbell will give you examples and in-class time to work up topics and narratives.

Step 5: Gather and Evaluate Evidence: Collect at least 5 academic sources, 3 non-academic sources, 2 personal/community artifacts, and 2 visual sources.

Step 6: Draft Your Energy Archaeology: Answer the question: How has energy shaped my life, and how does my personal history connect to larger systems?

**Your Final Product** will be a well-researched narrative that combines personal reflection, historical analysis, and evidence from multiple types of sources. You should turn in a multimedia paper that incorporates artifacts, images, data visualizations, and a written summary of your key findings. Your conclusion should explain why your energy story matters and how it connects to energy systems, reflecting on what you learned in this course from readings, listenings, discussions, presentations, and considering future implications. Your conclusion must:

- Revisit your central question or theme.

- Summarize major insights rather than details.

- Connect personal experiences to evolving trends in broader macro-systems.

- Reflect on how your understandings have changed since August!

The goal of this project is to help you connect personal experiences and knowledges with broader social, cultural, environmental, and political issues related to the history of energy. Your thoughtful and well-supported energy archaeology will demonstrate critical reflection and engagement with course concepts and content.

- ### Academic Integrity

  Student-teacher relationships are built on trust. For example, students must trust that teachers have made appropriate decisions about the structure and content of the courses they teach, and teachers must trust that the assignments that students turn in are their own. Acts that violate this trust undermine the educational process. The Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities and The Graduate Student Supplement define various forms of Academic Dishonesty and you should make yourself familiar with these. In this class, all assignments that are turned in for a grade must represent the student’s own work. In cases where help was received, or teamwork was allowed, a notation on the assignment should indicate your collaboration.

### Additional Academic Integrity Course Policy and Penalty Information:

Your professor is far more interested in engaging with \*your\* thoughts than with AI. Whenever you use somebody else’s words—or words “generated” for you—you MUST put them in quotation marks and cite their source. Don’t use AI to “generate” text. You may use it as a research assistant or text editor, but you MUST verify output and indicate within quotation marks \*anything\* that was generated by it—just as you would credit any source by citing it properly. We will put together an AI policy in the first weeks of class that ensures that we are all clear on what uses may/may not be made. At minimum AI use must be disclosed by citing it as you would any other verified source: in footnotes, end notes that show what AI tool you used, what you used it for, what prompts you used, and how you evaluate the contribution to your thinking and writing. To responsibly and ethically use an AI tool, the *Chicago Manual of Style*, 17th edition suggests the following format: ChatGPT, response to “Explain how to verify that AI citations are not made up,” OpenAI, July 23, 2026. https://www.chicagomanualofstyle.org/qanda/data/faq/topics/Documentation/faq0422.html

**Please do not present AI-generated text as original work.** Verify all AI output to make sure “hallucination” has not taken place. All AI-generated references must be verified for accuracy. Authors are responsible for catching these. Your professor is not interested in policing reliance on AI, but in motivating and partnering in your learning, supporting your educative process, and evaluating your progress (i.e. grading your work).

### Additional Course Specific DEI Information:

**Safe Space, Brave Space:** This classroom is intended to be a place where we can all be our full selves, explore new ideas, and talk about feelings and experiences that connect with the subject matter without *fear*. We promote this ethos by:

- active listening
- valuing divergent perspectives
- recognizing one another’s unique insights and senses of humor
- understanding the partiality of *all* our perspectives.

We all have limitations or biases that can lead to distorted, lopsided, or unethical attitudes, policies, and other outcomes. Please avoid shaming and blaming, using hateful or hurtful language, or name-calling (*ad hominem attacks*) both in person and online. We aspire to create a learning environment where people of all identities foster an inclusive and welcoming environment, strengthen our skills, enrich discussion, and reflect professionalism. Historians recognize that labels and definitions of gender, racial/ethnic categories, ability, and socioeconomic status vary across time and space. We avoid racial/ethnic/gender slurs and respect how people describe, express, and experience race, ethnicity, gender, and cultural heritage. We recognize that ableist language can devalue people with physical and mental disabilities and permeates everyday language, and aspire to thoughtful alternatives.
