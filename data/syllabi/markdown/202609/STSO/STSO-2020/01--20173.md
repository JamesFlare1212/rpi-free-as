## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

Welcome to the Co-Creation Lab, an experimental space for students in Studio III. This course builds on the foundations you've developed so far, pushing you toward more ambitious, self-directed, and conceptually rigorous design work. You'll be expected to think critically, work iteratively, and engage deeply with the formal and social dimensions of design. Each year, we choose a different theme for Studio III that aligns with our focus on societal systems and "complexity." This year's theme is: "Collaboration." Throughout the course, we will critically apply engineering design methods and design processes learned in previous studio classes, and we will deconstruct them, asking at every step:

1\) How can we make this moment in the design process collaborative?

2\) How is this moment in the design process already collaborative?

3\) What are the internal organization(s) and elements of this collaboration?

We're asking you to interpret "collaboration" broadly. "Collaboration" might mean designing with a user or a community of users. It might also mean building bridges between communities and translating values and concerns into product features. Furthermore, "collaboration" might mean rethinking how systems include—or exclude—people. In these cases, "collaboration" takes the form of accessibility, inclusion, and shared use across diverse bodies and abilities.

Finally, it also refers to more institutionalized forms of organizing work, such as cooperatives and mutual aid networks. In these cases, "collaboration" can also mean participating in decision-making and in the horizontal governance of an evolving digital infrastructure.

This semester centers on two main projects. The first is a 10-week participatory design project developed in collaboration with an organization at RPI. You'll work directly with this organization, grounding your design process in real relationships, needs, and feedback. The second project is the first iteration of your portfolio. You'll revisit selected projects from Studio I and II, refining and developing them to a more advanced level—in execution, veneer, and how you frame your work as a designer.

Come in curious, open, and ready to craft design solutions collectively. Let's get started.

## Course Goals

### Goals:

**LEARNING OUTCOMES**

The learning outcomes that I expect all students to achieve by the end of this studio, are as follows:

- **1)** **Successfully combining engineering design methods, formal design processes learned in previous studio classes, and their critical deconstruction in ways that are useful to the student design practice.**

- This course focuses on introducing you to a more structured design process grounded in engineering practice, including specific design methods found in that field. These methods are used to support problem formulation, concept generation, evaluation, audit, iteration, and implementation, in addition to project management.
- However, unlike what RPI’s engineering students encounter in IED (Introduction to Engineering Design), we teach you to reflect on and criticize these methods in order to develop your approach to design, one that must nevertheless remain viable within the design profession.

- **2)** **Continue developing the capacity to analyze design problem as involving** **complex social systems.**

- Identifying and defining systemic problems and developing meaningful solutions will be part of our evaluation.

- **3)** **Demonstrating skills in prototyping, making and fabrication, evaluation, and design aesthetics, as consistent with a second-year design student.**

- We expect all students to acquire, at a basic level, practical skills in drawing, CAD, 3D printing, and woodworking. In addition, you are expected to develop or advance your making skills in at least one other area (e.g. metalworking, embedded controls, textiles, design software). You are expected to use these skills to design and build meaningful prototypes that contribute directly to your design efforts.
- Your ability to describe and evaluate a prototype in writing and in verbal form will also be evaluated as part of this learning outcome. Finally, we also expect you to begin developing and refining your aesthetic sensibility in a way that is consistent with your own career goals and will be demonstrate this through a curated portfolio.

- **4)** **Developing your design identity in ways that include capacity to engage with STS that characterize this program.**

- o DIS is a social science-based design program that incorporates the unique perspectives of STS. While our program provides you with considerable flexibility in forging your own identity as a designer, this in turn incurs the need for you to also develop the capacity to reflect upon, forge, and re-present this identity within the world of design. This is a critical part of the DIS degree program.
- o You should have begun this work already especially in Studio II; now, in Studio III you will be given an opportunity to extend the identity work you’ve begun, while also expanding your capacity to incorporate STS and social science perspectives into your design projects.

5. **Demonstrating effective group work and other communication and professional skill sets.**

- o Most designers work in a social setting, and must work well with clients, end-users, businesspeople, and others. Again, we will be conducting our class as a simulated professional design studio and expect professional conduct throughout.
- o Selected exercises that you do—preparation of portfolio, written reports, and oral presentation—as well as your contribution to group work and the class will be assessed, more or less individually, in constituting this final component of your grade.

## Grading Criteria

### Criteria Details:

As mentioned in our class agreements above, your Engagement grade is not merely an attendance grade. It constitutes 12 points of your grade because I expect you to attend and actively engage with the material. To succeed in this class requires diligence in participation and engagement. It doesn’t only mean “speak” when we are conducting our synchronous discussions; it also means actively contributing to our space in Blackboard by sharing your thoughts on readings and uploading pre-lecture comments on readings in a timely manner.

You Engagement in the course will be graded on a scale for engagement, detailed below.

- Excellent (12): You have submitted eight or more pre-lecture comments on readings. You consistently contribute to class, referencing readings, past class discussions, and knowledge you bring from other spaces. You are respectful and encourage your colleagues to participate, asking them clarifying and engaging questions, and providing feedback on others’ projects. You submit project weekly homework diligently and in a timely fashion.

- Good (9): You have submitted eight or more pre-lecture comments on readings. You are present and prepared for class and engage sporadically with some references to readings and past class discussions. You provide some feedback to your colleagues’ projects but could engage in our conversations more actively. You mostly submit weekly project homework in a timely fashion.

- Adequate (6): You have submitted eight or more pre-lecture comments on readings. You are present and prepared for class, but your contributions are limited, with questions and comments often confined to clarifications or superficial observations about the readings. You rarely engage in Q&A for your colleagues’ presentations. You are inconsistent in submitting your weekly project homework.

- Minimal (3): You have submitted less than eight pre-lecture comments on readings. You are often unprepared for class or do not contribute, or contribute with single comments in discussions (i.e., “I agree with them.) Your weekly project homework is rarely submitted or is done sloppily.

- None (0): You have submitted less than 4 pre-lecture comments on readings, are unprepared for class, and do not contribute to discussions. Your weekly project homework is rarely done.

Academic Integrity

The Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities and The Rensselaer Graduate Student Supplement define various forms of Academic Dishonesty and procedures for responding to them. All forms are violations of the trust between students and teachers. Student-teacher relationships are built on trust. For example, students must trust that teachers have made appropriate decisions about the structure and content of the courses they teach, and teachers must trust that the assignments students submit are their own work. Acts that violate this trust undermine the educational process.

The Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities and The Rensselaer Graduate Student Supplement define various forms of Academic Dishonesty, and you should make yourself familiar with these. In this class, all assignments submitted for a grade must represent the student’s own work. In cases where help was received or teamwork was allowed, a notation on the assignment should indicate your collaboration. Submission of any assignment that violates this policy will result in a penalty. If found in violation of the academic honesty policy, students may be subject to two types of penalties. The instructor administers an academic grade penalty, and the student is reported to the Dean of Students or the Dean of Graduate Education as appropriate. The first violation results in a 0 grade for that assignment. The second violation results in failure of the course. If you have any questions regarding this policy before submitting an assignment, please request clarification.
