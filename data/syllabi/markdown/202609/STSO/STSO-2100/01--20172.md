### Additional Information for Course Information Section:

This course is designed to provide you with a basic understanding of qualitative social research, which you can apply in your chosen field of study. You will gain hands-on experience with data collection methods such as interviews and participant observation, and you will practice analyzing data such as historical documents, interview transcripts, and cultural texts. We will look closely at questions of ethics, politics, and social justice in the research process. I have designed the course to be interactive, with the idea that students learn best by doing. For that reason, the course is structured in a “flipped” manner, meaning that class time is mainly dedicated to working on exercises. I expect you to gain necessary background knowledge through reading the assigned texts outside of class.

## Unit 1: Advanced People Watching (8/28-9/8)

An essential feature of qualitative research is the practice of *noticing* what other people don’t consciously register. There are many good strategies for accomplishing this, which we will work on together this semester. In this first unit of course work, you will observe a public space on campus or connected to student life (e.g. sporting events, the dining hall, public performance events at EMPAC, Vasudha garden, Folsom Library, the student union, etc.).

KEY QUESTION

What do you notice when you observe a social setting with the eyes of an alien explorer?

GRADED ASSIGNMENT

Unobtrusive Qualitative Observation (500-1000 words) – Due by 12 pm on 9/8/26

An essential feature of qualitative research is the practice of *noticing* what other people don’t consciously register. In the assigned readings, “Body Ritual Among the Nacirema” and “Motel of the Mysteries,” an observer looks at mundane aspects of American life with the eyes of an alien explorer. In each of these “studies,” there are many humorous misinterpretations, but they produce a sense of strangeness that allows us to look at ourselves from a new perspective.

In this assignment, you will seek to achieve a similar sense of strangeness, while observing mundane everyday life at RPI. You will unobtrusively observe other people on campus for a minimum of one hour and write an analysis of what you see, hear, smell, and feel. Further instructions will be provided.

## Unit 2: The Purpose and Ethics of Qualitative Research (9/11-9/29)

This unit is an introduction to qualitative research, with an emphasis on the unique ethical challenges that can arise when collecting and using qualitative data about people. We will consider how anthropologists helped colonial rulers and how sociologists invaded people’s privacy, among other topics. We will ask how our own experience and positionality affect the knowledge that we produce. And we will examine the history and purpose of Institutional Review Boards.

**Note: Class work will be asynchronous on 9/25.**

KEY QUESTIONS

What is qualitative research? What is it not?

How can qualitative research be unethical? How can it serve powerful interests?

What mechanisms exist to ensure that research is conducted ethically?

REQUIRED READINGS

“Research Adventures on Indigenous Lands”

“Tearoom Trade: The Ethics of Studying Social Problems”

Chapter 6 (Reflexivity)

Chapter 7 (Ethics)

Chapter 8 (Working with IRBs)

GRADED ASSIGNMENT

Propose a Research Topic (500-1000 words) – Due by 12 pm on 9/29/26

In this short paper, you will describe a research topic that is relevant to your academic, personal, and/or career interests. Choose the topic in consultation with your classmates and instructor. The proposed topic must be something that can be examined using qualitative research methods, such as observation, interviews, or analysis of archives, but at this stage you do not need to know exactly which methods you would use. The paper must 1) explain the topic, 2) explain your aims and goals (personal/political, practical/strategic, professional/academic), 3) begin to define your research question, and 4) reflect on how the topic could be studied ethically and in alignment with your values.

## Unit 3: Building a Team of Knowledgeable Sources (10/2-10/23

In this unit, we will start to ask research questions and practice a research approach that focuses on *sources* (people) rather than free-floating information. We will apply this approach to a literature search. We will also consider methods of *sampling* primary sources that are commonly used in qualitative research. In the final part of this unit, we will dig into some of the underlying principles of qualitative research.

**Note: Class work will be asynchronous on 10/9.**

KEY QUESTIONS

What are the best ways to access and evaluate previous studies on a topic?

What kinds of primary sources do qualitative researchers use and how do they select them?

How do we know whether our findings represent reality?

REQUIRED READINGS

Chapter 9 (Reviewing the Literature)

Chapter 5 (Sampling)

Chapter 2 (Research Design)

Chapter 3 (Epistemology)

GRADED ASSIGNMENT

Select Research Sources (500-1000 words) – Due by 12 pm on 10/23

For this assignment, you will identify a minimum of ten people who are credible sources on the topic that you chose to investigate. Your paper should take the form of an alphabetical list of individuals, each followed by a short explanation of why you would consult them as a source. Good explanations include details like the person’s academic degree and directly relevant peer-reviewed publications that they have written. If a source on your list is not an academic researcher, a good explanation of why you would consult them as a source should include their relevant personal or professional experience with the topic.

## Unit 4: Analyzing Existing Qualitative Data (10/27 -11/10)

In this unit, we will practice some of the most important methods of data analysis used in qualitative research. This will include working with archives and historical documents, conducting *content analysis* on cultural texts, and *coding* transcripts of interviews.

KEY QUESTIONS

What does it mean to *analyze* qualitative information?

What techniques can be used to identify themes and patterns in qualitative data?

How does data analysis connect to *theories* about society?

REQUIRED READINGS

Chapter 16 (Archival and Historical Research)

Chapter 17 (Content Analysis)

Chapter 18 (Data Analysis and Coding)

Chapter 19 (Advanced Codes and Coding)

GRADED ASSIGNMENT

Analyze Documents (500-1000 words) – Due by 12 pm on 11/10

In this paper, you will report the results of preliminary qualitative analysis of *existing data* relating to your research topic. Think broadly and creatively about existing data that you can access. It could include university archives, news media content, visual culture such as objects and signs, published interviews, observations in public places, website content, official documents, etc. In this paper you must concisely explain your method of analysis and report your key findings with sufficient evidence to convince the reader that your interpretations are correct.

## Unit 5: Producing Original Qualitative Data (11/13-12/4)

In this unit, we will practice some of the most important methods of data collection used in qualitative research. These include in-depth interviewing, conducting focus groups, participant observation, and deep ethnography. Because we would need ethics approval to collect original data, we will conduct simulations in class. We will also learn more about these methods by studying research reports by scholars who have employed these methods effectively.

KEY QUESTIONS

What techniques can be used to gather information from individuals about their inner lives and experiences in society?

What techniques can be used to gain understanding of cultures and social situations?

What kinds of documentation do qualitative researchers use to transform observations into data?

REQUIRED READINGS

Chapter 10 (Intro to Data Collection)

Chapter 11 (Interviewing)

Chapter 12 (Focus Groups)

Chapter 13 (Participant Observation)

Chapter 14 (Deep Ethnography)

GRADED ASSIGNMENT

Ethnographic Fieldnote (1000-1500 words) – Due by 12 pm on 12/4

Collect primary qualitative data by conducting participant observation and writing an ethnographic fieldnote. Spend a few hours in a social space (such as a mall, coffee shop, family gathering, religious space, party) with the aim of producing a fieldnote that provides a thick description of the space. This fieldnote should include descriptions of the space, individuals, social interactions as well as your reflections. The guiding motivation of the fieldnote should be to transport the reader to the social setting with all its details. It is recommended, though not required, that you do this assignment during the Thanksgiving holiday.

**Unit 6: Writing a Proposal (12/8-12/11 + finals week presentations)**

In the final week of class, you will use class time to assemble your sources, document analysis, and ideas about research design into a short research proposal. Then, during finals week, you will give a presentation of your research proposal, answer questions about it, and submit a PDF of your slides.

GRADED ASSIGNMENT

Research Proposal (10 minutes of oral presentation + Q&A) – Finals Week, date TBA

Your objective is to develop a proposal for a research project on the topic that you have been exploring all semester. The proposed research must use qualitative research methods, since the purpose of this assignment is to demonstrate your understanding of the key ideas of this course. Your presentation must have four main sections and conclude with a works cited slide. You have a maximum of 10 minutes to give the presentation.

1. purpose and research question
2. literature review – a brief assessment of prior research on your topic
3. preliminary analysis of existing data – revisit Unit 4 assignment
4. research plan – describe the steps of collecting and analyzing data (interviews, ethnography, archival research) and state your expectations about what you may find
5. works cited – you must cite any studies discussed in your literature review, as well as any other sources used throughout the proposal, such as newspaper articles, websites, and course readings.

A grading rubric will be provided for this assignment. A portion of your grade will be based on your ability to answer questions about your proposal in a way that demonstrates both original thought and understanding of course material.

# Discussion Strategies

A good portion of our class meetings will focus on student discussion. To make this effective for everyone, I ask everyone to adhere to the following guidelines.

· Your contribution might be a question; you don’t have to be certain about an idea to make a meaningful contribution to the conversation.

· Talk to each other, not just to the discussion leader or professor.

· Listen and respond to other statements before introducing a new idea.

· Ask questions if you do not understand what someone has said, or you can paraphrase what another student has said for clarification (“I think you said this; is that right?”).

· Refer to evidence from the text to support your ideas.

· Pay attention to your “airtime”—how much you have spoken in relation to other students.

· Don’t interrupt.

# Inclusive Language

The Department of Science and Technology Studies aspires to create a learning environment in which people of all identities are encouraged to contribute their perspectives to academic discourse. This course has guidelines on language and terminology that aim to foster an inclusive and welcoming environment, strengthen academic writing, enrich discussion, and reflect best professional practices.

Gender-inclusive and non-sexist language: Language is gender-inclusive and non-sexist when we use words that affirm and respect how people describe, express, and experience their gender. Gender-inclusive/non-sexist language acknowledges people of any gender (e.g. first-year student versus freshman, chair versus chairman, humankind versus mankind). It also affirms non-binary gender identifications and recognizes the difference between biological sex and gender expression. Students may share their preferred pronouns and names, and these gender identities and gender expressions should be honored.

Non-racist language: The labels and definitions of racial and ethnic categories vary across history, societies, and even situations. In this classroom, we will avoid racial and ethnic slurs and strive to use words that affirm and respect how people describe, express, and experience their race, ethnicity, ancestry, or cultural heritage. Students may share the terminology that they prefer to describe their racial and ethnic identities, and these preferences should be honored.

Non-ableist language: Ableist language, which devalues people with physical and mental disabilities, permeates everyday language. However, there are many options for replacing common ableist rhetoric with more thoughtful alternatives. You are encouraged to read this guide and reflect on what you are communicating when you use ableist terms. https://www.autistichoya.com/p/ableist-words-and-terms-to-avoid.html

# Sexual Misconduct and Title IX

Title IX is a federal law prohibiting sex discrimination. The Title IX section of the Education Amendments Act of 1972 states, “No person in the United States shall, on the basis of sex, be excluded from participation in, be denied the benefits of, or be subjected to discrimination under any education program or activity receiving Federal financial assistance.” Under Title IX, sexual misconduct is considered a form of sex discrimination.

Sexual misconduct includes, but is not limited to: sexual harassment, sexual assault, dating and domestic violence, intimate partner violence, and stalking. Sexual misconduct is not tolerated at Rensselaer, and it is a violation of Institute policy, and state and federal law.

Faculty and other employees are considered mandated reporters when it comes to experiences of interpersonal violence (sexual assault, sexual harassment, dating or domestic violence, and stalking). Disclosures of interpersonal violence must be passed along to Rensselaer’s Title IX Coordinators who can help provide support and academic remedies for students who have been impacted. For more information or to report sexual misconduct, see https://sexualviolence.rpi.edu/.
