## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

This course develops quantum many-body theory from first principles, with an emphasis on connecting fundamental concepts to modern computational methods. Topics progress from the Schrödinger equation, hydrogen atom, and spin to Hartree–Fock and density functional theory, followed by symmetry, basis functions, second quantization, configuration interaction, perturbation theory, coupled cluster theory, and modern compression techniques used in scalable electronic-structure calculations.

The course is derivation-focused and mathematically rigorous, while remaining accessible to students without prior training in quantum mechanics. Students should be prepared to encounter mathematical tools from several areas and to independently review unfamiliar techniques when necessary.

## Course Text(s)

## Course Goals

### Goals:

By the end of the course, students should be able to:

- Develop a first-principles understanding of the fundamental concepts underlying quantum many-body theory.
- Derive and interpret key quantum-mechanical equations and approximations used in electronic-structure theory.
- Understand the theoretical foundations of Hartree–Fock, density functional theory, configuration interaction, perturbation theory, and coupled cluster theory.
- Use second quantization, symmetry, basis-set representations, and contraction techniques to formulate many-body problems.
- Compare major quantum many-body methods in terms of their approximations, accuracy, computational cost, and scalability.
- Understand modern compression and computational techniques used to make high-accuracy many-body methods applicable to larger systems.
- Develop the mathematical maturity needed to read, derive, and critically evaluate contemporary work in computational quantum many-body theory.

## Course Content

### Content Details:

The course will cover the following topics:

- Introduction to quantum many-body theory
- Wave–particle duality and the Schrödinger equation
- The hydrogen atom
- Quantum spin
- Hartree–Fock theory
- Density functional theory (DFT)
- Symmetries in quantum mechanics
- Basis-set representations, including Gaussian-type orbitals (GTOs) and plane-wave basis sets
- Second quantization
- Full configuration interaction (FCI), selected configuration interaction (SCI), and configuration interaction with singles and doubles (CISD)
- Møller–Plesset perturbation theory (MP)
- Coupled cluster (CC) theory
- Electron-repulsion integral (ERI) compression techniques
- Fast and scalable implementations of Hartree–Fock and MP2
- Modern compression techniques for scalable quantum many-body calculations

The course progresses from foundational concepts in quantum mechanics to contemporary many-body methods and computational techniques used in state-of-the-art electronic-structure calculations.

## Learning Outcomes

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes (CLOs):

- Explain the fundamental principles of quantum mechanics relevant to many-body systems, including wave–particle duality, the Schrödinger equation, and spin.
- Derive the central equations underlying major electronic-structure methods, including Hartree–Fock, configuration interaction, Møller–Plesset perturbation theory, and coupled cluster theory.
- Apply second-quantization and contraction techniques to formulate and manipulate quantum many-body problems.
- Analyze the role of symmetry and basis-set representations, including Gaussian-type orbitals and plane waves, in quantum many-body calculations.
- Compare and evaluate common many-body methods in terms of their approximations, accuracy, computational cost, and applicability.
- Explain the fundamental ideas underlying density functional theory and contrast density-based and wave-function-based approaches.
- Identify and analyze computational bottlenecks in electronic-structure calculations and explain how compression and related numerical techniques can improve scalability.
- Integrate mathematical tools from linear algebra, differential equations, Fourier analysis, and related areas to derive and interpret results in quantum many-body theory.

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes Assessment Measures:

Student achievement of the course learning outcomes will be assessed primarily through **interactive Jupyter notebook assignments and short quizzes**.

Throughout the semester, students will receive interactive Jupyter notebooks designed to reinforce the concepts, derivations, and computational methods introduced in class. Students will work through these notebooks independently and submit them according to the course schedule.

At the time each notebook is due, students will complete a short, approximately **five-minute quiz** assessing their understanding of the fundamental concepts addressed in that notebook. The notebooks will provide opportunities for guided application and exploration, while the quizzes will assess each student's individual understanding of the underlying theory and methods.

Together, these assessments will measure students' ability to understand and apply the fundamental concepts of quantum many-body theory, connect mathematical derivations with computational implementations, and interpret the behavior and limitations of the methods studied in the course.

### Additional Academic Integrity Course Policy and Penalty Information:

Students are encouraged to discuss course concepts and work collaboratively when completing the interactive Jupyter notebooks. Collaboration should support learning and understanding; however, each student is responsible for completing and submitting their own work and for being able to explain the reasoning, derivations, and computational results contained in their submission. Copying another student's work, code, or written responses without appropriate acknowledgment is not permitted.

The short quizzes associated with the notebooks are **individual assessments**. Collaboration, communication with other students, or use of unauthorized resources during a quiz is prohibited.

Plagiarism, unauthorized copying, falsification of results, or representing another person's work as one's own constitutes a violation of academic integrity. Students are expected to comply with all Institute policies regarding academic integrity.

For a **first academic integrity violation**, the student will receive a grade of zero on the affected notebook, quiz, or assessment, and the incident may be reported in accordance with Institute procedures. For a **subsequent violation**, the student may receive a grade of F for the course, subject to applicable Institute policies and procedures.

Students who are uncertain about whether a particular form of collaboration or use of an external resource is permitted should ask the instructor before submitting the work.
