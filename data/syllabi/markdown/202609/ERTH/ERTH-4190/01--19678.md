## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

Modern methods used in the analysis of environmental samples for monitoring and research purposes. This course will focus on field and laboratory techniques for assessing water quality, including the analysis of total suspended solids, fecal coliforms, pH, alkalinity, light, temperature, dissolved oxygen, chlorine, turbidity, dissolved phosphorous, nitrogen, and organic carbon, and other parameters if time permits. This course will also cover practical laboratory and fieldwork skills, including calibration, titration, sample collection and preservation, and deployment of in situ sensors and multiprobes. This course emphasizes the application of field sampling and data collection to better understand the natural and built environment. This is an immersive and interactive course, where roughly half of class periods will be spent in the field and the other half of class periods will be spent in the laboratory.

## Learning Outcomes

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes (CLOs):

- Use a lab notebook and construct lab reports to retain and evaluate environmental observations
- Calibration instrumentation for measurement of unknown samples
- Conduct fieldwork activities while adhering to leave no trace principles
- Deploy in situ sensors and multiprobes for the collection and interpretation of continuous data
- Recover, analyze, and preserve grab samples for subsequent laboratory-based analyses
- Demonstrate proficiency in various laboratory and instrumental techniques for assessing water quality
- Synthesize analytical data to understand the overall condition and ecological health of local waterways

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes Assessment Measures:

Student progress will be evaluated based upon the following:

**ACTIVE PARTICIPATION** – This course is hands-on and 100% in person. To get the most out of this course, it is critical that you attend each class in person and actively participate. Roughly half of class periods will be spent in the field, where you will get wet, sweaty, and dirty. The other half of class periods will be spent in the laboratory, where you will be handling samples, chemicals, and using various analytical instrumentation. You are expected to participate in all of these course-related activities. If you happen to miss a class, you are responsible for obtaining any material, announcements, or assignments covered during the class you missed. The active participation grade (90 total points; max 30 points per category) will **comprise 25% of your final course grade** and will be determined as follows:

| **Active participation rubric –** *Score ranges* | **Never/Rarely** | **Sometimes** | **Often/Always** |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Engagement** – Contributes to group discussions; listens to and engages in ideas raised by peers | 0 – 10 | 11 – 20 | 21 – 30 |
| **Attendance** – Attends class regularly; arrives on time and in the correct location | 0 – 10 | 11 – 20 | 21 – 30 |
| **Preparation** – Comes to each class fully prepared for scheduled field, lab, and classroom activities | 0 – 10 | 11 – 20 | 21 – 30 |

**WRITTEN ASSIGNMENTS** – Nearly all written assignments will be typed and submitted via Gradescope and include lab reports, data, and observations. The length, degree of detail, and format of each lab report will vary depending upon the research activity. Examples of what may be included in a lab report include: objectives and hypotheses, graphs and data summaries, statistical analysis, interpretation of data, and describing study limitations. Lab reports and other assignments will be written and submitted independently, not as a group. The weighted average written assignment grade will **comprise 40% of your final course grade**.

**LAB NOTEBOOK** – As part of this course, you will learn how to keep a lab notebook as a useful reference for yourself and others. While there is no one “correct” way to keep a lab notebook, efficient and effective notetaking is an essential part of conducting well-rounded and robust environmental research. At the end of the semester, you will be graded on your effectiveness in keeping a lab notebook. The lab notebook will be submitted to Prof. Wagner near the end of the semester. The lab notebook grade (50 total points; max 30 points for Content; max 10 points each for Organization and Readability) will **comprise 5% of your final course grade** and will be determined as follows:

| **Lab notebook rubric –** *Score ranges* | **Never/Rarely** | **Sometimes** | **Often/Always** |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Content** – Description of research activities are sufficient to accurately recall what was done; may include new ideas, unusual events/mistakes, sample info, preliminary data, etc. | 0 – 10 | 11 – 20 | 21 – 30 |
| **Organization** – Content is organized in a logical manner where relevant information can be looked up fairly quickly and easily | 0 – 2 | 3 – 6 | 7 – 10 |
| **Readability** – Handwriting is legible; grammar is sufficient to understand and follow key points | 0 – 2 | 3 – 6 | 7 – 10 |

**INDEPENDENT FIELD STUDY –** Students will take what they have learned over the course of the semester to formulate and carry out an independent field study. Each student will devise or select their own research objective and hypotheses, sampling plan, and analytical methods (from a list of methods covered in this course). *This is your opportunity to be creative!* Once all data is collected, each student will summarize and interpret the results, describe their findings, draw relevant conclusions, and describe study limitations. On the last day of class, students will present their field study in the form of a short PowerPoint presentation and submit a formal written report via Gradescope. Further instructions on the formal report and presentation will be provided later in the semester. The final field study grade (40% presentation; 60% formal report) will **comprise 30% of your final course grade**.

## Policies

### Other Course Policies:

**Attendance and tardiness** – Due to the hands-on and immersive nature of this course, it is essential that you arrive on time and prepared each day. Since most of this course takes place in the teaching lab or in the field, we will not be in the classroom most days. *If you expect to be late or absent, you must email, Webex, call, or text Prof. Wagner by 9am that day* (email address and cell phone number are listed at the top of the syllabus). If you call and Prof. Wagner does not answer immediately, please leave a voicemail or send a text instead. If you are late, we will not wait for you, but we can tell you where we will be if you are able to drive or find a ride to the field site on your own. Repeatedly being late or absent will impact your active participation grade (see above) and could impact other components of your grade since being present for field and lab work are essential to the course learning objectives.

**Late policy** – Many assignments in this course require group work and data sharing, so submission of assignments in a timely manner is essential for collaborative success and efficient grading by the instructor. Should the student encounter a severe, unexpected situation (e.g., illness, family emergency, etc.) in which it will not be possible to submit an assignment on time, they must email the instructor at least 24 hours in advance so appropriate accommodations can be made. If the student notifies the instructor less than 24 hours before the submission deadline or not at all, then the assignment will incur a grade penalty (25% drop in grade per day late), after 3 days late, the assignment will receive a grade of zero. Remember that submitting something incomplete or imperfect is often better than submitting nothing at all. ***I will be strictly adhering to this late policy.***

**Accommodations** – If you require special accommodation (e.g., learning challenges, religious holiday conflicts, planned classes you will be missing), please schedule a meeting with Prof. Wagner during the first week of class. If you have concerns about fieldwork (see “lab and field safety” below), please contact Prof. Wagner as soon as possible.

**Lab and field safety** – On days when we plan to be in the lab, you must wear closed-toe shoes to protect against spills and broken glassware. On days when we plan to be in the field, expect to get sweaty, wet, and dirty – There is also a good chance that we will be walking on uneven ground, in muddy areas, and/or wading into shallow water. Please dress accordingly and in a manner that is most comfortable for you! On field days, it is recommended that you wear shorts or pants that can be rolled up, and water-compatible boots/shoes/sandals. For example, Prof. Wagner tends to wear comfy summer clothes that can get wet/dirty and sandals with secure straps around the foot and heel because she doesn’t mind getting wet, they protect the bottom of her feet, and stay on easily. We do have a few pairs of knee-high rubber boots and waders that students may use during sampling. You are welcome to bring a back-up change of shoes or clothes if you desire. You must bring your own extra supplies, like water, snacks, sunscreen, bug spray, etc.
