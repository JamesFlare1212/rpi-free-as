## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

This course will introduce students to techniques used in modern observational astronomy research. We will practice these techniques on existing datatsets and obtain our own data with the Hirsch Observatory 16” telescope. Students will carry out their own small research projects and produce reports in an astronomy journal format. After taking this class, students will be prepared to carry out undergraduate or graduate research projects in observational astronomy. In particular, they will be able to reduce simple optical CCD images and spectra, and extract data from common on-line databases. They will have a working knowledge of LaTex, AstroPy and Stellarium. Laptops are required.

## Course Text(s)

## Learning Outcomes

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes (CLOs):

- Students will be able to compose a descriptive astronomy-style research report in LaTex, with graphs created using Python.
- Students will be able to describe optical telescopes and detectors using common vocabulary.
- Students will be able to interpret the reliability of data using statistical techniques.
- Students will understand coordinate systems and observational constraints, allowing them to plan astronomical observations.
- Students will be able to generate imaging data using a telescope and CCD camera.
- Students will be able to correct raw astronomical data (images and spectroscopy) using standard analysis techniques in AstroPy.
- Students will be able to calibrate photometric and spectroscopic data from optical telescopes.
- Students will be able to query on-line astronomical databases using SQL.

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes Assessment Measures:

Students' performance will be evaluated based on: attendance, homework, take-home projects, in-class projects, and a final project.

Homework, projects, and final projects will be due according to the timeline below. Students are encouraged to discuss homework assignments with each other, but the work that is turned in must be the student’s original work. ***Submitted homework must clearly show the steps leading up to each answer. **Data-taking for observational projects should be done with a partner**. For project papers that are written by more than one student, each student must submit a separate page that describes what part of the work and what part of the writing they were responsible for. Requests for homework re-grades should first be made in writing to the teaching assistant. Prof. Zheng will decide grading in cases where the issue is not resolved with the TA. Prof. Zheng grades all projects herself, so complaints for those should be made in writing to her.

### Additional Academic Integrity Course Policy and Penalty Information:

**Academic Dishonesty:** Students who cheat will receive a failing grade for the course. Cheating includes: copying, or allowing another student to copy, a test or assignment; altering a graded test or assignment before requesting regrading; copying submitted solutions from published solution manuals or previous years; using materials other than those that are allowed on an exam; misrepresenting the work of another group or individual as your own. It is considered cheating to help another student cheat. ***It is imperative in your written projects that you do not copy text or images from the web or other sources without attribution, and that you do not misrepresent your collected data. In particular, it is not allowed to use anything generated by AI/Chatbots in any submitted assignment.***

The environment in which students operate has many sources of solutions to homework and projects, including AI chatbots, online search, Chegg, student-organized archives, classmates, and other people. It is okay for a homework/project to consult any of these sources for ideas, information, and to check your work. However, **the work you actually turn in must be 100% your own**. You must show how and why each step in the homework solution is done, and you must have generated all the text, plots, data, and tables in the assignments yourself. A good test to determine whether you are within the course rules is to ask yourself whether your actions promote learning, whether you are learning the information and skills that the course is designed to teach you, and whether everything that you turn in is your own work.
