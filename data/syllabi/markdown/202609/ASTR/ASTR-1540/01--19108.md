## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

An introduction to astronomy and planetary science from an observational perspective. Students will learn the basics of observing the night-time sky, both with the unaided eye and through telescopic observation. Observations of Earth from space, the exploration of our Solar System, and the quest to find Earth-like planets in other solar systems will also be discussed. The course is suitable for non-physics and non-science majors.

## Course Goals

### Goals:

The course is designed to provide an introduction to the science of astronomy from an observational perspective at a level suitable for non-science majors. Students with a more specialized interest in astronomy may wish to consider taking the equivalent upper-level class ASTR 4120 Observational Astronomy (taught in the Fall semester).

## Course Content

### Content Details:

**General Knowledge and Current Phenomena:** A weekly discussion of the current sky and topical news items in Astronomy, Space and Earth Science. Planetarium software such as Stellarium will be used to illustrate the night sky.  
**Basics of Astronomical Observation:** Radiation and the electromagnetic spectrum; sources of radiation; the sky at different wavelengths; naked-eye astronomy; the Celestial Sphere; constellations and Messier objects; astronomical co-ordinate systems; the seasons; observing the Sun, Moon and planets; time keeping.  
**Telescopes and Observatories:** Optical telescope designs – refractors, reflectors, composites; the effect of Earth's atmosphere on astronomical observations; adaptive optics; site selection for ground-based optical observatories; ground-based versus airborne and space observatories; plans for the future.  
**Observational Astronomy:** Detectors for different wavelengths; imaging and spectroscopy; the Doppler effect; radio telescopes and receivers. Applications of these techniques in stellar and galactic astronomy; the stellar magnitude system; fundamental properties of stars; stellar evolution; the Sun as a star; the astronomical distance scale; galaxies and dark matter.  
**The Earth and its Environment:** The Earth as a planet; observing the Earth from space – images, spectra, topography. Applications of satellite technology – communication systems, remote sensing methods and applications in Earth and environmental sciences; the ozone hole; global warming; environmental surveillance.  
**Exploring our Solar System:** The nature and origin of our Solar System; processes that shape rocky planetary bodies; Mercury, Venus, the Moon and Mars – similarities and differences compared with Earth. The giant planets and their moons. Latest result from NASA missions such as New Horizons and Juno.  
**Exoplanets and the Quest for Life:** The circumstellar habitable zone and limits to life. Exoplanets – detection and study from Kepler mission. Comparisons between our Solar System and exoplanetary systems; the search for Earth-like planets in the habitable zones of other stars. The Drake Equation. Strategies for detecting life: microbial biosignatures; the search for extraterrestrial intelligence.

## Learning Outcomes

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes (CLOs):

- The student will gain familiarity with the night time sky and learn the basics of astronomical observation with the unaided eye and with optical telescopes.
- The student will gain an appreciation of the significance of astronomy, and insight into the synergy that exists between observational methods and scientific discovery that leads to advances in our understanding of the cosmos.
- The student will gain insight into the Earth from an astronomical perspective and will discover what can be learned about the Earth by studying it from space and how studies of the Earth as a planet inform our search for life on other bodies in our Solar System and beyond.

### Additional Academic Integrity Course Policy and Penalty Information:

When you place your name on a submitted piece of work, you are claiming that you are the original author of that work, excepting appropriately cited material. If you turn in work that is not your own, you are stealing credit for that work from the original author. Additionally, you are ignoring the steps that the instructor feels are necessary for you to understand the material, and interfering with a fair assessment of your abilities.  
**Please always produce honest, original work.** If you turn in dishonest work, you are implicitly undermining your education and the system of fairness that exists.

When using public sources of information such as books, papers and the internet, you must incorporate that information into your own discussion (with citation) rather than merely cut and paste. We have means to detect submissions that do not comply with this policy.

For all your work, including the projects, you may use AI programs e.g., ChatGPT **ONLY** to help generate ideas and brainstorm. You should note that the material generated by these programs may be inaccurate, incomplete, or lack correct citation. Using these programs may also stifle your independent and creative thinking. You **MAY NOT** submit any work generated by an AI program as your own (this includes AI generated art). If you include any material submitted by an AI program it should be cited like any other reference, but it will not be considered your own original work.

**Detection of plagiarism on projects (which includes the submission of AI generated content as your own) or proof of cheating during an Exam will result in a 0 on the assignment and possible failing grade in this course.**

Note that students have failed this course in the past as the result of such infringements. If you have any questions concerning these policies, please ask for clarification.
