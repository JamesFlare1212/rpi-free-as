## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

WRIT 2110 is an undergraduate course designed to help students become more self-possessed  
and versatile writers. It offers instruction in linguistic, rhetorical, and sociocultural concepts  
through an overarching focus on language: how it works, where it falls short, and how to harness  
it for specific purposes. Critical thinking and composing are treated as processes, which includes  
the creation, development, organization, and revision of ideas and arguments.  
What distinguishes this section is its structure. Rather than assigning the same sequence of papers  
to everyone, this course is built around choice. In the first two weeks you will articulate who you  
are, what you want, and what problems you care about. That statement becomes the anchor for  
every writing project you select for the rest of the semester. You will analyze genres before you  
write in them, justify why each genre serves your goals, and finish the term with a body of work  
that belongs to you rather than to the instructor's syllabus.  
Throughout the semester, working professionals from technology, the sciences, game  
development, marketing, public service, local business, and publishing will visit the class to  
describe how writing and communication actually function in their work

## Course Goals

### Goals:

This course intends to teach you to:  
• Recognize a wide range of strategic modes of communication and respond to them through  
meaningful critical inquiry, formulating both questions and answers.  
• Assess an unfamiliar writing task and plan an approach to it, including free-writing,  
brainstorming, source assessment, outlining, drafting, and revising in response to feedback.  
• Identify and implement specific writing strategies suited to a particular audience and purpose.  
• Organize written and oral work that argues, compares, defines, describes, or analyzes  
convincingly, with attention to tone, voice, genre, and style.  
• Develop a scholarly and personal voice through practice, class work, and reflection.  
• Explain your own rhetorical strategies, anticipating the questions and concerns an audience  
may raise

## Course Content

### Content Details:

The course covers the strategies named in the Institute course description:  
analyzing cause and effects · argumentation · compare and contrast · defining · describing · using  
dialogue, citation, and incorporating sources · narrating · explaining processes · drafting and  
revision · presentation and convincing an in-person audience  
Rather than treating each as a separate weekly unit, this section teaches these strategies as moves  
that particular genres make. You encounter them by analyzing genres that use them and by  
writing in genres you have chosen. The lecture and workshop sequence moves through five arcs:  
Foundations (Weeks 1–2). Identity and goals; the rhetorical situation; ethos, pathos, and  
logos; nine domains of writing and six functions of writing.  
• Genre literacy and research (Weeks 3–5). Conducting a genre analysis; reading like a  
writer; finding and evaluating examples; summary versus analysis; conventions versus  
creativity; integrating sources and citation methods across disciplines.  
• Style, structure, and design (Weeks 6–9). Plain language and jargon; the style debate;  
structure and organization across genres; principles of visual design; multimodal composition.  
• Voice, scale, and revision (Weeks 10–13). Tone, voice, and register; voice and identity in the  
age of artificial intelligence; micro-genres and macro-genres; revision as rhetorical decision-  
making.  
• Synthesis (Weeks 14–16). Writing for real-world problems; making and delivering  
presentations; transfer of writing skills across contexts.  
A running strand on style—parts of a sentence, clauses and phrases, punctuation, and word  
choice—threads through the whole semester and is practiced in the Sentence Log.

## Learning Outcomes

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes (CLOs):

- Analyze academic texts in order to cultivate an ability to recognize and adapt to the features of different genres and purposes in writing
- Understand and use written and spoken academic structures and conventions to produce texts for academic audiences
- Successfully engage in the processes of academic writing and presentations including reading, outlining, drafting, peer review and editing
- Find information from appropriate sources and integrate it into writing
- Successfully implement academic citation conventions and referencing

## Policies

### Attendance Policy:

Attendance Policy  
Attendance is mandatory and is an important factor in your success here. This course is unusually  
dependent on being in the room: from mid-October onward nearly every class period contains a  
workshop, a peer review session, or a visiting professional, and 120 points are earned through in-  
class work that cannot be reproduced afterward.  
You are granted four absences without penalty, no questions asked. This is not an allowance to  
plan around; it exists so that illness and unexpected personal matters do not damage your  
standing. Beyond four absences you begin losing the participation and in-class activity points  
attached to the days you miss. Missing more than seven class meetings means you cannot  
complete the requirements of this course; if you reach that point, speak with me and with your  
academic advisor about withdrawing rather than taking a failing grade.

  Student-teacher relationships are built on trust. For example, students must trust that teachers have made appropriate decisions about the structure and content of the courses they teach, and teachers must trust that the assignments that students turn in are their own. ### Additional Academic Integrity Course Policy and Penalty Information:

For plagiarism that is deemed unintentional (poor/lack of citation), the first violation will received a reduced score on the assignment. Further violations will result in a 0 grade for that assignment.

For plagiarism that is deemed intentional (copying someone else’s work), the first violation will result in a 0 grade for that assignment. The second violation results in failure of the course. If you have any questions concerning this policy before submitting an assignment, please ask for clarification.

Use of AI

AI programs such as Chat GPT may be used as an aid to your writing in this class for things such as but not limited to the following: help with choosing a topic, creating outlines, finding sources, making reference lists, and editing papers. If you use an AI tool, you should cite that tool and state at the end of your paper how you have used that tool. AI should never be used to write or translate your entire paper or large portions of your paper for you. This will result in a 0 grade for the assignment.
