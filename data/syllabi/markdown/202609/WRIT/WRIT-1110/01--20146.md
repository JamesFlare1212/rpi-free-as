## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

“Imagine that you enter a parlor. You come late. When you arrive, others have long preceded you,  
and they are engaged in a heated discussion, a discussion too heated for them to pause and tell you  
exactly what it is about… You listen for a while, until you decide that you have caught the tenor of the  
argument; then you put in your oar.”  
— Kenneth Burke, The Philosophy of Literary Form  
Every act of communication, written or spoken, is entering into an ongoing conversation. This course is  
organized to help you recognize that conversation and respond to it. Through reading, drafting, collaborating,  
revising, asking questions, and above all practicing, you will build a writing process you can rely on and a  
critical eye for the situations you write into.  
Over the semester you will write in four genres for four different audiences: a spoken personal narrative for the  
people in this room, a résumé and cover letter for an employer of your choosing, a researched problem/solution  
argument aimed at someone with the power to act on it, and a reflective portfolio addressed to yourself. Some  
of that writing will be formal and some informal, some assessed and some not, some polished and some  
deliberately messy. The genres will change, but the questions will not: Who is already talking about this? What  
am I adding? Who am I asking to listen, and what do I want them to do?  
The course carries a second inquiry, and it is about you: What are you good at? What do you want? What are  
you anxious about? And what do you owe the places you live in? Your first assignment asks you those  
questions. Your last assignment will ask you to read your own answers back and account for what changed.  
Over the course of this semester, you will come to understand communication not just as something you do,  
but as a mindful, ongoing negotiation between who you are, who you are becoming, and the world you inhabit.  
That is to say, this course is not only about writing, and not only about the work you will do at Rensselaer. It  
asks you to develop reading, writing, listening, and speaking practices you will need as a student, as a  
professional, and as a member of a community.  
One practical note: this is a workshop, not a lecture. You will write in nearly every class meeting, you will read  
your work aloud, and you will read your classmates’ work carefully enough to give useful feedback. This  
workshop asks you to show up fully, to practice often, to revise bravely, and to treat your developing voice as  
something worth cultivating.

## Course Goals

### Goals:

This course asks you to communicate with real audiences, in writing and aloud, toward genuinely different  
ends: to give an audience perspective on an experience, to present your abilities to someone deciding whether  
to work with you, and to propose a change that would improve a community you belong to. Each of those  
purposes demands something different from you, and learning to feel that difference is the point.  
To that end, the course sets out to teach you:  
● To read a rhetorical situation—genre, rhetor, audience, purpose, and exigence—and to use that  
reading to make deliberate choices rather than default ones.  
● To write in genres you have not written in before by studying working examples of each closely  
before attempting your own.  
● To build an argument out of claims, evidence, and stated reasoning, rather than assertion and  
accumulated quotation.  
● To conduct research in library databases and open sources, evaluate what you find, and enter an  
existing conversation—including by taking on sources that disagree with you.  
● To work recursively—to draft badly without concern, to take feedback without defending, and to  
revise substantively rather than cosmetically.  
● To design a document rather than merely fill one, treating page and screen layout, typography, images,  
and data displays as part of the argument instead of decoration.  
● To speak and to listen, adapting written work for a listening audience under a hard time limit, and  
attending to others’ work closely enough to be genuinely useful to them.  
● To give feedback specific enough to act on and to receive it the same way.  
● To move deliberately between registers, noticing the choices you make as a user of both Standard  
Academic English and the English you use everywhere else, and treating that as a range you command  
rather than a rule you keep failing.  
To weigh the ethics of what you write, what your sources are owed, what your audience is entitled to  
know, and what a piece of writing does in the world once it leaves you.  
Above all, the course intends to make you more confident. Writing and speaking get easier through repetition  
and almost no other way, which is why you will do both in nearly every class meeting

## Course Content

### Content Details:

The rhetorical situation · Reading, listening, and viewing rhetorically · Narrative structure and concrete  
language · Speaking from a written text · Audience analysis in professional genres · Document and visual  
design · Ethos, pathos, and logos · Argument architecture: claim, evidence, reasoning, warrant · Thesis  
construction · Research questions and database research · Source evaluation, summary versus analysis,  
synthesis · Paraphrase, quotation, and citation · Sentence style: subjects and verbs, emphasis, concision,  
combining, modification · Paragraph structure, transitions, and signposting · Introductions and conclusions ·  
Counterargument · Peer review and revision · Presenting technical work to a non-specialist audience ·  
Reflective and metacognitive writing.

## Learning Outcomes

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes (CLOs):

- Analyze the rhetorical situation of a text—genre, rhetor, audience, purpose, and exigence/context—and explain how those elements shape the choices its writer made.
- Compose in multiple genres—narrative, professional, and researched argument—adapting structure, tone, and evidence to a specific audience and purpose.
- Construct an argument that states a claim, supplies evidence, and explains the reasoning connecting the two.
- Locate, evaluate, and integrate outside sources, distinguishing summary from analysis and entering an existing conversation rather than mining it for proof.
- Apply a recursive writing process—invention, arrangement, drafting, peer review, conference, revision, and editing—and demonstrate substantive change between drafts.
- Revise at the sentence level for clarity, emphasis, and concision, explaining the effect of the choices made.
- Design written and presented documents in which layout, typography, and visual elements support the argument, appropriate to genre and audience.
- Deliver spoken presentations that adapt written work for a listening audience under a time constraint.
- Evaluate their own writing and development over the semester—including the language and register choices they make for different audiences—citing specific evidence drawn from their own work

## Policies

### Attendance Policy:

Attendance Policy  
Attendance is expected at every meeting. There is no separate attendance grade and no fixed allowance of  
“free” absences; instead, attendance is built into your participation grade, which is earned five points at a time  
in the room. If you are not there, you do not earn that day’s points, and they cannot be made up later.  
This is a workshop. The work of the course—peer writing groups, Style Labs, in-class drafting, listening to  
your classmates—happens in the room and does not exist in a form you can catch up on afterward. A student  
who misses often will find the participation grade reflects it, and they will also arrive at each deadline less  
prepared than the people who were there.

Some practical notes:  
● Let me know if you must miss class. You do not need to send me medical documentation or explain  
yourself.  
● If you miss a class, get the notes from a classmate before you email me asking what you missed.  
● Arriving substantially late, leaving early, or skipping the group work portion of a session will affect  
that day’s participation points.  
● If you add the course after the semester begins, contact me immediately; we will arrange makeup  
work, due within one week of the September 11 add/drop deadline.  
● If a serious illness, family emergency, or other significant circumstance disrupts your semester,  
contact me and the Dean of Students Office. There are institutional supports for these kind of events,  
and I would rather help you use them than watch you disappear.

### Additional Academic Integrity Course Policy and Penalty Information:

Academic Integrity  
Student-teacher relationships are built on trust. For example, students must trust that teachers have made  
appropriate decisions about the structure and content of the courses they teach, and teachers must trust that the  
assignments that students turn in are their own. Acts that violate this trust undermine the educational process.  
The Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities and The Graduate Student Supplement  
define various forms of Academic Dishonesty and you should make yourself familiar with these. In this class,  
all assignments that are turned in for a grade must represent the student’s own work. In cases where help was  
received, or teamwork was allowed, a notation on the assignment should indicate your collaboration.  
Violations of academic integrity may also be reported to the appropriate Dean (Dean of Students for  
undergraduate students or the Dean of Graduate Education for graduate students, respectively).  
All submitted work must be your own. Words, ideas, structures, and data from any source—print, digital,  
spoken, or generated—must be cited. Rephrasing a source in your own words does not remove the obligation  
to cite it; it changes which citation form you use.  
Collaboration is encouraged wherever this syllabus and the assignment sheets say it is: peer writing groups, in-  
class group work, and practice sessions are collaborative by design. Every polished project is individual work.  
Penalties:  
● First offense: a grade of zero on the assignment, a required meeting with me, and a report to the  
appropriate Dean.  
● Second offense: a failing grade in the course, and a report to the appropriate Dean.  
Artificial Intelligence  
Generative AI tools may be used in this course at specific stages and must be disclosed. You may use them to  
brainstorm, to help locate sources, to check your grammar, and to get feedback on writing you have already  
drafted yourself. You may not use them to generate prose that you submit as your own, to summarize a reading  
in place of reading it, or to produce the substance of any polished project.  
Any polished project on which you used an AI tool in a permitted way must include a short disclosure at the  
end: which tool, at what stage, and what you did with the output. A disclosure costs you nothing. An  
undisclosed use is an academic integrity violation and is handled under the penalties above.  
The reasoning, in case it is useful: these tools can produce the artifact but cannot do the part of writing that is  
figuring out what you think, cannot relay your experiences, cannot speak in your developed voice, cannot tell  
you whether a claim is true, and cannot know whether a particular audience will accept it. A person who  
cannot evaluate the output is not operating the tool. This course is about becoming the kind of writer who can.
