## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

This course will introduce students to concepts of modern physics including properties of light and waves, concepts of quantum mechanics, and special relativity. The course aims to build a strong foundation upon which to build a physics career.

## Course Text(s)

### Text Details:

**Required Texts:** Special Relativity for the Enthusiastic Beginner, by D. Morin; Light and Color in the Open Air, by M. G. J. Minnaert

**Recommended Text:** Mathematical Handbook of Formulas and Tables by Spiegel, Lipschutz, and Liu (Schaums Outline Series)

## Course Goals

### Goals:

Students will be able to employ fundamental physics concepts and careful logic to analyze problems and experiments in quantum physics, light and waves, and special relativity at the introductory level.

## Policies

### Other Course Policies:

**Exams:** I will supply you with a basic relations and math formula sheet for the course. You will be permitted to annotate a copy of this sheet for each exam. No other notes will be permitted.

**Lecture attendance and in-class activities:** Lecture class times involve both formal lecture and interactive activities. Attendance at lecture is not taken, but a large fraction of your grade is associated with lecture-class activities. In-class activities are turned in with Gradescope in LMS. It is expected that you will work on these during the allotted class time, in collaboration with the students sitting around you. However, you will have up until the beginning of the next class to turn them in. You can to the in-class assignment if you are not in class, but you will not benefit from the classroom interactions.

**Homework assignments:** Homework is due on the date indicated, usually at the start of class at 4 pm. Late homework will not be accepted for full credit without prior, written approval from the instructor. Homework can be handed in up until the beginning of the next class period, but the score will include a 25% deduction in points.

Most of your learning will be outside of class as you do homework, read the course materials, and do your own exploration of online materials. I encourage you to form regular study/discussion groups and to work collaboratively. (This practice will be encouraged through the mentoring sessions.) I encourage group meetings in the Huntington Room (JROWL 1C28) or Resnick Center (JROWL 1W20) so you can visit me, the TA, or Mentors when questions arise that you can't resolve among yourselves.

Homework, projects, and final projects will be due according the timeline below, and will be submitted in Gradescope. Students are encouraged to discuss homework assignments with each other, but the work that is turned in must be the student’s original work. ***Submitted homework must clearly show the steps leading up to each answer. Solutions that can only be understood by looking at the methods on another student’s paper will be evaluated for academic dishonesty.***

### Additional Academic Integrity Course Policy and Penalty Information:

**Academic Dishonesty:** Students who cheat will receive a penalty up to and including a failing grade for the course, depending on the severity of the offense. Cheating includes: copying, or allowing another student to copy, a test or assignment; altering a graded test or assignment before requesting regrading; copying submitted solutions from published solution manuals or previous years; using materials other than those that are allowed on an exam; misrepresenting the work of another group or individual as your own. It is considered cheating to help another student cheat. ***It is imperative in your written projects that you do not copy text or images from the web or other sources without attribution, and that you do not misrepresent your collected data. In particular, it is not allowed to use anything generated by AI/Chatbots in any submitted assignment.***

The environment in which students operate has many sources of solutions to homework and projects, including AI chatbots, online search, Chegg, student-organized archives, classmates, and other people. It is okay for a homework/project to consult any of these sources for ideas, information, and to check your work. However, the work you actually turn in must be 100% your own. You must show how and why each step in the homework solution is done, and you must have generated all of the text, plots, data, and tables in the assignments yourself. A good test to determine whether you are within the course rules is to ask yourself whether your actions promote learning, whether you are learning the information and skills that the course is designed to teach you, and whether everything that you turn in is your own work.
