## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

This course provides an upper-division introduction to probability theory, emphasizing both rigorous mathematical foundations and practical problem-solving. The course begins with discrete probability spaces and counting models, then develops conditional probability, Bayes’ formula, independence, random variables, expectation, variance, covariance, convolution, and generating functions. It then extends these ideas to continuous distributions and densities, including the uniform, exponential, gamma, and normal distributions, before concluding with the Poisson process, convergence concepts, the law of large numbers, and the central limit theorem.  
The course is designed to connect precise mathematical statements with intuition and computation. Lecture notes include worked examples, conceptual checkpoints, and exercises intended to help students develop both a rigorous understanding of probability and the ability to model and analyze random phenomena.

## Course Text(s)

The following textbooks are recommended as supplementary references:

- Dimitri P. Bertsekas and John N. Tsitsiklis, *Introduction to Probability*, 2nd ed., Athena Scientific.

These texts cover much of the material presented in the course and may be useful for alternative explanations, additional examples, and practice problems. Additional readings and supplementary materials may be provided throughout the semester as appropriate.

## Course Goals

### Goals:

The goals of this course are to:

- Develop a rigorous foundation in the basic principles of probability theory, beginning with discrete probability spaces and progressing to continuous probability models.
- Teach students how to formulate probabilistic models by identifying sample spaces, events, random variables, and probability distributions.
- Develop a clear understanding of conditional probability, Bayes’ formula, independence, and product probability spaces.
- Introduce the central concepts associated with random variables, including expectation, variance, covariance, correlation, convolution, and generating functions.
- Develop students’ ability to work with important discrete and continuous probability distributions and understand the relationships among them.
- Introduce stochastic-process ideas through the Poisson process and its associated counting and waiting-time distributions.
- Develop an understanding of fundamental asymptotic results in probability, including the law of large numbers and the central limit theorem.
- Strengthen students’ mathematical reasoning and problem-solving skills by connecting precise definitions and theorems with intuition, modeling, and explicit calculations.

## Course Content

### Content Details:

The course will cover the following topics:

- Foundations of discrete probability spaces, events, and probability measures
- Counting methods and urn models, including sampling with and without replacement
- Discrete probability distributions, including hypergeometric, Bernoulli, binomial, geometric, Poisson, and multinomial distributions
- Conditional probability, the law of total probability, and Bayes’ formula
- Independence of events and product probability spaces
- Random variables and their probability distributions
- Joint and marginal distributions and independence of random variables
- Expectation and transformations of random variables
- Variance, standard deviation, covariance, and correlation
- Sums of random variables and convolution
- Probability generating functions
- Continuous probability distributions, probability density functions, and cumulative distribution functions
- Joint densities and continuous random variables
- Uniform, exponential, gamma, and normal distributions
- Multivariate normal distributions
- The Poisson process, including counting processes, exponential waiting times, and gamma arrival times
- Markov’s and Chebyshev’s inequalities
- Convergence in probability and the weak law of large numbers
- Weak convergence and the central limit theorem
- Normal approximation to the binomial distribution and continuity correction

## Learning Outcomes

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes (CLOs):

- Formulate and analyze probabilistic models using sample spaces, events, conditional probability, independence, random variables, and probability distributions.
- Compute and interpret key quantities associated with random variables, including expectation, variance, covariance, distributions of sums, and probabilities for important discrete and continuous distributions.
- Apply and explain fundamental limit results in probability, including the law of large numbers and the central limit theorem, and use them to approximate and interpret probabilistic behavior in large-sample settings.

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes Assessment Measures:

Student achievement of the course learning outcomes will be assessed through **homework assignments and three midterm examinations**.

- **Homework Assignments (10% of final grade):** There will be 11 homework assignments (HAs) distributed throughout the semester. The **10 highest homework scores** will be counted toward the final grade, with the lowest score dropped. Collectively, the homework assignments account for **10% of the final course grade**. The assignments provide regular practice in probabilistic modeling, calculations, and mathematical reasoning and allow students to assess their understanding throughout the semester.
- **Three Midterm Examinations (90% of final grade):** There will be three midterm examinations, each worth **30% of the final course grade**. The examinations will assess students' ability to formulate probabilistic models, apply and interpret probability concepts and distributions, perform mathematical derivations and calculations, and explain the theoretical ideas developed in the course.

Together, the homework assignments and examinations provide both continuous formative assessment and comprehensive evaluation of the three Course Learning Outcomes.

### Additional Academic Integrity Course Policy and Penalty Information:

Students are encouraged to discuss course concepts and homework problems with one another. Collaboration should support understanding, but each student must write and submit their own solutions and should be able to explain all reasoning presented in their work. Copying another student’s solution, submitting work completed by someone else, or presenting material from an external source as one’s own is not permitted.

The three midterm examinations are **individual assessments**. Collaboration, communication with other students, use of unauthorized notes or resources, or any other form of assistance not explicitly permitted by the instructor is prohibited during examinations.

Plagiarism, unauthorized copying, falsification of work, and other forms of academic dishonesty are violations of the Institute’s Academic Integrity Policy.

For a **first academic integrity violation**, the student will receive a grade of zero on the affected homework assignment or examination, and the incident may be reported in accordance with Institute procedures. For a **subsequent violation**, the student may receive a grade of F for the course, subject to applicable Institute policies and procedures.

Students who are uncertain whether a particular form of collaboration or use of an external resource is permitted should consult the instructor before submitting the work.
