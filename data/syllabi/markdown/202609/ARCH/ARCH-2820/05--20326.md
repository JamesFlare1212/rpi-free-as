## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

Course Overview

Architectural Design Studio 3 (ADS 3) builds on the spirit of innovation and experimentation emphasized in Architectural Design Studios 1 and 2, while introducing more architectural constraints. Bachelor of Architecture students in ADS 3 are expected to demonstrate how design skills acquired in ADS1 and ADS 2 are used to resolve program, structure, and accessible route design in a small free-standing art gallery building. The course will be framed by three main topics:

1. **A Relation Between Art and Architecture**
2. **How a Museum or Gallery Presents Art**
3. **The Meaning and Significance of Landscape**

1. **Relation Between Art and Architecture**

As aesthetic and cultural enterprises, art and architecture are closely related. But while art generally maintains purposeful uselessness to emphasize its aesthetic capacities, architecture is always at some level utilitarian. Relationships between the aesthetics of art and the utilitarianism of architecture are highlighted when designing museums, galleries, and other spaces for art. In his essay Design and Crime, the art critic Hal Foster implies this question in a general sense – how much is one willing to let the aesthetic and utilitarian concerns of architecture become conflated?

It could be argued that many of the most visible contemporary museum projects, Ateliers Jean Nouvel’s National Museum of Qatar for example, are quite extreme conflations of aesthetics and utility, and that in many high-profile contemporary museums architecture becomes at least as much of an aesthetic statement as the art it contains. Completed in 2025, Calder Gardens, located in Philadelphia, PA is a monographic museum dedicated to sculptor Alexander Calder, designed by Swiss architecture firm Herzog Demeuron. The building was designed to house specific sculptures from the Calder Foundation collection, “[embodying] a kind of ‘no-design’ architecture, allowing the works of art to express their diversity and ambiguity across numerous spatial contexts,” according to Jacques Herzog.

In ADS3 students will design a new gallery building on the grounds of Storm King Arts Center in New Windsor, New York. Storm King Arts Center is a 500-acre outdoor museum founded in 1960, which contains sculptural works from the world’s most renown 20th century and contemporary artists. Over the course of the semester, students must formulate a position regarding how and how much a building to house art should be aestheticized in relation to the art it contains.

*This old debate [about the appropriateness of Art Nouveau ‘total design’] takes on a new resonance today, when the aesthetic and utilitarian are not only conflated but all but subsumed in the commercial, and everything – not only architectural projects and art exhibitions but everything from jeans to genes – seems to be regarded as so much design. (Hal Foster, Design and Crime, 2002)*

**2. How a Museum or Gallery Presents Art**

Ideas of what a building dedicated to showing art and design should be, and how it should present art to visitors, are many and have shifted over time. Today, different museums and galleries present art and engage visitors in different ways. In the book *Towards A New Museum*, Victoria Newhouse describes several categories of the contemporary museum including: cabinets of curiosities that surprise, delight, and ask visitors to make connections between objects; sacred spaces that invoke qualities of ecclesiastical architecture in order to raise the experience of viewing art to religious ritual; monographic museums that educate visitors about a particular artist’s development; museums as entertainment that attempt to engage touristic rather than academic culture; and museums as environmental art that become active rather than passive players in how art is viewed and experienced.

In recent years, arts institutions such as the Museum Boijmans Van Veuningen Art Depot in Rotterdam, designed by MVRDV and the Victoria & Albert East Storehouse in London by Diller Scofidio Renfro have approached the design of the museum as art storage warehouse open to public visitation.

In designing a new gallery building for Storm King, students must consider how the existing institution and the new proposal falls into one or more of these categories – or seeks to define a new category.

*When the term museum was first used in the Renaissance in reference to private collections, it evoked a different experience from what we now know. In one of the museum’s earliest incarnations, the Cabinet of Curiosities, natural and art objects were jumbled together on the walls and ceilings, cupboards and drawers of one or two rooms. Their purpose was to surprise and delight: viewers had to find the special objects that attracted them and then make their own connections… (Victoria Newhouse, Towards a New Museum, 1998)*

**3. The Meaning and Significance of Landscape**

Within the field of architectural design, the term ‘landscape’ refers to stretches of land that do not contain buildings. These lands may be highly designed and tightly controlled by human activities, or they may be more wild, and less controlled by human activities. Landscapes are more than just topography and the makeup of the ground; they are agglomerations of biological and geological processes unfolding in time scales from hours to eons. Consider ‘landscape’ as distinct from ‘nature.’ While both these terms refer to culturally constructed ideas regarding environments in the earth’s biosphere, ‘nature’ speaks to the environment in general, and ‘landscape’ specifically highlights the ground as a datum within the environment that is charged with design potentials.

Outside of dense urban development, architecture and landscape are intimately tied together. In the simplest terms, the relationship between architecture and landscape might be seen as a visual and/or formal relationship. For example, a building might appear (as most do) as an object or form that is visually distinct from the surrounding landscape. On the other hand, a building might also be designed so that visually it appears to be more continuous with the landscape.

Visual relationships between landscape and architecture are important, but landscapes are more than just visual constructions; they are geological, biological, and anthropogenic systems. At Storm King, consider the geological processes that have brought the the terrain into being, the biological processes that sustain plant and animal life on the site, and the anthropogenic processes that have dramatically transformed the site through purposeful landscape design to accommodate site-specific sculptural works.

In designing a new building for Storm King, consider landscape from both the visual and natural systems perspectives. Consider how landscape becomes an active participant in the architectural design, and how it plays a part in creating an accessible and inclusive experience for viewing art and design.

To gain an understanding of how other architects on other sites have approached and resolved small museum and gallery projects, the semester will begin with an analysis of this type of building. Students will select one small (approx. 5-20,000 sf) built museum or gallery from a curated list and analyze it in a series of models and diagrams that demonstrate understanding of how the design was conceived and how the building works. Particular attention must be paid to how program, circulation, and structure relate to the architect’s design agenda.

**3. Assessment of Existing Institution**

*Assessment Measure #3 for ADS3 – Assessment of the Existing Institution:*

*200-word text and 5+ annotated sketches – made by hand on site during the site visit – which describe and assess functioning of the existing buildings and site in relation to the institution’s stated mission. Preference should be given to orthographic drawing methods such as plan, section and elevation views.*

*Assessment Measure #3 for GAD1 – Assessment of the Existing Institution:*

*500-word text and 7+ annotated sketches by hand, which describe and assess functioning of the existing museum and site in relation to its stated mission. This text must make specific reference to Storm King Arts Center and readings assigned by the section critic.*

Storm King Arts Center is a 500-acre outdoor museum located in Hudson Valley devoted to modern and contemporary sculpture which was founded in 1960 by Ralph Ogden and H. Peter Stern. Originally conceived as a museum for Hudson River School painting, it’s focus shifted to sculpture after an acquisition of 13 works by sculptor David Smith in 1967. In subsequent decades, Storm King became a major venue for site-specific contemporary sculpture by major artists such as Richard Serra, Mark di Suvero, Isamu Noguchi, Andy Goldsworth, and Maya Lin. Through it’s approach of actively reshaping the landscape of the property to accommodate sculptural works rather than acting as a passive, neutral backdrop, Storm King Arts Center positioned itself at the intersection of the Land Art, Minimalism, and post-war contemporary art movements.

Based on a visit to the site, presentations from the institution’s leadership, learning about the institution from its website, researching its history, and becoming familiar with the artists who have been exhibited at Storm King Arts Center, students must make their own assessment of how the existing buildings and site work and/or do not work in terms of the museum’s stated mission. *Additionally, students should gain a general understanding of how an arts center (and museums in general) function legally as a non-profit organization, or for-profit attractions, or a combination of public/private institution, how they maintain funding, raise capital to acquire art, pay staff, and ultimately commission architects to design museums.*

**4. Site Plan**

*Assessment Measure #4 for ADS3 and GAD1 – Site Plan:*

*Drawing of existing Storm King site at 1/32” scale. Clearly indicate all site elements listed below using carefully calibrated drawing graphics.*

Understanding and representing all aspects of an architectural project’s site through drawing is a crucial step in the design process. In this case, the site includes existing buildings and landscape. Draw the Storm King site, show all relevant site information including:

1. Building footprints
2. Building entrances (service and public)
3. Parking
4. Roads
5. Sidewalks
6. Curbs
7. Curb cuts (if applicable)
8. Vegetation
9. Stairs
10. Contour lines of topography, revision of contour lines to accommodate building

This drawing must be presentation quality, it may be in color or black and white, it may include rendered elements and/or shadows. This drawing of existing elements will be considered a final presentation quality drawing and it will subsequently be updated to represent the design proposal.

**5. Predesign (Building Massing)**

*Assessment Measure #5 for ADS3 and GAD1– Predesign-Building Massing:*

*Physical model showing how program of the new building is organized internally and placed in relation to the ground. 1/16” scale, model materials to be specified by section critic.*

The primary goal of this semester is to design a small freestanding gallery building on the Storm King Arts Center grounds with a precise tectonic language, highly considered and resolved interior spaces, and a clear daylighting strategy. Begin the predesign process with an exploration of program and how it informs building massing.

In architecture ‘program’ refers to activities occurring in a building and on its site. At its most fundamental level an architectural program specifies an activity and size for a building (i.e. museum), a zone within a building (i.e. exhibit spaces), and an individual space within a building (i.e. gallery). Decisions regarding design of a building are made in direct response to program. Program for this project is a 5500 square foot (sf) freestanding building containing two main programmatic zones: exhibit space, spaces where art is shown, and public space, non-exhibit spaces which all museum goers can access. Individual spaces that must be included are as follows:

1. Main entrance and lobby, 1000sf
2. Gallery space, 2000sf
3. Art Storage 350SF
4. Education and community space, 500sf
5. Cafe 500SF
6. Circulation 1000SF
7. Restrooms 150SF

Develop a physical massing model that explores and resolves distribution and organization of program. Note that a massing model works with simplified spatial volumes rather than detailed form, and that this massing model must represent the new building in relation to the existing building and site.

**6. Predesign (Response to Context)**

*Assessment Measure #6 for ADS3– Predesign-Response to Context:*

*Annotated plan and section diagrams demonstrating how the new addition relates to specific grids, axes, nodes, datums and views in relation to existing buildings and landscape.*

*Assessment Measure #6 for GAD1 – Predesign-Response to Context:*

*Annotated plan and section diagrams demonstrating how the new building relates to specific grids, axes, nodes, datums and views in the existing building and site. 1/16” scale. 300-word text describing how proposed contextual relationships between existing and new architecture relate to the current mission and architecture of the Storm King Arts Center, as well as how its mission and architecture is proposed to be supported, altered and/or critiqued through the addition. This text must make specific reference to the mission of the Storm King Arts Center and readings assigned by the section critic.*

In addition to developing massing models, the predesign phase of the project includes developing plan and section diagrams that demonstrate how program and massing relate to context of existing building and site. Consider the following:

1. how the new building is placed in relation to existing buildings and other site features
2. how alignments of grids, axes, nodes, or heights (datums) between the new and existing buildings and other site features are created or purposefully broken
3. how new circulation elements join with and/or alter existing circulation elements
4. how visitors approach and enter the building
5. how staff, maintenance and deliveries approach and enter the building
6. how views from site to building, building to site, and within the building are maintained, created and/or altered
7. solar orientation of the new addition

**7. Design Synthesis + Parti**

*Assessment Measure #7 for ADS3 and GAD1 – Design Synthesis / Parti:*

*Two floor plans minimum (ground level, upper level), two sections minimum (longitudinal and cross), two elevations at 1/4” scale.*

While it is assumed that three-dimensional computer modeling will occur through all phases of project development, two-dimensional orthographically projected floor plans, sections, and elevations will also be used instrumentally in design. These drawings MUST:

1. Be built up from scratch – a three-dimensional model may be used as a reference underlay to help produce the drawing, however it must not be included in the drawing
2. Be used to explore and evaluate design decisions bring together aspects of the precedent analysis and generative drawings with design decisions made through explorations of massing, programming, and context
3. be used to explore how natural light is brought into exhibit and public spaces
4. describe all components of architecture at accurate scale
5. be used to describe accurate floor, wall, and roof thicknesses
6. be used to describe simple floor, wall, and roof assemblies
7. show how all public spaces and restrooms are accessible
8. be graphically clear through effective use of line weight and line type

Refer to Architectural Graphic Standards as well as the reference file provided to the studio for information regarding architectural drawing standards.

Refer to the 2010 ADA Standards for Accessible Design for the requirements of accessible routes through and around buildings: [https://www.ada.gov/law-and-regs/design-standards/2010-stds/#401-general](https://www.ada.gov/law-and-regs/design-standards/2010-stds/#401-general)

**8. Grading and Accessibility**

*Assessment Measure #8 for ADS3 and GAD1– Grading and Accessibility Plan:*

*One plan showing parking and grading with 6” interval topography lines at 1/16” scale.*

The project must include 2-4 accessible parking spaces and an area for delivery vehicles to park and unload in proximity to the building. Designing these areas will include involve grading, which is the process of reshaping land, around the building. Develop a 1/16” scale plan showing the following:

1. how accessible parking and delivery areas are incorporated into the landscape, and how their relationship to the main entrance of the building, and views to and from the building are carefully considered
2. topography lines showing how land around the building has been reshaped to achieve the desired design, and provide appropriate slopes for the parking areas, roads, and walkways
3. how vehicles circulate into and through the parking and delivery areas
4. an accessible route from the accessible parking spaces to the building

**9. Tectonic Model**

*Assessment Measure #9 for ADS3 and GAD1 – Tectonic Model:*

*Physical model of at least half the building and surrounding site, 1/4” scale. Model materials to be determined with section leader’s guidance. A series of photograph documenting the daylighting study of the new building.*

‘Tectonics’ refers to how construction is approached as an art rather than a prosaic technical endeavor. A tectonic approach to architecture implies that assembly, structure, space, and aesthetics are all intimately related. This model must represent at least half the volume of the building (it may be a section model) and how the building sits in the ground. It must explore and show the following:

1. the structural system of the building
2. detailed design of interior space development
3. detailed exterior envelope development
4. design of at least one daylighting element
5. accurate floor, wall, and roof thicknesses
6. simple floor, wall, and roof assemblies

Photograph the model in sunlight to test performance of the daylighting system.

**10. Three-Dimensional Imagery**

*Assessment Measure #10 for ADS3 and GAD1 – Three-Dimensional Imagery:*

*Three rendered views of the new addition in relation to existing building and site. Size and format of images to be determined with section leader’s guidance.*

Three rendered views of the project are required: one aerial view, one eye level view of building exterior, and interior view within the building. Test and save multiple camera options for each view. Once views have been selected, add detail to the three-dimensional model in areas visible in the views. Images must be rendered as photo realistically as possible in Rhino/VRay. Develop a material and texture palette, a technique for handling context, and an overall aesthetic that complements and reinforces conceptual ideas of the project.

Using skills acquired in Digital Constructs 3, use AI Generative Fill in Photoshop to develop background imagery. AI image generation must NOT be used to visualize the building itself. Do not default to a conventional sunny day, blue sky, green vegetation aesthetic. Images must be started at least three weeks before the final review and be developed iteratively until the final review.

*Assessment Measure #11 for ADS3 – Project manifesto:*

1. The relationship between art and architecture
2. How the gallery presents art

## Policies

### Attendance Policy:

Attendance is required at all class meetings during the semester. Two (2) unexcused absences from class will result in the lowering of the grade by one (1) letter. Four (4) unexcused absences from class during the term result in an automatic F. Whether an absence (health, immediate family death, officially approved field / sport trip, etc.) is excused or unexcused is determined by the Students Experience Office (4th Floor Academy Hall, x8022, se@rpi.edu), upon presentation of required documentation, according to established policies and procedures (see http:// http://se.rpi.edu/policies/ea/).

Students are required to work in studio during studio hours, and have the required materials and basic tools to develop the assignments. Work includes participation in site visits, design and development, pinups, presentations, discussions and field trips. Failure to work in class with undivided attention and disruptive behavior (tardiness, leaving early, lack of participation, general socializing, etc.), as well working on assignments from other classes during class, will count as absences.

### Other Course Policies:

**Digital Constructs 3**

AD3 will be accompanied by Digital Constructs 3 on Wednesdays. Half of each studio will attend the digital session 2-4pm, and the other half will attend 4-6pm. Digital sessions will introduce new, and reinforce known, tools and techniques that support development of studio projects.

**Pin-ups**

Periodically students will be required to present their work publicly in a formal review process. These presentations are a critical part of the architectural education. It is an opportunity for the student and the faculty to judge the project and critically evaluate the process of design. It may be appropriate at points in the semester to evaluate only selected projects in order to convey information relevant to the entire class. In this situation it is important for each student to witness and participate in the review process. Even if your project is not being reviewed, the criticism and discussion should inform the evaluation of your own progress and design.

**Desk Critiques**

The design studio environment involves two-way communication. The role of the studio critic is to evolve and strengthen the student’s design process. Every time the student meets with the faculty, he or she must have new iterations of work based on previous conversations. If the student has not produced new work, the faculty reserves the right to skip the critique and continue with other students who have produced new material.

**SoA Lectures**

Attendance is MANDATORY at all School of Architecture’s lectures regardless of whether lectures occur in-person or on-line. Unexcused absences from lectures will result in one letter grade dropped from the final studio grade.  
**Environmental Hazards**

THE USE OF SPRAY ADHESIVES, PAINTS AND AEROSOLS IS NOT PERMITTED IN OR ON THE GREENE BUILDING. If you must use these materials, use the JEC high bay spraybooth or use these materials outside away from windows and doors and against a backdrop to protect lifeforms and building materials from overspray.

THESE MATERIALS CAN BE HARMFUL TO YOUR HEALTH, THE HEALTH OF OTHERS, AND THE ENVIRONMENT. FOLLOW ALL DIRECTIONS ON THE LABELS. Epoxies and resins shall not be used in the Greene building and if used, should comply with all procedural safeguards and not returned to the Greene Building prior to being adequately off-gassed. Acrylic and other materials, used in the laser cutter or any other process that will create dangerous off-gassing must be adequately off-gassed in the shop off-gassing chamber. This material must be left in the off-gassing chamber for the appropriate time as listed or taken from the Greene Building until the proper time has passed. If any student is caught violating this policy the following may occur:

1. The student may be charged for the cost of cleaning / repairing any damage to the building and its surroundings,
2. The material being worked on will not be included in the review of that project. It will be disqualified from consideration in the evaluation of the project.

WHEN USING ADHESIVES AND OTHER POTENTIALLY TOXIC MATERIALS, FOLLOW THE USER GUIDELINES. IF IT SMELLS BAD - IT IS BAD. IT IS UNACCEPTABLE TO SUBJECT OTHERS TO IT AS IT MAY POSE SERIOUS HEALTH RISKS. IF IT DOESN’T SMELL BAD IT STILL MAY BE BAD. READ AND FOLLOW THE USER GUIDELINES. YOUR RESPONSIBILITY IS NOT ONLY TO YOUR OWN HEALTH, BUT ALSO FOR THE HEALTH OF OTHERS IN THE ENVIRONMENT.

### Academic Policies:

- ### Academic Integrity

  Student-teacher relationships are built on trust. For example, students must trust that teachers have made appropriate decisions about the structure and content of the courses they teach, and teachers must trust that the assignments that students turn in are their own. Acts that violate this trust undermine the educational process. The Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities and The Graduate Student Supplement define various forms of Academic Dishonesty and you should make yourself familiar with these. In this class, all assignments that are turned in for a grade must represent the student’s own work. In cases where help was received, or teamwork was allowed, a notation on the assignment should indicate your collaboration.

  Violations of academic integrity may also be reported to the appropriate Dean (Dean of Students for undergraduate students or the Dean of Graduate Education for graduate students, respectively).

  If you have any question concerning this policy before submitting an assignment, please ask for clarification. In addition, you can visit the following site for more information on our Academic Integrity Policy: [Students Rights, Responsibilities, and Judicial Affairs](https://info.rpi.edu/dean-students/student-rights-responsibilities-and-judicial-affairs).

**Policy on collaboration and cheating/plagiarism**

Interdisciplinary collaboration is a critical component of this course. All assignments that are turned in for a grade must accurately represent the student’s own work, and properly cite authorship of work that was not solely completed by the student. Any miss-representation of work shall be evaluated and could result in a failing grade for the assignment. A subsequent infraction by the student will result in a failing grade for the course.
