## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

The Doctoral Seminar 1 is a preparatory research seminar for second-year PhD students in the Built Ecologies Program. The course supports students in defining and critically articulating the theoretical and technical background of their emerging research topic in advance of the PhD qualifying examination, which will take place at the end of the semester or, when appropriate, by the end of the following semester.

The qualifying examination is a prerequisite for proceeding toward dissertation work, but it is not an examination of the dissertation proposal itself. Rather, it evaluates each student’s command of the bodies of knowledge that inform the proposed area of research, including relevant theories, methods, technical frameworks, disciplinary debates, and unresolved questions. In this context, expertise requires the ability to distinguish established knowledge from open research questions, situate those questions within broader intellectual and technical contexts, and explain their significance with clarity and scholarly rigor.

At the beginning of the semester, each student will identify three broad areas of expertise that will constitute the principal areas of examination for the qualifying exam. These areas will be developed in consultation with the course instructor and the student’s advisor. At least one area should be situated within architecture, or within the student’s primary discipline, while at least one other area should be drawn from a discipline outside of the student’s primary discipline.

The semester is organized into three sections, each corresponding to one selected area of inquiry. For each section, students will develop a focused bibliography, construct a conceptual diagram that relates key ideas, figures, technologies, and historical developments, and prepare a concise written response to a question formulated by the course instructor in coordination with the student’s advisor. Through these activities, students will learn to classify and relate concepts, build coherent intellectual narratives, communicate complex material clearly, and respond to questions in a rigorous and disciplined manner. The course therefore prepares students to demonstrate critical understanding of the theoretical and technical background of their research area, without requiring or evaluating a dissertation proposal.

## Course Goals

### Goals:

Upon completion of the course, students will be expected to achieve the following objectives, each of which corresponds to the learning outcomes listed below:

1. Identify the limits of established knowledge within a selected field of inquiry and recognize unresolved scientific or scholarly questions that define the field’s current research frontier.
2. Synthesize relevant bodies of knowledge, articulate the intellectual and technical contexts from which new research questions emerge, and communicate the significance of those questions with clarity, precision, and concision.
3. Organize concepts, ideas, figures, and technologies into well-justified classifications that clarify relationships and contribute to a coherent intellectual narrative within the student’s area of expertise.
4. Formulate concise, rigorous, and analytically grounded responses to questions relevant to the student’s selected areas of expertise.

## Learning Outcomes

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes (CLOs):

- Identify the boundary between established knowledge and unresolved scientific or scholarly questions within a field of inquiry.
- Synthesize established knowledge, articulate the context from which new questions emerge, and communicate the significance of those questions in language that is clear, precise, concrete, and concise.
- Develop a well-justified classification of concepts that organizes relationships among ideas and technologies and constructs a coherent intellectual narrative within the student’s field.
- Prepare concise and rigorous responses to questions relevant to the student’s area of expertise.
- The ability to prepare a comprehensive bibliography for their area of inquiry

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes Assessment Measures:

All Course Objectives and Outcomes will be assessed as 3 hand-in projects (areas of inquiry/questions) that will be evaluated by the course instructor at the end of each semester section.

Each area of inquiry will also be evaluated on a weekly basis based on oral presentations the students give for each area of inquiry/ questions

### Additional Academic Integrity Course Policy and Penalty Information:

The Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities defines various forms of Academic Dishonesty and procedures for responding to them. Academic dishonesty violates the trust between students and teachers. Students must trust that teachers have made appropriate decisions about the structure and content of the courses they teach. Teachers must trust that the assignments students turn in are their own performance. Violations of this trust undermines the educational process. Cheating, plagiarism (defined as the lack of attribution or citation of the work of others), willful damage or theft of another person’s work violate academic integrity. The first instance of the breach of this trust will merit a failing grade in this course and the violator(s) will be reported to the Dean of Students for disciplinary action. The definitions and examples presented below are samples of types of academic dishonesty and are not to be construed as an exhaustive or exclusive list. The academic dishonesty policy applies to all students, undergraduate and graduate, and to scholarly pursuits and research. Additionally, attempts to commit academic dishonesty or to assist in the commission or attempt of such an act are also violations of this policy. • Academic Fraud: The alteration of documentation relating to the grading process. For example, changing exam solutions to negotiate for a higher grade or tampering with an instructor’s grade book. • Collaboration: Deliberate facilitation of academic dishonesty in any form. For example, allowing anotherstudent to observe an exam paper or allowing another student to “recycle” one’s old term paper or using one another’s work in a paper or lab report without citing it as another’s work. • Copying: Obtaining information pertaining to a graded exercise by deliberately observing the paper of another student. For example, noting which alternative a neighboring student has circled on a multiplechoice exam. • Cribbing: Use or attempted use of prohibited materials, information, or study aids in an academicexercise. For example, using an unauthorized formal sheet during an exam. • Fabrication: Unauthorized falsification or invention of any information in an academic exercise. Forexample, use of “bought” or “ready-made” term papers, or falsifying lab records or reports. • Plagiarism: Representing the work or words of another as one’s own through the omission of acknowledgment or reference. For example, using sentences verbatim from a published source in a term paper without appropriate referencing, or presenting as one’s own the detailed argument of a published source, or presenting as one’s own electronically or digitally enhanced graphic representations from any form of media. • Sabotage: Destruction of another student’s work. For example, destroying a model, lab experiment,computer program, or term paper developed by another student. • Substitution: Utilizing a proxy, or acting as a proxy, in any academic exercise. For example, taking anexam for another student or having a homework assignment done by someone else.
