## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

This is a option (vertical) design studio for third and fifth year undergraduate students.  
Architectural representation is the discipline's most powerful instrument. For five centuries it has let us do two things at once: describe a building precisely and imagine one that does not yet exist. We do not draw after reality; we draw to produce new realities, holding the measurable and the immeasurable in a single image. But that generative power has been trained almost entirely on one building: the static, visible matter of walls, fl oors, and details. A building is also an energetic system, the heat, air, and moisture moving through and around it, shaped by that same matter whether we intend it or not. This is the thermodynamic life of a building: conduction, radiation, convection, and the evaporation and condensation of moisture, the heat and mass transfer that govern how a building gains, stores, and loses energy, and how it feels to the body. Material enters here as a thermal actor, chosen for its conductivity, storage, and surface qualities. Selecting a material is already a deeply architectural act, and one we perform without seeing its thermodynamic weight.  
We have a refi ned design language for the fi rst building and leave the second to technical diagrams that sit outside the language of design. Representation has never been turned on this second building for a reason: conventional drawing is a snapshot by nature, and energy is a fl ow that exists only in time, from the instantaneous plume of air off a warm surface to thermal mass charging by day and discharging by night to ground coupling that shifts across the seasons. The energy also runs across scales we tend to skip, down to the molecular, where a material absorbs and stores heat, and up to the atmosphere, where radiation is filtered and trapped. For visual thinkers, what is not drawn falls out of perception: it is never unpacked, never intentionally designed. The energy a building moves is real the moment it is built, but if it was never drawn, it was never designed on purpose. It happened by accident.  
Invisible Buildings Studio turns representation's generative power on that second building. We treat energy effi ciency not as an engineering problem handed off after the design is set, but as a design opportunity available from the fi rst sketch, so that what makes a building good is no longer how little it exchanges with its surroundings but how well it shapes that exchange. To design that exchange on purpose, we slow the process down and work across scales with four linked tools. We build salt-bath water models, where dense saline stands in for warm air so buoyancy-driven fl ow becomes visible and testable. We use an AI-supported, gamified buoyancy learning lab to ground the underlying physics. We draw, using drawing to park these fl ows in place and study them. And we develop new modes of representation as a generative tool, stretching the conventions we already have rather than replacing them, to hold fl ow across time and scale. Through this loop of making, visualizing, and drawing, an understanding that begins at the molecular level of the material is scaled up to the building, its interiors, and its fl ow dynamics. Read this way, heating and cooling stop being only mechanical services and become experiential qualities a design can reach for, warmth, the movement of air, the feel of a surface, a designable thermal experience that carries a lineage from Heschong's Thermal Delight in Architecture forward.

## Course Content

### Content Details:

The studio runs in three phases. Phase 1 (weeks 1 to 3) builds your foundation. You learn the studio's core design principles and the hands-on skills to use them: how to boil a design down to its key principles, build a model that stands in for it, run that model in the salt bath by a set protocol, and read what the experiment tells you. At the same time we build the building science behind buoyancy, so you can shape the fl ow of air in a building on purpose, and we look at how other designers have drawn the invisible. This phase carries [Assignment 1], the AI-supported gamifi ed buoyancy lab, and [Assignment 2], your fi rst water-bath test for the studio library. It closes with a pin-up.  
Phase 2 (weeks 4 to 5) turns to precedent. With the principles, the skills, and the physics in hand, you study who has designed with these ideas and who has found ways to draw them. From that study you make a fi rst design proposal and refi ne it for a larger pin-up tied to a visit from Salmaan Craig of UCLA, who leads an in-class workshop, gives a public lecture, and reviews your proposals. Phase 3 (weeks 6 to 15) is design. You develop the strategy from Phase 2 toward the midterm review in week 8. By the two-thirds review in week 12 you are settling the design and producing critical drawings, a fi nalized parti model, and a site model. The semester ends with a penultimate review in week 14 and the fi nal review in week 15. Phases 2 and 3 are both guided by [Assignment 3].  
Throughout the semester we will be using class Miro Board for desk crits and sharing resources. Please be sure to save this link to be used for the rest of the semester. Additionally, for all of your keystone submissions you will be using the class BOX folder, here.

### Other Course Policies:

## Collaborative Work

Working collaboratively with a partner is a challenging and rewarding experience. Collaboration is essential in the architectural profession and learning to be effective in a collaborative team is a critical component of this course. Establish a set of best practices for collaborative work within your team, including the following:

- Outline goals, expectations, meeting schedule, and mode(s) of communication
- Assign responsibilities and be accountable to your collaborators
- Develop a timeline for completing project deliverables

Other components of a successful collaboration are open communication, active listening, respect for your collaborator(s), addressing conflict immediately (either directly with each other, or including your instructor), staying on task, and being flexible.

## Pin-ups

Periodically students will be required to present their work publicly in a formal review process. These presentations are a critical part of the architectural education. It is an opportunity for the student and the faculty to judge the project and critically evaluate the process of design. It may be appropriate at points in the semester to evaluate only selected projects in order to convey information relevant to the entire class. In this situation it is important for each student to witness and participate in the review process. Even if your project is not being reviewed, the criticism and discussion should inform the evaluation of your own progress and design.

Failure to attend one or more pin-ups for the full class period may result in a grade deduction.

## Desk Critiques

The design studio environment involves two-way communication. The role of the studio critic is to evolve and strengthen the student’s design process. Every time the student meets with the faculty, he or she must have new iterations of work based on previous conversations. If the student has not produced new work, the faculty reserves the right to skip the critique and continue with other students who have produced new material.

## Attendance and Timely Completion of Work

Attendance is required at all class meetings during the semester. Two (2) unexcused absences from class will result in the lowering of the grade by one (1) letter. Four (4) unexcused absences from class during the term result in an automatic F. Whether an absence (health, immediate family death, officially approved field / sport trip, etc.) is excused or unexcused is determined by the Students Experience Office (4th Floor Academy Hall, x8022, se@rpi.edu), upon presentation of required documentation, according to established policies and procedures (see http://se.rpi.edu/policies/ea/).

Students are required to work in studio during studio hours, and have the required materials and basic tools to develop the assignments. Work includes participation in site visits, design and development, pinups, presentations, discussions and field trips. Failure to work in class with undivided attention and disruptive behavior (tardiness, leaving early, lack of participation, general socializing, etc.), as well working on assignments from other classes during class, will count as absences.

All work must be submitted for review and discussion on the dates noted under each assessment measure. However, final assessment of work will not occur until the final review. Students must respond to feedback received from the section instructor and midterm jury to refine and develop work.

For students with an ADHD diagnosis, extra time applies only to exams, and quizzes completed in class. ## Policy on collaboration and cheating/plagiarism

Interdisciplinary collaboration is a critical component of this course. All assignments that are turned in for a grade must accurately represent the student’s own work, and properly cite authorship of work that was not solely completed by the student. Any misrepresentation of work shall be evaluated and could result in a failing grade for the assignment. A subsequent infraction by the student will result in a failing grade for the course.
