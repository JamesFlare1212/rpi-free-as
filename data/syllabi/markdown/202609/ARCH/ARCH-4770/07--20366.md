### ARCH 4770 - Architectural Design Studio 5

---

This is an upper division architectural design studio that explores topics of contemporary interest to the discipline. Students apply for this studio based on their personal interest and professional objectives. Three such studios Architectural Design Studio 5, Architectural Design Studio 6, and Architectural Design Studio 7, are required for the degree. This course is offered in the fall and spring semesters and is required of all architecture undergraduates in the B.Arch. program.

## Course Goals

### Goals:

This Vertical Studio will take up the impending demolition of a massive concrete building - the Albany Central Warehouse - as an opportunity to explore architecture’s role in cycles of making, remaking, unmaking the built environment in ways that aim to creatively and critically expand the discipline’s capacity to take on a massive design challenge - the global climate crisis.

A long-waited local takedown amidst the need for a global drawdown. The studio will undertake experiments in creative resourcefulness that engage both of these situations. Working at an architectural scale with a planetary scope, it will explore the field of adaptive reuse in ways that aim to support and drive climate adaptation. This work is intended as a counterproposal to the current plans to erase the Central Warehouse. It is also intended to offer alternatives to practices that treat the design, construction, and destruction of buildings as an engine for, and measure of, endless growth. Fueled by the mass consumption of finite natural resources, this socio-ecological paradigm has been described as “a universe in which all that is solid melts into air.” Such a characterization of the impermanence of all human artifacts and systems seems to be particularly prescient as our contemporary moment is increasingly shaped by a reckoning with a world that we have created which is now pushing the limits of the Earth’s capacity to sustain all forms of life.

The design studio will address fundamental - even existential - questions about architecture’s relation to building within the context of a changing and warming climate. With its apparent solidity and former use as a cold storage facility in mind, the Albany Central Warehouse will be taken up as a framework for reimagining architecture’s most basic function of shelter and elemental property of mass for the common good. This transformation will include a public “cooling center” facility whose role will be to provide temporary refuge from extreme heat along with other public services associated with short- and long-term climate adaptation.

This inquiry will take up the notion of architecture as a “moving project” in terms of adaptability, circularity, and ephemerality. As such, it will challenge the traditional view of buildings as permanent structures, singular objects, with fixed use types. This effort will combine speculative thinking with pragmatic, material-based work, orienting its study towards an expansive exploration of the multivalent ways that architecture changes over time.

## Learning Outcomes

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes (CLOs):

- OUTCOME 1: Research 1a. ability to use / develop specialized knowledge - Students will conduct and implement discipline-specific research on the fields of adaptive reuse and urban mining using circular building construction / deconstruction methods.
- OUTCOME 1: Research 1b. ability to use / develop broad and integrative knowledge - Students will conduct and implement interdisciplinary research on the urban transformation of the city of Albany, NY identifying social and ecological factors, architectural and infrastructural typologies, and spatial and material conditions.
- OUTCOME 2 Intellectual Design Skills 2a. ability to design - Students will learn to differentiate between, and demonstrate the ability to design for, different types of architectural change (use, configuration, material, symbolism, etc.), different durations/frequencies of change (temporary/permanent, continuous/incremental, etc.), and various realms/scales of impact (building, urban block, region, planetary, etc.).
- OUTCOME 2 Intellectual Design Skills 2b. ability to design using information resources - Students will learn to analyze existing site conditions and a program brief through the use of base documentation and demonstrate the ability to use this information in the design of strategic interventions into an existing built fabric of a large urban building and complex site with precision, clarity, and impact.
- OUTCOME 2 Intellectual Design Skills 2c. ability to design engaging diverse perspectives - Students will learn to identify the diverse perspectives of project stakeholders and demonstrate the ability to integrate alternative worldviews and lived experiences into the design of an abandoned food storage warehouse into a new public structure with a mix of use types including a public cooling center.
- OUTCOME 2 Intellectual Design Skills 2d. ability to design with ethical reasoning - Students will learn to make informed value judgements regarding the appropriate allocation of limited resources (material, spatial, ecological, financial, etc.). Students will demonstrate the ability to articulate the ethical dilemmas inherent in the choices of where, when, and for whom these resources will be distributed and how they are integrated into a project design.
- OUTCOME 2 Intellectual Design Skills 2e. ability to design with quantitative fluency - Students will learn to develop a building's design in relation to specific types and quantities of material stockpiles available for reuse (i.e. urban mining). Students will demonstrate the ability to interpret and synthesize different types of information (written/numerical/visual), establish and calculate spatial areas and material "take-offs", analyze multiple organizational "test-fit" scenarios, and determine the project's scope of work.
- OUTCOME 3 Collaboration 3a ability to collaborate with team - Students will demonstrate the ability to work effectively in teams using collaborative methods and interpersonal skills. Students will demonstrate the ability to develop a strategy for group work (knowledge/info sharing, ideation, workflow, project management, etc.) that recognizes different group member’s strengths, documents the strategy so that others may understand it, implements the strategy, communicates and evaluates the results, and modifies the strategy as needed.
- OUTCOME 4 Civic and Global Engagement 4a. ability to engage the civic realm - Students will demonstrate the ability to engage issues that are in the public interest, specifically the expansion of material reuse and circular construction activities in the NYS Capitol Region and related issues of urban redevelopment and public space.
- OUTCOME 4 Civic and Global Engagement 4b. ability to engage the global realm - Students will demonstrate the ability to engage issues of global consequence, specifically climate adaptation and sustainable design activities aimed at transforming urban buildings, cities, and regions into viable places of future habitation in response to global climate change.
- OUTCOME 5 Design Execution 5a. Midterm Review - Students will demonstrate the ability to implement their learning in Outcomes #1-4 in a clear, complete, and compelling way in a presentation that includes diagrams, mappings, orthographic drawings, axonometrics, renderings., and models
- OUTCOME 5 Design Execution 5b. Final Review - Students will demonstrate the ability to implement their learning in Outcomes #1-4 in a clear, complete, and compelling way in a presentation that includes diagrams, mappings, orthographic drawings, axonometrics, renderings, and models.
- Additional grade requirements - Active Participation

### Academic Policies:

- ### Academic Integrity

  Student-teacher relationships are built on trust. For example, students must trust that teachers have made appropriate decisions about the structure and content of the courses they teach, and teachers must trust that the assignments that students turn in are their own. Acts that violate this trust undermine the educational process. The Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities and The Graduate Student Supplement define various forms of Academic Dishonesty and you should make yourself familiar with these. In this class, all assignments that are turned in for a grade must represent the student’s own work. In cases where help was received, or teamwork was allowed, a notation on the assignment should indicate your collaboration.

  Violations of academic integrity may also be reported to the appropriate Dean (Dean of Students for undergraduate students or the Dean of Graduate Education for graduate students, respectively).

  If you have any question concerning this policy before submitting an assignment, please ask for clarification. In addition, you can visit the following site for more information on our Academic Integrity Policy: [Students Rights, Responsibilities, and Judicial Affairs](https://info.rpi.edu/dean-students/student-rights-responsibilities-and-judicial-affairs).

### Additional Academic Integrity Course Policy and Penalty Information:

**STATEMENT OF ACADEMINC INTEGRITY**

The Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities defines various forms of Academic Dishonesty and procedures for responding to them. Academic dishonesty violates the trust between students and teachers. Students must trust that teachers have made appropriate decisions about the structure and content of the courses they teach. Teachers must trust that the assignments students turn in are their own performance. Violations of this trust undermines the educational process. Cheating, plagiarism (defined as the lack of attribution or citation of the work of others), willful damage or theft of another person’s work violate academic integrity. The first instance of the breach of this trust will merit a failing grade in this course and the violator(s) will be reported to the Dean of Students for disciplinary action.
