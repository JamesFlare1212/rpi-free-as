## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

This seminar considers the shared domains between architecture, architectural culture, and the history and theory of the environment. It begins with the proposition that, much like a building, city, or landscape, environment is constructed. This is to say that just as there are many “natures,” there is also a surfeit of definitions of environment that range from ideas about natural resources in classical antiquity, to theories of enclosure and production in the modern era, to more recent explorations concerning the relations between the natural environment and issues of settler colonialism, resource extraction, social justice, and economic inequality. With this in mind, this class will consider a wide selection of readings from different disciplines as a means to examine how the history and theory of environment underlie the most fundamental aspects of architectural design, from site selection, to considerations about materials, and even to issues of drawing and representation.

With this in mind, this class considers the history and theory of the environment within three distinct, yet interrelated frames: Climates, Accelerations, and Imaginaries. You will note that all of these are presented in the plural, the implication being that just as there is more than one conception of climate, there are also different manifestations of accumulations and imaginaries. As this course will demonstrate, all play a vital role in determining the relations between the history and theory of the environment and the realms of architectural culture, practice, and even architectural history.

## Course Text(s)

### Text Details:

The majority of the readings will come from the following texts:  
Nick Axel, Daniel A. Barber, Nikolaus Hirsch, and Anton Vidolke, eds.,  
Accumulation: The Art, Architecture, and Media of Climate Change (Minneapolis: University of Minnesota Press, 2022)  
Daniel Ryan, Jennifer Ferng, and Eric L’Herureux, Drawing Climate: Visualising Invisible Elements of Architecture (Basel: Birkhäuser, 2021)  
James Graham, Caitlin Blanchfield, Alissa Anderson, Jordan Carver, and Jacob Moore, eds., Climates: Architecture and the Planetary Imaginary (Zürich: Lars Müller Publishers, 2016)  
Kim Förster, ed. Environmental Histories of Architecture (Montreal: Canadian Centre for Architecture, 2022)  
Texts will be provided as linked PDFs on the course syllabus by the instructor.

## Course Goals

### Goals:

The required readings in this course come from a variety of fields and have been selected to provide you with a wide-ranging and nuanced understanding of the ways the history and theory of environment dovetail with architectural culture and history. That being said, there will be instances when the readings will seem very far afield from issues pertaining to the design of buildings and cities. With each class meeting, our job is to make these connections in a way that is clear, useful, and compelling.  
Engaged Participation and Class Presentations.. Since this is a seminar-style course, emphasis will be on engaged class participation. To be an engaged participant means more than just contributing to class discussion every week. Engagement includes, but is not limited to the following:  
Preparation (reviewing readings and material before class)  
Focus (avoiding distractions during in-person and online activities)  
Presence (engaged and responsive during class)  
Asking Questions (in class, out of class, online, offline)  
Listening (hearing what others say, and also what they’re not saying)  
Specificity (referring to specific ideas from readings and discussions)  
Synthesizing (making connections between readings and discussions)  
In addition, and as part of your participation grade, you will be required to lead the  
discussion in one class beginning the week of October 26. I will share the requirements  
and expectations for this part of your class well before the week of October 26. Please  
note that you are required to sign-up for a presentation by October 19.

- ### Academic Integrity

  Student-teacher relationships are built on trust. For example, students must trust that teachers have made appropriate decisions about the structure and content of the courses they teach, and teachers must trust that the assignments that students turn in are their own. Acts that violate this trust undermine the educational process. The Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities and The Graduate Student Supplement define various forms of Academic Dishonesty and you should make yourself familiar with these. In this class, all assignments that are turned in for a grade must represent the student’s own work. In cases where help was received, or teamwork was allowed, a notation on the assignment should indicate your collaboration.

  Violations of academic integrity may also be reported to the appropriate Dean (Dean of Students for undergraduate students or the Dean of Graduate Education for graduate students, respectively).

  If you have any question concerning this policy before submitting an assignment, please ask for clarification. In addition, you can visit the following site for more information on our Academic Integrity Policy: [Students Rights, Responsibilities, and Judicial Affairs](https://info.rpi.edu/dean-students/student-rights-responsibilities-and-judicial-affairs).
