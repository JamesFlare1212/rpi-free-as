*‘It is an unfortunate choice of memorial. Memorials are built to give context and, possibly, meaning to suffering that is otherwise incomprehensible. We do not memorialize bus accidents, which by nature are contextless, meaningless. To treat the Vietnam dead like the victims of some monstrous traffic accident is more than a disservice to history; it is a disservice to the memory of the 57,000.’*

-Karal Ann Marling and Robert Silberman, “The Statue Near the Wall: The Vietnam Veterans Memorial and the Art of Remembering,” *Smithsonian Studies in American Art* 1, no. 1 (Spring 1987); re-quoted in The New Republic

*The Washington Monument. The Bennington Battle Monument. The Lincoln Memorial. The Jefferson Memorial.* These structures have the words *monument* or *memorial* in their names. We understand them as iconic (and conventional) American commemorations of famous people and wars. The *USS Arizona* (BB-39) *Memorial* is less “normative” as it is a viewing platform located over the sunken battleship in the Bay of Pearl Harbor. Lost on December 7, 1941, it is both a grave and a cenotaph. One watches over its agonizing decay via glass portal in the floor. All of these are also works of architecture.

Architect Maya Lin’s 1982 *Vietnam Veterans Memorial* also commemorates a war, but it foregrounds the 57,000+ soldiers who lost their lives in one of America’s most unpopular conflicts. One can touch the names of real people: friends, family members, parents, spouses. It is an incredibly moving experience.

Similarly, artist Chris Burden’s work, *The Other Vietnam Memorial,* 1991 is a freestanding ‘vertical Rolodex’ of 3 million computer-generated Vietnamese surnames, as no comprehensive list exists. Here, anonymity is the medium of the work as much as the etched copper plates, aluminum and steel. One might consider these both ‘nonuments’ in that Maya Lin’s challenged the tradition of war memorials in every way (materially, lack of figuration, ALL the names), and Burden’s version is a lament for an enemy that also made sacrifices, most of them NOT soldiers, but civilians.

This semester we will look at the long-debated roles of artist vs. architect, sculpture vs. architecture and endeavor to understand the spectrum of social roles that are serviced by the idea of monuments, memorials, varied populations (those honored, those excoriated) and the self-aggrandizing ‘commemorative architectures’ that seem to follow both empires and democracies.

**1.0 Overview** Monument/Nonument: The Portable Monument/On the Mall/Off the Wall

**Monument/Nonument** is a vertical studio focused on the history of war memorials and other civic commemorative architecture in the United States. Key themes include:

**Traditions, Critically Assessed:** By examining how American commemorative architecture shapes national memory, the course will reveal how the shift from traditional, heroic war monuments to more abstract works (like Maya Lin’s 1982 Vietnam Veterans Memorial) opened up the genre to allow for critical counter-monuments.

**Who is Being Honored? Who is speaking for the dead?** The course will explore the idea of single voices, collective voices (“the will of the people”) and the strong influence that governments, corporations and seemingly benevolent institutions (like educational facilities and small towns) have had on who is selected for commemoration.

**Who is Omitted? What is Erased?** The course will delve into the ‘opposite of monuments: their dismantling, undoing of history and ‘cleansing’ of groups, like indigenous peoples, wrongly convicted felons and those groups previously made invisible by laws, ghettoizing architecture and elimination from the historical record.

**In other words:**

**The Pre-Lin Era** explores traditional civic monuments, neoclassical war monuments and triumphalist structures designed to project heroic sacrifice, state power and unified national narratives.

**The Post-Lin Era** examines how contemporary “nonuments” challenge monumental permanence, invite public absence, and confront marginalized histories, trauma and dissent.

Architecturally Speaking, “nonuments may also challenge received notions of what is materially appropriate for monuments, with less durable, structurally slight and even easily damaged

**Theories of Space and Time:** How memorials manipulate the perception of space and time compared to the occupiable physical experience of architecture.

**Urban Projections:** The way cities and the built environment are represented, interpreted, and utilized in the collection and projection of history.

**Aesthetics and Design:** Analyzing the architectural aesthetics within memorials and how architectural techniques (the grid, order, seeming chaos, chance operations, public address, and wayfinding) create specific spatial and emotional effects.

**Historical Context**: Examining the historical discourses around the emergence of popular print, photography and an increasingly literate population forced a ‘reading’ of urban spaced shaped by its own history, and the commemoration of events that both ‘happened here’ and happened elsewhere. In other words, history’s influence on architecture in the late 19th, early 20th and early 21st centuries.

**Experiential Design:** Exploring immersive encounters between people, media and places, often involving new media and digital fabrication techniques.

Look at the news. Funding cuts in education the science, public health, the arts. As artist Mark Dion said at a 2024 interview at the Tang Museum with painter Alexis Rockman, “The arts and the sciences may not be the same thing, but they have the same enemies.”

**1.4 Learning from Architecture: The Endless Loop of Experimentation Emerging from Convention**

Washington D.C., like Troy, NY is a city of memorials, monuments and public spaces. Increasingly—due to surveillance technology and the branded “Flock” cameras—these spaces are less open to civic gatherings that push back against the oppressive policies that come with surveillance. In this way, places of discourse become places of scrutiny by those in power, with the tools and the tactics to control those seeking voice.

**1.5 Criticism and Capitalism**

In *The Poetics of Space* (1957), Gaston Bachelard asserts that “if one would ask us, what is the most precious benefaction of the house, we would say: the house accommodates dreaming, the house protects the dreamer, the house allows us to dream in peace”. The claim can easily be made that the primary role of housing is safety—including protection from the elements, but also as an affront, literally, to society. It is an emblem of the culture but can also be seen as being apart from it. This is the main thesis of many artists seeking to make public space ‘safe space’ again. Film director Alfred Hitchcock’s main thesis: that the most sinister thing is not a far off, foreign threat, but is right next door. In 2026 America, his fears are playing out for all to see and share.

**3.0 Site(s)**

There are 3 sites this semester, in order of address: 1) Downtown Troy, likely in the vicinity—but not limited to—Monument Square. Multiple locations will be the site for the Portable Monument Project. 2) The Mall, in Washington, D.C. This will be the site of a two-person project to design a new memorial for this highly charged and internationally scrutinized seat of American power. 3) The site of the (now demolished) East Wing of the White House. Arguably the most highly-charged architectural site in the world (perhaps after NYC’s hallowed grounds of the 9/11 attacks), it will host 5 4-person team projects.

- **1** **Site and Building Considerations**

1. A) The first site borders (and links) multiple Troy programs and the project will facilitate new conversations around Troy’s history and future.
2. B) Currently, paved roads serve both cars and pedestrians downtown and particularly around Monument Square. It is a dangerous place for pedestrians with no railing and no space to walk other that the road. Even EMPAC does not provide much public outdoor event space. An increase in the public participation of campus events will be facilitated by the project.
3. C) Hang out spaces are key to establishing a lively urban condition. There will be informal gathering spaces that could be used for the practice of free speech/public address and the enactment of spontaneous and planned performances.
4. D) The project will provide additional classroom and fabrication space for the SoA. As with the LRC located in downtown Troy and CASE co-located in Brooklyn, the SoA needs satellite spaces in which to make, test and deploy innovative architectures.
5. E) Finally, to clarify, Project 2.2 is not a building, per se, and will have a more open program. Project 2.3 will have a sealed envelope and a set of given programs, with student teams inventing least one additional program per student.

## Course Goals

### Goals:

STUDIO OBJECTIVES

To develop an awareness and understanding of the role of the architect. To understand the political role of the architect within an economic structure, especially that of a firm working at the highest government levels.

To develop an awareness of the importance of the designer/client dialogue. To explore computation and other strategies (such as data mining and A. I.) as part of the research required to establish viable programs for said site. To leverage these tech approaches in tandem with traditional methods such as precedent studies, personal and analytical writing, sketching, model-making and other visioning tools.

To collaborate on an architectural program of sustainability, restoration and improvement, mindful of historical contexts and emergent materials. To see architecture as an agent of interpersonal and cultural exchange.

To engage the site first as a set of limits and adjacencies, both physical and programmatic.

To design an architectural context of site and construction that integrates and invigorates a neighborhood condition, an economic lever for the property owner thereby creating a productive urbanism of sustainability.

## Course Content

### Content Details:

**5.0 Learning Outcomes**

**Outcome 1** Research

**1a. Ability to use/develop specialized knowledge** – Students will conduct and implement interdisciplinary research into the history of American monuments, developing an awareness of aspects of designer approaches, innovations, and their influence on generations citizens, artists and architects.

**1b. Ability to use/develop broad and integrated** knowledge – Students will conduct and implement interdisciplinary research into the history of world commemorative architecture, developing an awareness of how design, construction and material techniques, narratives and the proliferation of such ideas has made (for Millenia) memorial architecture a global language.

**Outcome 2** Intellectual Design Skills

**2a. Ability to design** – In each of the three projects, students will conduct and implement discipline-specific research on the historic and analogous relationships between architecture and memorials (language, honorifics, representation, abstraction, ceremony, protest and anniversary). As time is a major component of both architecture and memory students will critically negotiate both.

**2b. Ability to design using information resources** – Students will learn to identify various traditions and innovations memorials as an aid to design. With a wealth of Memorial sites online—and access to the various proscribed readings—students will be engaged and informed in their design process by their collection and critical consumption of architectural and memorial datasets and precedents.

**2c. Ability to design using diverse** **perspectives** – it is a popular claim (or trope) that there are 3 universal languages: mathematics, music, and cinema. Students will learn empathy via encounters with residents from disparate world cultures, and as part of an international campus will incorporate and value diverse origins to produce architectural designs that are expansive, inclusive and reflective of their many users.

**2d. Ability to design with ethical** **reasoning** – as a continuation of 2c, students will learn and embrace the New York State laws and the Institute’s policies regarding community access, high standards of safety, social justice and awareness for how ethical design concerns matters of space, material and public policy. Students will demonstrate the ability to articulate and incorporate these concerns via a vigorous dialogue with stakeholders.

**2e. Ability to design with quantitative fluency** – in dialogue with both users and administrators of the Mall in Washington, students will demonstrate the ability to respond—via design—to multiple stakeholders’ concerns, ranging from maintenance (custodial, grounds) to security (ease of access, protection from bodily harm) to budgetary oversight (initial fit-up, upkeep, wear and tear) to adaptive re-use depending on evolving community needs.

**2f. Ability to design with communicative fluency** – Students will learn to *listen* to stakeholders’ concerns and critically incorporate those ideas (ranging from the programmatic to the aesthetic, from the material to the social). They will learn to re-articulate the problem(s) via a spectrum of communication tools including surveys, drawing proposals, historical programs and user feedback loops.

**Outcome 3** Collaboration

**3a. Ability to collaborate with team** – Students will demonstrate the ability to work effectively with various 3-person teams, in the second and third portion of the studio, as the 1st Portable Monument portion is a solo project. By developing effective communication skills, shared research, project oversight and rotating leadership (on each task there will be a team leader and 2 team subordinates; each student plays both roles in a revolving fashion), the team will learn to work efficiently while still remaining experimental. In this way each team members’ interests and strengths will be leveraged for the best possible project outcome.

**3b. Ability to collaborate with a community partner** - Students will demonstrate the ability to work effectively with our partners: The City of Troy, independent shopkeepers and independently maintained semi-public spaces. This is entirely dependent on where student choose to site their first project. By being mindful of the specific goals and audiences of these entities, students will collaborate with them directly, assuring mutually shared expectations and outcomes. Close contact will enable smooth negotiation of obstacles, program changes and other unanticipated outcomes, as well as agreed upon goals.

**Outcome 4** Civic and Global Engagement

**4a. Ability to engage the civic realm** – Students will demonstrate the ability to engage local/civic issues as well as Institute priorities, both of which are shared concerns in the Capital Region. An engaged citizenry makes for more informed design and this kind of transparency is cost effective and more efficient, saving on costs associated with the initial misinformation—or lack of information/public sentiment. In this sense, there is a community design aspect to the project. Negotiating with propery owner and viewers alike makes for a kind of participatory architecture.

**4b. Ability to engage the global realm** – Students will demonstrate the ability to engage in social and ethical issues that continue to define our present ‘age of anxiety.’ As these monuments can create bridges between cultures, it is essential to have multiple tools for the communication, translation and the dissemination of design ideas that may seem radical—even objectionable—to some groups.

**Outcome 5** Design Execution

**5a. Midterm Review** – Students will—as individual designers—research and present project 2.2 and 2.3 in ways that demonstrate their ability to reflect the learning outcomes enumerated and elaborated above, that is, outcomes 1-4. The means of demonstrating this will be a series of diagrams, writings, and abstracted physical study models (involving video displayed via projection and on screens) that help to illuminate a proposed landscape architecture intervention. The project will also be realized via drawing sets and model photography.

**5b. Final Review** – Students will—in teams of 4—research and present project 2.3 in ways that demonstrate their ability to reflect the learning outcomes enumerated and elaborated above, that is, outcomes 1-4. The means of demonstrating this will be a series of diagrams, writings, and a final model of their proposed building and landscaping. The project will also be realized via drawing sets, a short video and previously generated model photography by the individual team members.

## Policies

### Attendance Policy:

Attendance is required at all class meetings during the semester. Two (2) unexcused absences from class will result in the lowering of the grade by one (1) letter. Four (4) unexcused absences from class during the term result in an automatic F. Whether an absence (health, immediate family death, officially approved field / sport trip, etc.) is excused or unexcused is determined by the Students Experience Office (4th Floor Academy Hall, x8022, se@rpi.edu), upon presentation of required documentation, according to established policies and procedures (see http:// http://se.rpi.edu/policies/ea/).

Students are required to work in studio during studio hours and have the required materials and basic tools to develop the assignments. Work includes participation in site visits, design and development, pinups, presentations, discussions, and field trips. Failure to work in class with undivided attention and disruptive behavior (tardiness, leaving early, lack of participation, general socializing, etc.), as well working on assignments from other classes during class, will count as absences.

STUDIO POLICY:

Pin-ups Periodically students will be required to present their work publicly in a formal review process. These presentations are a critical part of the architectural education. It is an opportunity for the student and the faculty to judge the project and critically evaluate the process of design. It may be appropriate at points in the semester to evaluate only selected projects to convey information relevant to the entire class.

Desk Critiques The design studio environment involves two-way communication. The role of the studio critic is to evolve and strengthen the student’s design process. Every time the student meets with the faculty, he or she must have new iterations of work based on previous conversations. If the student has not produced new work, the faculty reserves the right to skip the critique and continue with other students who have produced new material.

SoA Lectures Attendance is MANDATORY at all School of Architecture’s lectures regardless of whether lectures occur in-person or on-line.

### Academic Policies:

- ### Academic Integrity

  Student-teacher relationships are built on trust. For example, students must trust that teachers have made appropriate decisions about the structure and content of the courses they teach, and teachers must trust that the assignments that students turn in are their own. Acts that violate this trust undermine the educational process. The Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities and The Graduate Student Supplement define various forms of Academic Dishonesty and you should make yourself familiar with these. In this class, all assignments that are turned in for a grade must represent the student’s own work. In cases where help was received, or teamwork was allowed, a notation on the assignment should indicate your collaboration.

  Violations of academic integrity may also be reported to the appropriate Dean (Dean of Students for undergraduate students or the Dean of Graduate Education for graduate students, respectively).

  If you have any question concerning this policy before submitting an assignment, please ask for clarification. In addition, you can visit the following site for more information on our Academic Integrity Policy: [Students Rights, Responsibilities, and Judicial Affairs](https://info.rpi.edu/dean-students/student-rights-responsibilities-and-judicial-affairs).

### Additional Academic Integrity Course Policy and Penalty Information:

Academic Integrity The Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities defines various forms of Academic Dishonesty and procedures for responding to them. All forms are violations of the trust between students and teachers. Student-teacher relationships are built on trust. For example, students must trust that teachers have made appropriate decisions about the structure and content of the courses they teach, and teachers must trust that the assignments that students turn in are their own performance. Acts that violate this trust undermine the educational process.

Policy on collaboration and cheating/plagiarism Interdisciplinary collaboration is a critical component of this course. All assignments that are turned in for a grade must accurately represent the student’s own work, and properly cite authorship of work that was not solely completed by the student. Any miss-representation of work shall be evaluated and could result in a failing grade for the assignment. A subsequent infraction by the student will result in a failing grade for the course.

## Other Course-Specific Information

### Additional Course Information:

**6. PROCESS NOTES**

Assignments are just that: work given by the professor. They are the manifestation of certain objectives or goals deemed important by both the individual teacher, the specific educational program, and in the case of architecture, NAAB and NCARB. But they are only a small part of your total education, which—if you are lucky—will be an ongoing process and far outlast your time in school. Be curious about the projects and the assignments as a metaphor for the world and its opportunities and challenges. On a personal note: after my first year at the Rhode Island School of Design, I said to myself that if I could make art over the summer without assignments from my teachers, I might have a chance of becoming an artist. Similarly, if you are able to invent other “unassigned” aspects for the work you present—either individually or in teams—you will take possession of the project and make it your own.
