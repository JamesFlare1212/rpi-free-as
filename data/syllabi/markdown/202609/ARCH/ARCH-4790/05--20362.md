## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

“…it was the discovery of fire that originally gave rise to the coming together of men, to the deliberative assembly, and to social intercourse.”

\- Vitruvius, The Ten Books on Architecture, 20-30 BCE

Architectural representation is the discipline's most powerful instrument. For five centuries it has let us do two things at once: describe a building precisely and imagine one that does not yet exist. We do not draw after reality; we draw to produce new realities, holding the measurable and the immeasurable in a single image. But that generative power has been trained almost entirely on one building: the static, visible matter of walls, floors, and details. A building is also an energetic system, the heat, air, and moisture moving through and around it, shaped by that same matter whether we intend it or not. This is the thermodynamic life of a building: conduction, radiation, convection, and the evaporation and condensation of moisture, the heat and mass transfer that govern how a building gains, stores, and loses energy, and how it feels to the body. Material enters here as a thermal actor, chosen for its conductivity, storage, and surface qualities. Selecting a material is already a deeply architectural act, and one we perform without seeing its thermodynamic weight.

We have a refined design language for the first building and leave the second to technical diagrams that sit outside the language of design. Representation has never been turned on this second building for a reason: conventional drawing is a snapshot by nature, and energy is a flow that exists only in time, from the instantaneous plume of air off a warm surface to thermal mass charging by day and discharging by night to ground coupling that shifts across the seasons. The energy also runs across scales we tend to skip, down to the molecular, where a material absorbs and stores heat, and up to the atmosphere, where radiation is filtered and trapped. For visual thinkers, what is not drawn falls out of perception: it is never unpacked, never intentionally designed. The energy a building moves is real the moment it is built, but if it was never drawn, it was never designed on purpose. It happened by accident.

Invisible Buildings Studio turns representation's generative power on that second building. We treat energy efficiency not as an engineering problem handed off after the design is set, but as a design opportunity available from the first sketch, so that what makes a building good is no longer how little it exchanges with its surroundings but how well it shapes that exchange. To design that exchange on purpose, we slow the process down and work across scales with four linked tools. We build salt-bath water models, where dense saline stands in for warm air so buoyancy-driven flow becomes visible and testable. We use an AI-supported, gamified buoyancy learning lab to ground the underlying physics. We draw, using drawing to park these flows in place and study them. And we develop new modes of representation as a generative tool, stretching the conventions we already have rather than replacing them, to hold flow across time and scale. Through this loop of making, visualizing, and drawing, an understanding that begins at the molecular level of the material is scaled up to the building, its interiors, and its flow dynamics. Read this way, heating and cooling stop being only mechanical services and become experiential qualities a design can reach for, warmth, the movement of air, the feel of a surface, a designable thermal experience that carries a lineage from Heschong's Thermal Delight in Architecture forward.

## Policies

### Attendance Policy:

Attendance is required at all class meetings during the semester. Two (2) unexcused absences from class will result in the lowering of the grade by one (1) letter. Four (4) unexcused absences from class during the term result in an automatic F. Whether an absence (health, immediate family death, officially approved field / sport trip, etc.) is excused or unexcused is determined by the Students Experience Office (4th Floor Academy Hall, x8022, se@rpi.edu), upon presentation of required documentation, according to established policies and procedures (see http://se.rpi.edu/policies/ea/).

Students are required to work in studio during studio hours, and have the required materials and basic tools to develop the assignments. Work includes participation in site visits, design and development, pinups, presentations, discussions and field trips. Failure to work in class with undivided attention and disruptive behavior (tardiness, leaving early, lack of participation, general socializing, etc.), as well working on assignments from other classes during class, will count as absences.

All work must be submitted for review and discussion on the dates noted under each assessment measure. However, final assessment of work will not occur until the final review. Students must respond to feedback received from the section instructor and midterm jury to refine and develop work.

For students with an ADHD diagnosis, extra time applies only to exams, and quizzes completed in class. It does not apply to projects or labs where the students are able to plan more time outside of class to complete assignments. Note that a studio class such as AD5/7 consists solely of project-based assignments. Students are encouraged to attend faculty office hours and tutoring sessions to help plan the completion of studio, lab or project-based assignments.

### Other Course Policies:

Points may be deducted from the final grade in the following circumstances:

- Failure to attend midterm review for the full period (-5 points max.)
- Failure to present at the midterm review (-10 points max.)
- Failure to attend final review for the full period (-5 points max.)
- Failure to present at the final review (ground for failing the course, -10 points min.)

### Academic Policies:

- ### Academic Integrity

  Student-teacher relationships are built on trust. For example, students must trust that teachers have made appropriate decisions about the structure and content of the courses they teach, and teachers must trust that the assignments that students turn in are their own. Acts that violate this trust undermine the educational process. The Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities and The Graduate Student Supplement define various forms of Academic Dishonesty and you should make yourself familiar with these. In this class, all assignments that are turned in for a grade must represent the student’s own work. In cases where help was received, or teamwork was allowed, a notation on the assignment should indicate your collaboration.

  Violations of academic integrity may also be reported to the appropriate Dean (Dean of Students for undergraduate students or the Dean of Graduate Education for graduate students, respectively).

  If you have any question concerning this policy before submitting an assignment, please ask for clarification. In addition, you can visit the following site for more information on our Academic Integrity Policy: [Students Rights, Responsibilities, and Judicial Affairs](https://info.rpi.edu/dean-students/student-rights-responsibilities-and-judicial-affairs).
