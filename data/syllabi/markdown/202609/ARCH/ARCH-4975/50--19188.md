## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

This course explores the development of Renaissance and Baroque art and architecture in Rome, situating monuments, artists, and urban planning projects within their cultural, political, and religious contexts. Through a combination of lectures, site visits, and discussions, students will critically engage with key works, historical texts, and architectural treatises to gain a comprehensive understanding of Rome as a center of innovation and artistic exchange.

## Learning Outcomes

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes (CLOs):

-  Identify and analyze the main architectural and artistic typologies of Renaissance and Baroque Rome (churches, palaces, piazzas, villas).
-  Recognize the stylistic contributions of major artists and architects including Alberti, Bramante, Raphael, Michelangelo, Bernini, and Borromini.
-  Explain the historical, religious, and political contexts that shaped artistic production in Rome between the 15th and 18th centuries.
-  Interpret primary sources, including treatises and biographies, and connect them to the monuments studied.
-  Critically evaluate urban planning projects and master plans as reflections of ideological and cultural change.
-  Conduct on-site visual analysis and engage in scholarly discussion about works of art and architecture.

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes Assessment Measures:

ON-SITE OBJECT/MONUMENT PRESENTATION

Each student will give **one individual on-site presentation** during the semester. Presentation topics and dates will be assigned by the instructor according to the course calendar.

The purpose of this assignment is to develop the ability to analyze a work of art or architecture **in situ** and to communicate that analysis clearly to others. The presentation should therefore not consist primarily of biographical information or a summary of facts gathered from secondary sources. Students should use the **monument, building, urban space, or work of art itself as the principal object of investigation**.

Oral Presentation

**Length:** 8 minutes, followed by approximately 2 minutes of questions and discussion. The presentation should address the following:

1\. Identification and Historical Context

Identify the work or monument, artist/architect, date, patron or commissioner when relevant, original function, and historical context.

2\. Visual and/or Architectural Analysis

Analyze what can be observed directly on site. Depending on the assigned work, this may include plan, elevation, façade, spatial organization, materials, light, scale, movement, decoration, iconography, relationship to the urban setting, or the viewer's experience of the work.

3\. Interpretation and Significance

Explain why the work is significant within the history of Renaissance or Baroque Rome. The presentation should move beyond description to interpretation.

4\. Connection to the Course

Relate the work to themes, artists, architectural typologies, historical developments, primary sources, or other monuments discussed during the semester.

5\. Scholarly Research

Students must consult a minimum of three scholarly sources. Course readings should be used whenever relevant. General websites, tourist guides, Wikipedia, blogs, and similar non-scholarly sources do not count toward the required bibliography.

Written Deliverable

A one-page presentation sheet must be submitted to the instructor before the beginning of the on-site class in which the student presents.

The sheet should include:

- Student name
- Title of assigned monument/work
- Artist or architect, date, patron, and location, when applicable
- 3–5 principal analytical points
- One relevant quotation from a primary source or scholarly text, when appropriate
- Bibliography of at least three scholarly sources

The written sheet is intended as a concise record of the student's research and argument. It should **not**

be a transcript of the oral presentation.

Presentation Expectations

Students should speak to the group rather than read a prepared text. Notes may be used, but presentations that consist primarily of reading from a phone, tablet, or written script will not receive full credit for oral communication.

Whenever possible, students should direct the group's attention to specific features of the work or monument while speaking. The goal is to help classmates understand how to look at, analyze, and interpret the object in front of them.

Students should be prepared to answer questions from the instructor and classmates following the presentation.

**FINAL EXAM**

The final exam will be a cumulative, analytical assessment worth 25 points and will evaluate students’ understanding of the principal themes, monuments, artists, architects, and primary sources studied throughout the semester. Rather than focusing primarily on memorization, the exam will emphasize the ability to recognize, analyze, compare, and interpret works of Renaissance and Baroque art and architecture.

This approach reflects the course learning objectives, particularly the development of visual analysis, historical interpretation, and the ability to connect monuments with their cultural, political, religious, and urban contexts.

The exam will consist of four sections:

**Visual Identifications (5 points) -** In the identification section, students will recognize selected monuments or works and briefly explain their historical and artistic significance.

**Comparative Visual Analysis (8 points) -** The comparative section will ask students to analyze two works or buildings in relation to issues such as geometry, spatial organization, architectural vocabulary, light, movement, patronage, and the experience of the viewer. Images, plans, and architectural details may be provided as part of the questions.

**Primary Source/Concept Analysis (5 points) -** The primary-source section will present a short passage from a text studied during the semester, such as Alberti or Vasari, and students will connect the ideas expressed in the passage to a relevant monument, artist, or architectural problem. These authors are included among the primary sources assigned for the course.

**Synthetic Essay (7 points)**. The final essay will require students to construct a clear argument using several specific examples from both Florence and Rome and to address broader themes such as the relationship between Renaissance and Baroque, the transformation of Rome, patronage, classical tradition, or the changing relationship between architecture and the viewer.

### Additional Academic Integrity Course Policy and Penalty Information:

**Academic Integrity**

The Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities defines various forms of Academic Dishonesty and procedures for responding to them. Academic dishonesty violates the trust between students and teachers. Students must trust that teachers have made appropriate decisions about the structure and content of the courses they teach. Teachers must trust that the assignments students turn in are their own performance. Violations of this trust undermines the educational process.

**Policy on collaboration and cheating/plagiarism**

Cheating, plagiarism (defined as the lack of attribution or citation of the work of others), willful damage or theft of another person’s work violate academic integrity. The first instance of the breach of this trust merits a failing grade in this course and the violator will be reported to the Dean of Students for disciplinary action.

Interdisciplinary collaboration is a critical component of the course. All assignments that are turned in for a grade must accurately represent the student’s own work, and properly cite authorship of work that was not solely completed by the student. Any misrepresentation of work is evaluated and could result in a failing grade for the assignment. A subsequent infraction by the student results in a failing grade for the course.
