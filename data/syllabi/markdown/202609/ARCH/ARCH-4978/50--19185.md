## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

Still in the present times women face obstacles and barriers to achieve their social and professional goals. This setting is an outcome of the disparity of the civil rights, determined by an aside position of women in the history. The feminist movement, especially in the ‘70s, has prompted the essential enhancement to the study of the history of women, and their role in the secularized society.

The feminist movement opened a path to study and investigate women in the arts giving birth to feminist art history and lately to gendered art history. As such, recent investigations are pointing to different ways women interacted with visual arts, trying to disrupt commonplace ideas surroundings medieval or modern women.

The aim of this course is that of exploring the history of architecture and the history of art through-and- by the history of women, analyzing crucial concept to better understand marginalization, emancipation and challenges women had to front and overcame to get equal opportunities and chances.

During the course we will study leading protagonists of women in the arts, and mythological, biblical or historical characters analyzed as study-case whose legacy in many regards is still affecting our language, habits, and cultural constraints.

The course will be held in class and onsite, taking advantage of the works of art present inside the major museums, churches, and historic buildings.

## Learning Outcomes

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes (CLOs):

- Students will be able to familiarize themselves with gender studies.
- Students will be able to recognize and interpret works done by and/or for women.
- Students will be able to understand the importance of art as a means of power, communication, and empowerment.
- Students will build a critical gendered perspective on social, cultural, and economic issues.

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes Assessment Measures:

1. Hands-On Project — AIWAC Conference — 20%

   **AIWAC Conference Lab: Women, Art & the Production of Knowledge**

   Students would experience the conference from inside not only as audience members. The 18 students will be divided into six teams of three.

   Team 1 — Speaker Research & Introductions

   Research speakers in advance. Prepare concise professional biographies and, where appropriate, 60–90 second introductions.

   Team 2 — Conference Program & Content

   Check names, titles, institutional affiliations, abstracts, panel descriptions, and program consistency. This teaches something surprisingly valuable: **scholarly precision**.

   Team 3 — Audience & Conference Experience

   Help with registration, welcoming participants, directions, session transitions, materials, and the practical experience of attendees.

   But they should also observe: How does the organization of a scholarly event affect participation and intellectual exchange?

   Team 4 — Student Rapporteurs

   Assign students to panels. They take analytical notes and identify:
   - central argument(s);
   - methodology;
   - principal case studies;
   - important questions raised;
   - connections to Women in the Arts.

   They subsequently produce a 500-word panel report.

   Team 5 — Questions & Scholarly Discussion

   Students research speakers beforehand and formulate two informed questions for their assigned session.

   The important requirement: questions cannot simply be “What inspired you?” They must emerge from

   the speaker's research or presentation.

   Team 6 — Conference Documentation / Intellectual Map

   Document the intellectual content of the conference and identify recurring concepts such as visibility, marginalization, attribution, gender, patronage, archive, body, identity, artistic agency, historiography, etc.

   Individual AIWAC reflection

   Even though students work in teams, I would require a brief individual reflection:

   **500–750 words:** choose one idea, methodological problem, or case study encountered at AIWAC. Explain how it changed, complicated, or reinforced your understanding of one issue studied in Women in the Arts.

   That makes the conference assistance academically assessable rather than simply service.

   FINAL WRITTEN EXAMINATION — 25 POINTS

   The Final Written Examination is a cumulative assessment designed to evaluate students' ability to critically interpret the principal themes, works, artists, patrons, historical figures, and methodological questions examined throughout the semester.

   The examination will emphasize **analysis, comparison, interpretation, and argumentation rather than the memorization of isolated facts or dates**.

   Students should demonstrate their ability to examine art and architecture through a gender-aware art- historical perspective and to connect individual works and historical figures to broader questions concerning **gender, representation, artistic agency, power, patronage, professional identity, religion, social status, and historical visibility**.

   Exam Structure

   The examination is worth 25 points and consists of three sections.

   Section I — Short Analytical Responses: 8 points

   Students will answer two short analytical questions, worth 4 points each.

   Each response should develop a concise argument supported by one or more specific examples studied during the semester.

   Questions may address issues such as:
   - gender and representation;
   - female identity and social status;
   - marriage, motherhood, and family;
   - the relationship between women and public authority;
   - artistic and professional identity;
   - patronage as a form of agency;
   - the relationship between the female body and social or religious ideals;
   - the distinction between historical evidence, interpretation, and speculation.

   Section II — Comparative Essay: 7 points

   Students will choose one of two comparative questions.

   The essay should compare at least **two** specific works, artists, patrons, or historical figures, preferably from different periods or cultural contexts.

   Students should identify both similarities and differences, but comparison alone is not sufficient. The response must develop an argument explaining why those similarities and differences are historically significant.

   Possible themes may include representation and agency, private and public identity, marriage and professional life, patronage and authorship, religious and secular models of womanhood, or changing opportunities available to women.

   Section III — Synthetic Essay: 10 points

   Students will choose one of two broad questions addressing a central intellectual problem of the course.

   The essay must present a clear thesis and support it through at least four specific examples from different moments of the semester.

   Students will be expected to connect individual case studies to broader methodological and historical questions, rather than discussing four examples independently.

   Possible themes include:
   - ¾ Gender and Art History:

   What does a gendered approach allow us to understand about an artwork, monument, artist, or historical figure that a conventional analysis might overlook?
   - ¾ Visibility and Historical Memory:

   Why have some women's contributions to art and architecture become marginalized, forgotten, misattributed, or defined primarily through their relationships with men?
   - ¾ Agency and Power:

   How have women negotiated social, political, religious, and professional systems that restricted their access to authority?
   - ¾ Representation versus Agency:

   What is the difference between representing women and understanding women as active participants in artistic and cultural production?
   - ¾ Rewriting Art History:

   Does recovering women artists simply add forgotten women to the existing history of art, or does their recovery require us to reconsider how art history itself has traditionally been constructed?
   - ¾ Rewrite the Museum Label:

   During one of your museum visits, you have read a lot of museum labels. Imagine to later rewrite it from a gender-aware perspective: What does the existing label tell us? What does it leave out? What changes if women, patronage, marriage, power, labor, or the body become part of the interpretation?

   Preparing for the Examination

   Students are responsible for the principal artists, works, monuments, historical figures, readings, lectures, films, class discussions, and on-site sessions covered during the semester.

   Students should review the work presented by their classmates during the on-site sessions; however, they will not be expected to reproduce specialized information that appeared exclusively in another student's presentation.

   Preparation should focus particularly on the ability to:
   - identify important course examples and explain their significance;
   - connect works to their historical and social contexts;
   - compare examples from different periods;
   - distinguish visual or documentary evidence from interpretation;
   - use gender as an analytical category rather than simply identifying the gender of an artist or subject;
   - construct a clear thesis and support it with specific evidence;
   - recognize the limitations of the historical evidence available to us.

### Additional Academic Integrity Course Policy and Penalty Information:

**Academic Integrity**

The Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities defines various forms of Academic Dishonesty and procedures for responding to them. Academic dishonesty violates the trust between students and teachers. Students must trust that teachers have made appropriate decisions about the structure and content of the courses they teach. Teachers must trust that the assignments students turn in are their own performance. Violations of this trust undermines the educational process.

**Policy on collaboration and cheating/plagiarism**

Cheating, plagiarism (defined as the lack of attribution or citation of the work of others), willful damage or theft of another person’s work violate academic integrity. The first instance of the breach of this trust merits a failing grade in this course and the violator will be reported to the Dean of Students for disciplinary action.

Interdisciplinary collaboration is a critical component of the course. All assignments that are turned in for a grade must accurately represent the student’s own work, and properly cite authorship of work that was not solely completed by the student. Any misrepresentation of work is evaluated and could result in a failing grade for the assignment. A subsequent infraction by the student results in a failing grade for the course.
