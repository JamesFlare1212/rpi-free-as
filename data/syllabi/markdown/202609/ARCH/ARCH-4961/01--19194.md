1. **INTRODUCTION**

The seminar will explore the life, work and influence of artist Marcel Duchamp (1887-1968), inventor of the readymade, grandfather of conceptual art, lover of puns and anagrams (see course title). By examining his ideas and those of his peers including his artist siblings (Raymond Duchamp-Villon, Jacques Villon, Suzanne Duchamp); friends (Maya Deren; Joseph Cornell; Man Ray; Eric Satie; Constantin Brancusi; Hans Richter; Francis Picabia); acolytes and admirers (John Cage; Robert Rauschenberg; Jasper Johns; Merce Cunningham; Octavio Paz), we will critically map his impact on 20th and 21st century art and architecture.

Speculated as “the world’s first feminist artist” by historian and curator Helen Molesworth, Duchamp continues to be relevant to art discourse, 58 years after his death. Or does he? The course will feature readings, response papers, field trips and (possible) discussions with some of the world’s leading Duchamp authorities, like Donald Shambroom (independent scholar), Francis M. Naumann (historian and gallerist) and Michael Taylor (Director, Virginia Museum of Fine Arts).

Each semester the Duchamp Seminar contextualizes an anniversary of a particular object, concept or influence. For example, in 2017 we examined the 100-year legacy of *Fountain,* deemed the most influential art object of the 20th century. In 2016 we looked at *With Hidden Noise,* arguably among his most enduringly enigmatic works, but also a performative object with links to magic and vaudeville. 2023 marked the 100th anniversary of Marcel Duchamp abandoning painting, and (seemingly) art altogether. But that date also comes close to the 200th anniversary of the invention of photography, whose radical influence on art we are linking to these nascent days of A.I. (artificial intelligence), becoming broadly available to the public online.

This semester we will look closely at the film works (by and) of Marcel Duchamp, his interest in mechanical objects and his participation in so-called “performance art” (his participation in films, actions and other live performances predates the 1960s-era term).

Significantly, we are lucky to be able to view (in person) the massive exhibition MARCEL DUCHAMP organized by the Museum of Modern Art (MoMA) and the Philadelphia Museum of Art (PMA).

2026 is the 100th anniversary of *Anémic Cinéma*, 1926. This is a 7-minute silent film made in collaboration with his longtime friend, American artist Man Ray (cinematographer) and Marc Allégret, and is co-credited to Duchamp’s alter-ego, Rrose Sélavy. The film consists of a sequence of 19 alternating spinning disks. Ten disks are inscribed with verbal puns and alliterations in French, creating witty wordplay as they rotate. Nine disks have spiral designs (which Duchamp called "Rotoreliefs") that create an optical illusion of three-dimensional depth when spun on a phonograph turntable. The title itself, "Anémic Cinéma," is an anagram and a near palindrome, reflecting the wordplay central to the work.

## Course Goals

### Goals:

3. 2. **COURSE MISSION**

   To establish a “first contact” with Duchamp, his ideas and those modernist thinkers in his circle. To critically engage the work, his biography and the myths surrounding him—as a way to evaluate his impact on contemporary thought, art, architecture and music.
   3. **COURSE OBJECTIVES**

   To develop an awareness and understanding of the chronology of Duchamp’s work as it relates to the development of modernist and post-modern thought

   To develop an awareness and understanding of the basic concepts of Duchamp’s work including his use of machines, architectural drawing conventions, his invention of the Readymade, his adoption of gender-bending personae and the role of “non-art” and “anti-art”

   To develop an awareness and understanding of Duchamp’s influence on architecture

   To explore historical — and evolving — relationships between ethics and aesthetics. What does it mean to work in the avant-garde? What is the value of antagonism, radical experimentation and genre-breaking? What roles does the artist adopt when he or she or they are perceived as rebellious?

## Learning Outcomes

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes (CLOs):

- Students will be able to articulate the role of the artist in terms of modernist history, particularly that of Marcel Duchamp and those operating within the contemporary era and ethos.
- B) Students will be able to articulate the gestalt of the modern into the postmodern and relate European and American modernity to global art history: associations, traditions, consequences, innovations and critical rebellion.
- C) Students will be able discuss the structure of any artwork, work of design, architecture, performance, and work of music, with a particular emphasis on Duchamp's influence, by becoming a more receptive, critical and sensitive viewer.
- D) Students will be able to articulate a history and the development of Marcel Duchamp's artistic development, rebellion and canonization
- E) Students will be able to analyze the architectural language of Marcel Duchamp's art in regard to: his roots in Impressionism and Fauvism; his transition into Cubism and break from group association; his innovative practice re: filmmaking, animation, machinery, performance, gender-bending alter egos; his invention of the Readymade; his inevitable exit from painting to pursue chess, etc.
- F) Students will be able to discuss the influence of Duchamp’s work on contemporary scientific thought, historical events and the evolution of art education.
- G) Students will learn how to critically deconstruct a Duchamp's work and translate those ideas into a work of small-scale installation. The Duchamp Seminar is a "history/theory course where we made things."
- H) Students will experience the collaborative nature of contemporary art practice by participating in at least one team project.

## Policies

### Attendance Policy:

**Participation** It is expected that all students will participate in a productive and meaningful way. This includes being on time for class sessions, having completed all assignments in a timely manner, being actively engaged in group discussions, presentations and crits. There is ample evidence in my 40 years of teaching that a class can excel or falter depending on the overall group dynamic. With at least one project in dialogue with a classmate, it is self-evident that good, creative and clear communication is essential.

**Attendance** Attendance is essential to the successful completion of the course and will be taken at each class. The primary source of the course material is class sessions, and each student is responsible for all of the material presented, discussed and reviewed in class. As life insists, there are occasional challenges to our schedules, and absences will evaluated on an individual basis, providing the student makes up the required viewing and the work that was due on the date of the absence. This may mean a discussion of the content of the work with professor and fellow students.

### Other Course Policies:

**PROCESS NOTES**

Assignments are just that: work given by the professor. They are the manifestation of certain objectives or goals deemed important by both the individual teacher, the specific educational program, and in the case of architecture, NAAB and NCARB. But they are only a small part of your total education, which—if you are lucky—will be an ongoing process and far outlast your time in school. Be curious about the projects and the assignments as a metaphor for the world and its opportunities and challenges. On a personal note: after my first year at the Rhode Island School of Design, I said to myself that if I could make art over the summer without assignments from my teachers, I might have a chance of becoming an artist. Similarly, if you are able to invent other “unassigned” aspects for the work you present—either individually or in teams—you will take possession of the project and make it your own.

**ATTENDANCE**

A word on studio etiquette. In my studio you are expected to refrain from cell phone use, unless you are documenting work-in-progress. Research should take place via the laptop and by reading the books that I provided and that you seek out. Similarly, you are expected to arrive on time and stay for the duration of studio. Last semester I encountered students wandering off during crits—and sometimes nit returning until much later. As a young art student I eventually learned that the super power of crits was not that someone focused on YOUR work, it was that you sat through 15 OTHER STUDENT’S crits and began to absorb the full value of a non-linear, project-based education. Architecture is a team sport, and it is best played in dialogue, not monologue. Finally, it is your responsibility to budget your time and have work ready to present. For example, I would much rather have you in class than have you waiting for prints to emerge from the VCC! Proofread and print your work early, show up on time for your teammates and contribute material, vocally and with constructive critical input. As the semester advances, I am much more interested in your collective input than my own voice.

### Additional Academic Integrity Course Policy and Penalty Information:

As a member of the SoA Artificial Intelligence Subcommittee, we are currently in the process of examining the reasonable use of A.I. in conducting research and the subsequent use of A.I. in creating scholarly papers and design strategies. The policy of this instructor is to pursue penalties for outright plagiarism, that is, "old-fashioned" plagiarism, and to allow for the acknowledged use of A.I.in generating new insights into art, particularly that of Marcel Duchamp. Each aper that utilized A.I. will be subject to full citations of the prompts used and a critical analysis of content. What is accurate? What is false? What has been invented?  
A grade of 0 (zero) will be given (for that assignment) for the first violation of the plagiarism policy. The student will receive a grade of F for subsequent violations. However, the use of A.I. as a research/writing tool is allowed, if acknowledge within the paper, and does not constitute the bulk of the intellectual content.  
Group work is encouraged in at least one assignment and the students will qualitatively and qualitatively assess each others' participation.
