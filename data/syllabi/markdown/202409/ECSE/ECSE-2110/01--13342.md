## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

This course introduces the major components of today’s power system such as transformers, electric machines, and transmission lines. Renewable energy sources and systems are discussed, including wind and solar energy. Integration of energy sources with the grid is addressed.

## Course Text(s)

### Text Details:

Hadi Saadat, Power System Analysis, 3rd Ed., PSA Publishing, 2012. ISBN: 9780984543809.

## Course Goals

### Goals:

In this course students will:

- Learn and apply fundamental concepts used to derive simple models of electrical power components and networks used to perform electrical energy system analysis.
- Develop a broader understanding of multiple aspects related to the design, planning and operation of electric energy systems with focus on renewable energy sources and integration of such resources into the grid.

## Course Content

### Content Details:

Introduction to Electrical Energy/Power Systems

Fundamental Principles and Computations for Power System Modeling

1. Complex power and power balance. Power factor. Complex power flow.
2. Balanced three- phase circuits. Y and ∆ connections and ∆-Y transformation.
3. Per-phase (positive sequence) analysis. Balance three-phase power. Per-unit system and change of base.

Physical Modeling of Electrical Generators, Transmission Apparatus and Networks

1. Classical generator models and parameters: salient pole and round-rotor machines.
2. Power transformers: equivalent models and parameters, three-phase transformer connections and positive-sequence models, autotransformers, tap-changing and regulating transformers, three-winding transformers.
3. Principles of Transmission Lines: line resistance, inductance, and capacitance; multi-conductor transmission lines, magnetic field induction, electrostatic induction, corona phenomena.
4. Transmission Lines Models and Phenomena: short, medium, and long line models; voltage and current wages; surge impedance loading; complex power flow; power transmission capability; line compensation.
5. Transmission line compensation, dynamic line rating, sub-synchronous resonances
6. Load modeling
7. Substations

Methods for Analysis of Power Networks in Steady State

1. Power flow analysis: rationale, solution of nonlinear algebraic equations, bus admittance matrix, power flow problem formulation, introduction to power flow solution methods.

Renewable Energy Sources and Systems

1. Wind and solar energy sources
2. Challenges with the integration of renewable energy sources with the grid.

## Learning Outcomes

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes (CLOs):

- Using first principles derive the equations to model electrical components used for energy system analysis.
- Describe, derive, and compare models of the most common equipment used in power network models.
- Construct mathematical models for computing the steady state performance of EES.
- Compute, analyze, and reflect on the performance of a power system under steady state.
- Apply a power system and general purpose software tools to analyze the performance of a power system, and evaluate it for different operating conditions.
- Reflect and analyze broader factors related to electrical power grids, as for example, policy, regulation, etc.

COLLABORATION AND ACADEMIC DISHONESTY:

Intellectual integrity and credibility are the foundation of all academic work. A violation of Academic Integrity policy is, by definition, considered a flagrant offense to the educational process. It is taken seriously by students, faculty, and Rensselaer and will be addressed in an effective manner. If found responsible for committing academic dishonesty, a student may be subject to one or both types of penalties: an academic (grade) penalty administered by the professor and/or disciplinary action through the Rensselaer judicial process described in this handbook. Three relevant academic integrity violations to emphasize include:

Collaboration: Collaboration is defined as deliberately facilitating an act of academic dishonesty in any way or form; for example, allowing another student to observe an exam paper or allowing another student to "recycle" one's old term paper or using one another's work in a paper or lab report without citing it as another's work.

Copying: Copying is defined as obtaining information pertaining to a graded exercise by deliberately observing the paper of another student; for example, noting which alternative a neighboring student has circled on a multiple-choice exam.

Plagiarism: Plagiarism is defined as representing the work or words of another as one's own through the omission of acknowledgment or reference. Examples include using sentences verbatim from a published source in a term paper without appropriate referencing, or presenting as one's own the detailed argument of a published source, or presenting as one's own electronically or digitally enhanced graphic representations from any form of media.

The Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities defines the full list of forms of Academic Dishonesty and you should make yourself familiar with these. In this class, all assignments that are turned in for a grade must represent the student’s own work. In cases where help was received, or teamwork was allowed, a notation on the assignment should indicate your collaboration. If you have any questions concerning this policy before submitting an assignment, please ask for clarification.

You cannot interact with Chegg.com or any other online/internet assignment sharing system for any graded assignment. Interaction with it results in an automatic failure for the entire course. A result of the violation using online resources to assist in graded assignments will result in a formal report. As a warning, there are ways that I can easily detect your use of these online resources that compromise academic integrity.
