## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

In this studio, we will walk through a typical structural/architectural collaboration process. The studio will take the form of a mock structural design office. Engineering students will team with Architecture students from the IDD Architect Studio to develop and document efficient, forward-thinking architectural projects. In the process Engineering and Architecture students will become familiar with the working relationship between Engineer and Architect, and gain insight into this invaluable collaborative process.

## Course Goals

### Goals:

Aims

Through the progression of the studio, students will become comfortable with the design process, and the daily tasks of a structural engineering consultant. This includes learning how to perform effectively within a team of engineers, working on a variety of projects, and the close collaborative relationship between engineer and architect (as well as other disciplines). Students will implement structural and architectural vocabulary and concepts, allowing them to exchange ideas on a technical level with fellow structural engineers and architectural collaborators. The roles of other project team members (such as owner, MEP consultant, etc.), will also be highlighted.

## Learning Outcomes

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes (CLOs):

- Become more comfortable with the structural design process by performing design-related tasks within a team
- Improve collaborative skills by working in a multi-disciplinary team of structural engineers and architects
- Gain experience in interpreting architectural designs structurally, proposing complementary structural systems to enhance the architectural expression of real-world projects
- Become comfortable with integrating peer feedback into design problems through design charettes and discussions
- Produce documentation establishing the design criteria, and describing critical structural considerations
- Implement analysis techniques, document results, and effectively interpret analysis results in conversation with team members
- Gain insight into the role of structural detailing and how it informs structural and architectural decision-making

## Grading Criteria

### Criteria Details:

Team Participation and Contribution 50%  
Final Report and Presentation 50%

A(+/-): work meets all requirements and exceeds them. Presentations are virtually flawless, complete, and finely detailed. Work exhibits professional, “museum quality” level of craft. Student has developed an individual design process that shows a high level of independent thought and rigor. Work shows evidence of intense struggle to go beyond expectations, and beyond the student’s own perceived limits of their abilities.

B(+/-): work meets all requirements. Presentations are complete and finely detailed. Work exhibits professional level of craft. Student has developed an individual design process that shows a high level of independent thought and rigor.

C(+/-) work meets minimum requirements. While presentations may be complete, student has struggled to develop an individual design process and/or is lacking in craft or design resolution

D(+/-): work is below minimum requirements but student should not be required to repeat the course. Presentations are incomplete, student has struggled to develop an individual design process and/or is lacking in craft or design resolution.

F: work is well below minimum requirements. Student does not develop adequate design process, and / or does not finish work on time.

INC: grades of “incomplete” are not given under any circumstances unless there is evidence of a medical or personal emergency. Classes / work missed due to illness must be explained with a physician’s note.

### Additional Academic Integrity Course Policy and Penalty Information:

As a student you are expected to conduct yourself in a manner that reflects the ethical ideas of the profession of architecture. Any act of academic dishonesty not only raises questions about an individual’s fitness to practice architecture, but also demeans the academic environment in which it occurred. Giving or receiving aid in examinations, and plagiarism are a violation of an assumed trust between the school and the student. Plagiarism, i.e., the presentation as one’s own work of words, drawings, ideas and opinions of someone else, is a serious instance of academic dishonesty in the context as cheating on examinations. The submission of any piece of work (written, drawn, built, or photocopied) is assumed by the school to guarantee that the thoughts and expressions in it are literally the student’s own, executed by the student.
