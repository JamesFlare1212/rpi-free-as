## Course Goals

### Goals:

This course is designed to familiarize graduate-level students with modern techniques in and associated with mass spectrometry. Material will be presented in a variety of methods including traditional lectures, in-lab demonstrations, “field trips” to nearby mass spectrometry labs/facilities, and hands-on experiments. Throughout the semester, the instructor will present historical perspectives and scientific discussion as to how and why many of the modern technologies were developed. Students will gain an appreciation for the strengths and weaknesses of mass spectrometry in the context of analytical sciences such as detection and quantification of analytes in samples as well as structural elucidation. The development of instrumentation is key in the realm of mass spectrometry; as such, a significant portion of the course will focus on ionization source/mass analyzer design as well as associated technologies including vacuum systems, pressure measurement, ion detection, and ion optics. The course will also cover the physical nature of ionization which will be presented through molecular electronic structure, dynamics, and kinetics.

## Learning Outcomes

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes (CLOs):

- Interpret mass spectra generated by different ionization sources
- Demonstrate, through written and oral examination, a proficient knowledge of modern mass spectrometry instrumentation
- Distinguish between different mass analyzer capabilities based on resolving power, acquisition rate, mass accuracy, etc.
- Explain the principles and underlying processes that govern ionization of atoms and molecules

### Other Course Policies:

Calculator: Only non-graphing calculators will be permitted during exams (scientific calculators are acceptable).

Problem Sets/Quizzes: Homework problem sets will be given throughout the semester. These problem sets will serve as aides to help students prepare for exams as well as supplement topics covered in lecture. Problem sets should be hand-written and submitted to Prof. Shelley’s mailbox (in the main Chemistry office) prior to the deadline. Late assignments will NOT be accepted without a valid excuse. In addition, announced and unannounced quizzes will be given in class; students will be given 5 to 10 minutes to complete each quiz. Often, the questions will come from material covered in recent lectures and lab experiments.

Laboratory Experiments: To strengthen concepts learned in the lectures, students in the graduate-level course (CHEM 6130) will perform at least two laboratory-based experiments/demonstrations. Experiments will be performed outside of scheduled class time or on days without a lecture. While the experiments are required for the graduate version of the course (CHEM 6130), they are entirely optional for CHEM 4130. However, performing these experiments will help strengthen concepts and provide hands-on experience with a variety of mass spectrometers. Below is a list of tentative experiments:

- Accurate mass techniques

- Electron ionization fundamentals: Appearance and Bond dissociation energies

- Understanding ion optics through SIMION simulations

- Use of high resolving power to differentiate isobaric interferences

- Determining peptide sequence with tandem MS
