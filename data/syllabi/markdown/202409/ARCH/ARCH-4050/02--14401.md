## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

#### Course Description

Cities are first and foremost social projects which, in turn, produce social products. In this course we are interested in understanding the negotiated construction of architecture, landscapes, and infrastructure, as they emerge from social and political acts, having far-reaching impacts on human and (nonhuman) ecological life. We are concerned with becoming conscious of the many ongoing and contested theories of the urban and of “nature” and the environment. We acknowledge that there cannot be any single ‘unifying’ urban or environmental theory which will encompass the depth of understanding of the subject matter in all contexts and times. Rather, in this course students will be provided with a diverse set of texts and authors from which to position themselves within ongoing debates of what constitutes urban theory and how one may approach studying urban and environmental conditions in practice and in scholarly research.

How to write proper bibliographic citations:

- Always put in quotation any phrase copied from another source. This wording can be from a PDF, book, journal, magazine,etcetera.
- Wikipedia cannot be cited as a reference in academic writing. Wikipedia may be used for fact-checking, in which case it is useful. ## Course Goals

### Goals:

**Goals and Objectives**

Pedagogical Overview Consciously or unconsciously, when humans have established settlements of any scale, those interventions have always had an intimate connection to and impact upon the geography and environments in which they occur. One goal of the course is to develop an understanding of the works of architecture, landscape, and urbanism as historically-produced embodiments of collective hopes, desires, and anxieties, as much as the unique visions of individual authors. Another goal is to consider how the history of these ideas continue to bear on the globalized context of contemporary urban discourse. Themes we will address include the relationship between ideal visions & material realities; between architectural form & social change; the role of technologies, modes of production, and cultural movements in the production of urban space. This course will ask students to question the roles and responsibilities of Architects, Urbanists, and Landscape Architects in response to constructing in a variety of environmental and social conditions in highly diverse urban contexts.

#### Objectives Outline

1. Expose students to exemplary urban design, landscape architecture, and ecological stewardship projects, ideas, and concepts.
2. Offer students critical and analytical methodologies for establishing and organizing original research for urban analysis and urban design.
3. Provide strategies for the documentation and presentation of original urban analysis and design research.
4. Create a research compendium of all final projects, course writing, visualizations, and original research completed in the seminar.

## Course Content

### Content Details:

**Content**  
This course is about learning how to identify and unpack the cultural values, in terms of ideologies and policies, that have led to the construction of various landscapes and cities. Readings and visual resources will be shared from which students will engage in forms of critical dialogue with the course topics, primarily through written responses. The Instructors will create presentations that lead the discussion on various topics over the course of the semester. This course serves as both an introductory seminar on urbanism, as well as a workshop for students to develop unique and personalized research on a range of issues that affect: cities, construction, infrastructure, the environment, and landscapes.

“For most of known human history, cities and landscapes have been generated all but unconsciously, through a panoply of normative social practices that persistently refuse precise coordination. This is to say that although, at various moments, urbanism has been—and continues to be—planned, the practices entailed do not constitute a unitary concern; instead, the outlines of ‘the urban’ are reliant on the good graces and chance intersections of political, economic, social, aesthetic, and technical concerns. What, then, is the significance of knowledge about cities for the contemporary architect? Though the city can be seen from numerous perspectives, it is generally held that human agency is the primary force shaping them. Yet once shaped, how are they controlled, managed, altered, and renewed?” -- T. Verebes

The course outlines the various patterns and processes through which architects, landscape architects, and urbanists have responded-to and influenced pressing demographic, environmental, and global issues within the contexts of the many and varied forms of urbanity.

#### Content Outline

1. Vocabulary for understanding urban and landscape theories and practical knowledge for application in the field.
2. Historical trajectories of urbanism, landscape, and environmentalism which include: comparative studies of similarity and difference in urban and environmental contexts, close readings of case studies of specific urban conditions and environments and landscapes in a specific historical period; as well as the influence of the design professions within urban morphologies and the deployment of environmental interventions.
3. Contemporary urban and landscape design strategies for architects to consider when dealing with 21st century urban scenarios, especially those involving landscapes, urbanism, social and environmental justice.

### Other Course Policies:

#### Assignments & Grading

Location Weekly readings will be posted on the course LMS site or delivered to students via email. By enrolling in the course, you are automatically given access to the course LMS.

Expectations There will be approximately 50-100 pages of reading per topic. Lectures will be difficult to follow without knowledge from the readings. Your Weekly Critical Reflections are reliant upon your takeaways and analysis of the reading material.

Please take note: It will be virtually impossible to catch up on late reading responses or missed Lectures.

Reading Best Practices Some of the texts are difficult, and will require a second reading. For best results: first skim and mark key points in your initial read through, follow through by rereading a second time more carefully and critically while taking detailed notes. Best practices include reading with a pen and/or highlighter to mark your major observations and to take notes in the margins. It is also recommended that you keep one ‘tab’ on the main text, while at the same time keeping the footnotes nearby. Footnotes help you further understand where the author is coming from and where the ideas they are writing about are derived. At times authors will offer even more insightful information in their footnotes than in the main text itself.

Critique, Not Summarization Your reading responses will be viewed not only as solutions, but as methods of framing (non-obvious/oblique/etc) questions. Responses should be thoughtful and articulate. Do not summarize the texts. Responses must be your own thoughts and questions about the ideas the authors are addressing.

### Additional Academic Integrity Course Policy and Penalty Information:

Student-teacher relationships are built on trust. For example, students must trust that teachers have made appropriate decisions about the structure and content of the courses that they teach, and teachers must trust that the assignments that students turn in are their own. Acts, which violate this trust, undermine the educational process. The Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities defines various forms of Academic Dishonesty and you should make yourself familiar with these. In this course, assignments that are turned in for a grade must represent the student’s own work. For work that is specifically assigned as collaborative, all parties are encouraged to apportion the work equitably, may consult with people outside the group who have insights or knowledge of value to the study, and must acknowledge the sources of such collaboration and/or assistance. No form of academic dishonesty - cheating, plagiarism, or failure to follow critical guidelines in coursework - will be tolerated. Submission of any assignment that is in violation of this policy will result in a penalty of an ‘F’ grade for the given assignment. If you have any question concerning this policy before submitting an assignment, please ask for clarification.

Intellectual integrity and credibility are the foundation of all academic work. Academic dishonesty is, by definition, considered a flagrant offense to the educational process. It is taken seriously by students, faculty, and Rensselaer and will be addressed in an effective manner. If found in violation of academic dishonesty policy, students may be subject to two types of penalties: the instructor administers an academic (grade) penalty, and the student may be subject to the procedures and penalties of the student judicial system outlined in this handbook. Since academic dishonesty is a violation of the Grounds for Disciplinary Action, the student may be subject to any of the following sanctions: disciplinary warning; disciplinary probation; disciplinary suspension, expulsion and/or alternative actions as agreed on by the student and hearing officer. It should be noted that no student who allegedly commits academic dishonesty will be able to drop or change the grade option for the course in question. The definitions and examples presented below are samples of types of academic dishonesty and are not to be construed as an exhaustive or exclusive list. The academic dishonesty policy applies to all students, undergraduate and graduate, and to scholarly pursuits and research. Additionally, attempts to commit academic dishonesty or to assist in the commission or attempt of such an act are also violations of this policy.

Academic Fraud • The alteration of documentation relating to the grading process. For example, changing exam solutions to negotiate for a higher grade or tampering with an instructor’s grade book.

Collaboration • Deliberate facilitation of academic dishonesty in any form. For example, allowing another student to observe an exam paper or allowing another student to “recycle” one’s old term paper or using one another’s work in a paper or lab report without citing it as another’s work.

Copying • Obtaining information pertaining to a graded exercise by deliberately observing the paper of another student. For example, noting which alternative a neighboring student has circled on a multiple-choice exam.

Fabrication • Unauthorized falsification or invention of any information in an academic exercise. For example, use of “bought” or “ready-made” term papers, or falsifying lab records or reports.

Plagiarism • Representing the work or words of another as one’s own through the omission of acknowledgment or reference. For example, using sentences verbatim from a published source in a term paper without appropriate referencing, or presenting as one’s own the detailed argument of a published source, or presenting as one’s own electronically or digitally enhanced graphic representations from any form of media.

Sabotage • Destruction of another student’s work. For example, destroying a model, lab experiment, computer program, or term paper developed by another student.

Substitution • Utilizing a proxy, or acting as a proxy, in any academic exercise. For example, taking an exam for another student or having a homework assignment done by someone else.
