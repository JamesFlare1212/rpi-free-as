## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

This course, The Ethos of Architecture, is a necessary  
introduction for beginning architecture students into the  
theory and practice of architecture across the wide spectrum  
of issues, concerns, and values that constitute the discourse of  
the building design disciplines, practices, and their associated  
professions. It is important for each of you to determine  
your own position within this discourse. In this respect, this  
course will present you with an abundance of information.  
Sometimes, it may seem overwhelming. The importance of  
this information may not register with you right away or even  
by the end of the semester. It may not register with you this  
year or even by the time you graduate. It may not register  
with you until you’re in your thirties or your forties. However,  
it will eventually register with you and it will do so uniquely,  
which is the point. The information of this course is not  
information for information’s sake but what you will ultimately  
make of it; how it registers will be on your terms not mine nor  
anyone else’s terms. Therefore, perhaps the most important  
part of such an ethos is the questions you will have to ask  
both of yourself and of others. The answers to such questions  
will allow the information of this course and all your other  
encounters with architecture to become uniquely your own.

## Course Goals

### Goals:

One thing is certain about the study, design, and production of  
architecture; the architect can never take anything for granted.  
Therefore, an essential goal of this course is to stimulate  
your curiosity about architecture and the larger world and to  
introduce you to key issues of architecture regarding space,  
time, form, critique, technology, aesthetics, societal and  
cultural contexts, etc. Crucial to this goal is to encourage you  
to pursue and develop creatively your own singular vision and  
voice in architecture throughout your education and practice.  
Fundamental to this process is for you to cultivate the capacity  
to challenge, question, and critique your own subjectivities,  
biases, and presuppositions as well as those of others.  
To summarize, the general goals for this course as well as for  
the breadth of your architectural education are to introduce  
and inculcate within you the culture of architecture. Five  
broad principles are predicates for the culture of architecture:  
a. CREATIVITY is essential to the conceptualization, analysis,  
design, and production of architecture.  
b. CRITICAL THOUGHT & INQUIRY regarding the range of  
issues that affect architecture encourage independent thinking  
in the design process.  
c. CURIOSITY about ideas and the larger world leads to your  
self-motivation and taking initiative to identify and respond to  
these concerns and to go beyond them.  
d. COLLABORATION with others is a necesary and legitimate  
means to discover the world beyond one’s own horizons.  
e. COMMUNICATION of your ideas and creations is crucial  
to the success of the other four principles. Precision and  
intellectual integrity are necessary adjuncts to coherent  
communication.  
None of these five principles, CREATIVITY, CRITICAL  
THOUGHT & INQUIRY, CURIOSITY, COLLABORATION, and  
COMMUNICATION, exists in isolation from the others. Be  
open to their unexpected interaction.

### Academic Policies:

- ### Academic Integrity

  Student-teacher relationships are built on trust. For example, students must trust that teachers have made appropriate decisions about the structure and content of the courses they teach, and teachers must trust that the assignments that students turn in are their own. Acts that violate this trust undermine the educational process. The Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities and The Graduate Student Supplement define various forms of Academic Dishonesty and you should make yourself familiar with these. In this class, all assignments that are turned in for a grade must represent the student’s own work. In cases where help was received, or teamwork was allowed, a notation on the assignment should indicate your collaboration.

  Violations of academic integrity may also be reported to the appropriate Dean (Dean of Students for undergraduate students or the Dean of Graduate Education for graduate students, respectively).

  If you have any question concerning this policy before submitting an assignment, please ask for clarification. In addition, you can visit the following site for more information on our Academic Integrity Policy: [Students Rights, Responsibilities, and Judicial Affairs](https://info.rpi.edu/dean-students/student-rights-responsibilities-and-judicial-affairs).

### Additional Academic Integrity Course Policy and Penalty Information:

The Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and  
Responsibilities defines various forms of Academic Dishonesty  
and procedures for responding to them. Academic dishonesty  
violates the trust between students and teachers. Students  
must trust that teachers have made appropriate decisions  
about the structure and content of the courses they teach.  
Teachers must trust that the assignments students turn in are  
their own performance. Violations of this trust undermines  
the educational process. Cheating, plagiarism [defined as the  
lack of attribution or citation of the work of others], willful  
damage or theft of another person’s work violate academic  
integrity. The first instance of the breach of this trust will  
merit a failing grade in this course and the violator[s] will be  
reported to the Dean of Students for disciplinary action. The  
second infraction will result in name[s] of the violator[s] being  
submitted to the Dean of Students for judicial review and  
possible expulsion from the Institute.
