## Course Content

### Content Details:

**Spolia-Spolia** Design Seminar

**Course Overview**

Spolia, spolia-spolia, spolia over spolia over spolia over spolia, perpetually unfolding.

01 Spolia (from the latin *spolium*: spoils) are recognizable architectural elements (or groups of elements, or parts of elements, or groups of parts of elements), often made of stone, that are encountered and/or looted from old structures and used anew for the purpose of ornamentation in an altogether different architectural artifact, resonating with, celebrating, establishing dominion or appropriation of, or overshadowing their provenance, and fundamentally carrying within them multiple allegories that are projected as enigmas. Spolia is applied onto a larger building, which in principle operates as a passive background to its figural presence, yet sometimes it competes with it in a struggle of equals, making it difficult to recognize which piece works as a figure onto which one working as a background. Equally, but more boldly, spolia constitute material organizations that, through a profanation process that involves the evaluation, recognition, decontextualization, segregation, categorization, organization, and repositioning of elements, are migrated from one building into others, to trigger and put at work a perpetually incomplete process of architectural transformation. Spolia carry (or rather, miscarry), through that migration process, an active form of knowledge (a form of knowledge that *does* things to matter), turning the latent capabilities of an architectural material (distorted by the migration process) into an actual architectural force and a source of a new form of sense that transfers vital material, organizational, and cultural impulses and messages from object to object. Spolia is live material, whose inertness is overtly challenged, recharged, turned into a force, and effectively made a vector of change. Spolia do not simply decorate architectures that persist as such but transform the architecture itself of the building into which it is embedded. In its subtle versions, it incorporates disturbances that, because of their minuteness and isolation from its context, create sparks that trigger an entire revolution in the concept of a piece of architecture. And in its wilder ones, it redefines its very organization.

02 Spolia-spolia. The incorporation of spolia into an already spoiled building structure introduces, in this context, a second level of ornamentation (the ornamentation of an ornamentation) and/or of transformation (the transformation of a transformation). This involves that the simple (although often ambiguous) figure-background relation of the single spolia is turned into a self-contradictory manifold of figure-background and figure-figure relations that alternate or overlap, and disrupt the status of both figure and background, which then ambiguously turn into one another in an inscrutable manner. A spolia-spolia operation altogether blurs the distinction between background and figure. It is not a matter of relative dominance, but a matter of consecutiveness. What comes before and after, if one becomes the other in a process? What is fundamentally lost, because of this ambiguity, is the background, the plane of support, the canvas, which sets back proportionally to the degree in which the uncertainty grows. This loss creates a point of no return to the source, by which, when iterated, the transformation process is turned into a lineage (not only a segment) of differentiation, flattening the historical process lying behind a linear idea of architectural evolution, and turning it into an instantaneous action where history happens all at once, thus squeezing the temporal unfolding of architecture as a discipline into the suddenness and objectual condition of a single building artifact. The spolia-spolia-spolia-spolia complex can be thus seen as a strong authorial trans-historical act, the consistency of which is artificial and deliberate, the form of which can be intensified, manipulated, turned upside down, looped back, skipped over itself, and become redundant at wish, and the content of which sets allegories free from any remnant or residue of a linear and progressive historical determinism. Such artificiality opens the process of differentiation to procedural thinking and flips the dependence of architecture both on the mandates of linear historical narratives and on the dependence of figure-ground compositional methods.

03 The seminar explores, systematizes, and unfolds the spolia-spolia method as a mode to create architectural organizations and spaces through iterative indexical differentiation by means of the montage of architectural images (figures, in principle) onto architectural images (backgrounds). A series of more-or-less complex but discrete organizational materials coming from different (but not remote) buildings (of a relatively close age and the same typology), are spoiled off their provenance and incorporated in a spatial organization, which works as background (or rather, a battlefield) of a progressively but not linearly transformed iterative process. For this purpose, the iterative indexical process operates alternately according to two modes: sequential-accumulative and parallel-synthetic, redefining the type by saturating it with foreign organizational modalities. Each iteration consists of the statement of the architectural type displayed in an image, a quick analysis of alternative organizational models within the typology, the recognition of a series of material compounds in other projects that carry transformative ideas, the construction of a taxonomy of figures and varieties, the selection of one of them under a hypothesis of transformation, the thorough mapping of singularities in the background image, and the systematic running of the figures across those, populating the background image according to roughly defined rules. Iterations accumulate onto the spatial-organizational configuration, increasing its saturation while avoiding the erasure of previous operations, rather complexifying their reach and spectrum of sense. Each iteration works both as an autonomous operation, adding a new layer to the organization, and cumulatively, having the new layer relate to the immediately previous state of things of the organization, rather than to the alleged typological purity of the initial background. The autonomy of the indexical operations enable additional operations of resetting the order of the sequence, not as a whole, but partially, thus intertwining the iterative linearity of the process of accumulation.

04 The background image consists of a singular, conventional, wide, all-encompassing, single-vanishing-point, human-centric perspective of a certain spatial configuration that carries within it (particularly around its centre) the spatiality of a public space, thus turned architectural interior (even if exterior or semi-exterior in nature). Public interiors come from six typologies ubiquitously present in central Rome: piazzas, courtyards, atriums, naves, facades, and landmarks, all of which are assumed to carry the potential of introducing a public space node (precisely located at the centre of the image) in the continuum of the city, thus tearing and opening the city fabric through a singular operation. This background constitutes the base material to be transformed, the purpose being the deviation of the type though figural saturation, the opening of its operative spectrum as an architectural weapon, and the configuration of a singularity transcendent of its given conceptual framework (the alleged essential invariance of the type, naturalized by its concept and carried by its form). More specifically, the aims of the iterative spolia-spolia process are to put in crisis the essentiality of the type, to recognize the transformative power of the organizational modalities (commonly disregarded as invisible nuances, irrelevant accidents, or undesired errors), to stretch the reach of adaptive and transformative capabilities the type, to go beyond its fixed, however blandly, conceptual limits, and to create a new and more complex type by means of a new synthesis. The created image-based proposition, in this context, works both as one among many operative samples of the systematic variations of an architectural model, and as a lighthouse that guides its potential transformations, consequently insinuating and illuminating their horizon.

The work is individual, in the context of teams, and uses image material collected on site in Rome, attentively edited and dissected, and thoroughly compiled and archived, from the typologically framed and modally defined case-study buildings provided by the course. The resulting material is complementary to that produced by the Design Studio.

**Operativity**

Spolia-Spolia consists of an iterative procedure in which modally foreign but typologically consistent architectural materials, in the form of images (regarded in principle as figures) are integrated into an image of an architectural project, to be transformed, which carries within key ideas of its spatiality (regarded in principle as background). Both image background and image figures belong to three case studies that are framed within a typology. Students work with a base image and iteratively integrate into it a series of diverse figures of the other two from the same typology. Visits to the case studies take place during the first two weeks of classes, in conjunction with the Studio visits. Students produce a photographic survey of their case study, as well as of the case studies of their teammates.

## Learning Outcomes

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes (CLOs):

- 01 Research. 01a_Ability to use/develop specialized knowledge. Students live in Rome throughout the semester. Through the direct interaction with canonical architectural pieces, the involvement with urban conditions and phenomena, and the fluid access to museums, artwork, and bibliographical resources in the city, students augment their disciplinary baggage and construct an ability to attentively observe and document multiple forms and scales of knowledge about buildings, cultural artifacts and sites that nurtures their imagination of the architectural project with specific observations. 01b_Ability to use / develop broad and integrative knowledge. Students live in Rome throughout the semester, engaging in a culturally immersive process that, aside primarily consisting of regular visits to Renaissance and Baroque buildings in the city center and of participating in real time in the cultural life of the city in the context of its omnipresent history, is fostered by a series of field trips to the outskirts of the city, to Tuscany, and to Sicily, providing a rich and multilayered context of historical and cultural significance to the architectural projects, which deepens the referentiality of their projects.
- 02 Intellectual-visual design skills. 02a_Ability to design with imaginative openness. Students are conceptually and visually nurtured to develop an ability to ground design decisions on a hands-on visual approach to architecture as material. This approach is articulated by visually studying and literally using the organizational knowledge embedded in existing buildings as sources of design intelligence. 02b_Ability to design with communicative fluency. Students are challenged to fluidly communicate with (and be influenced by) different interlocutors at different stages of the course, from local professors of various backgrounds to student colleagues of other universities in the Rome Center, to guest professors that participate in reviews and the lecture series program.
- 03 Design execution. 03a_Midterm Review. Students demonstrate the ability to implement the learning outcomes of an iterative process of image-based design in a sophisticated and compelling way through a mid-term presentation to guest jurors and an intermediate submission of the production, including: a portfolio presentation and submission containing organizational descriptions, photographic surveys, image gathering and manipulation, and an archive of source material. 03b_Final Review. Students demonstrate the ability to implement the learning outcomes of an iterative process of image-based design in a sophisticated and compelling way through a final presentation to guest jurors and an intermediate submission of the production, including: a portfolio presentation and submission containing organizational descriptions, photographic surveys, image organization and classification, and an archive of source material.

**Pin-ups and Reviews**

Students are periodically required to present their work in a formal review process. Presentations are a critical part of the architectural education. They are opportunities to judge the project and critically evaluate the design process. It may be appropriate at points in the semester to evaluate only selected projects to convey information relevant to the entire class. In this situation, it is important for each student to witness and participate in the review process. Even if his/her project is not reviewed, the criticism and discussions inform the evaluation of his/her progress and design. Failure to attend one or more pin-ups for the full class period may result in a grade deduction.

**Desk Critiques**

The Seminar environment involves two-way communication. The role of the Seminar professor is to evolve and strengthen the student’s design process. Every time the student meets with the faculty, he/she must have new iterations of work based on previous conversations. If the student has not produced new work, the faculty reserves the right to skip the critique and continue with other students who have produced new material.

### Additional Academic Integrity Course Policy and Penalty Information:

**Academic Integrity**

The Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities defines various forms of Academic Dishonesty and procedures for responding to them. Academic dishonesty violates the trust between students and teachers. Students must trust that teachers have made appropriate decisions about the structure and content of the courses they teach. Teachers must trust that the assignments students turn in are their own performance. Violations of this trust undermines the educational process.

**Policy on collaboration and cheating/plagiarism**

Cheating, plagiarism (defined as the lack of attribution or citation of the work of others), willful damage or theft of another person’s work violate academic integrity. The first instance of the breach of this trust merits a failing grade in this course and the violator will be reported to the Dean of Students for disciplinary action.

Interdisciplinary collaboration is a critical component of the course. All assignments that are turned in for a grade must accurately represent the student’s own work, and properly cite authorship of work that was not solely completed by the student. Any misrepresentation of work is evaluated and could result in a failing grade for the assignment. A subsequent infraction by the student results in a failing grade for the course.
