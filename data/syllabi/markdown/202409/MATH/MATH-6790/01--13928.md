## Course Content

### Content Details:

Aim and Topics

This class will present tools of mathematical analysis used in applications and attempt to dispel the mystery of the gobbledygook associated with it. It will attempt to achieve two somewhat disparate goals: (i) Introduce rigorous mathematical thought via carefully chosen topics that progress through elementary analysis starting with number sequences and ending with uniform convergence. Select proofs and the quickest possible sequence of theorems/results will be presented that bring the class along this path. (ii) Survey results from real and functional analysis to bring awareness of them and make possible their use in other classes and research. This part will be be largely heuristic and will present abstract results only in connection with concrete applications. Any proving will be done in applying the abstract results.

The class will start with a review of some important calculus results, viewed from the point of analysis. It will then continue to cover topics that may range from Lebesgue integration, functional approximation, Banach and Hilbert spaces, and various types of convergence. The applications will range from convex analysis, random walks and Wiener processes, distributions, probability, to quantum mechanics, existence theorems in odes and PDEs, and spectral theory.

There are a few topics that I will almost completely avoid in this class, so as not to distract from the more important technical classes like those in computation, optimization, methods of applied mathematics, or partial differential equations. Primarily, these topics include linear algebra, calculus of variations, detailed studies of Sturm-Liouville problems, and Green’s functions.

## Learning Outcomes

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes (CLOs):

- You will be able to prove results from elementary mathematical analysis and apply them to other branches of mathematics.
- You will be able to use results from Lebesgue integration to derive results in mathematical fields such as Fourier and functional analysis, as well as probability theory.
- You will learn basics facts from functional analysis and will be able to apply them to problems in differential equations, optimization, numerical analysis, and quantum mechanics
- You will be able to read a broad range of (applied) mathematics papers without being bogged down by jargon and lack of understanding of concepts in analysis.

## Policies

### Other Course Policies:

Logistical structure of the class

Piazza: Piazza will be the main forum for all the communication outside of classroom and office hours, and this will include the announcements. I will also put the daily build of the class notes, the homework assignments, due dates, and perhaps some pages from textbooks, on Piazza.

Questions in Addition to Office Hours: If it’s not class time or office hour and you want an answer more-or-less right-away, please ask the question on Piazza. You can use the “private question” mode on Piazza if necessary. In case of an emergency (that does not include homework questions), we may be able to set up a WebEx appointment in my personal room, rensselaer.webex.com/meet/kovacg.

Homework: A problem or two will be assigned and graded weekly. This will be one of the main means of assessment of your work, so you should put a real effort into it. I encourage collaboration on the homework. However, copying is forbidden. In other words, you can exchange ideas as much as you want, but, in the end, you must write your work up on your own without looking at anybody else’s work. Copying off the internet is also forbidden as is using AI helpers such as ChatGPT.

Gradescope: Please submit your homework through Gradescope. You should submit a single scanned or typeset .pdf file. Place different problems on different (sets of) pages, and connect the pages to the corresponding problems in Gradescope. Please submit the homework on time. Late homework will not be accepted without prior arrangements.

Exams

I am planning to give two in-class exams, a midterm and a final. If I determine that your work on the homework is sufficiently good and you are working sufficiently independently, I will cancel the exams. I will let you know about 4 weeks into the semester. If given, the exams will constitute 70% of the grade.

### Academic Policies:

- ### Academic Integrity

  Student-teacher relationships are built on trust. For example, students must trust that teachers have made appropriate decisions about the structure and content of the courses they teach, and teachers must trust that the assignments that students turn in are their own. Acts that violate this trust undermine the educational process. The Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities and The Graduate Student Supplement define various forms of Academic Dishonesty and you should make yourself familiar with these. In this class, all assignments that are turned in for a grade must represent the student’s own work. In cases where help was received, or teamwork was allowed, a notation on the assignment should indicate your collaboration.

  Violations of academic integrity may also be reported to the appropriate Dean (Dean of Students for undergraduate students or the Dean of Graduate Education for graduate students, respectively).

  If you have any question concerning this policy before submitting an assignment, please ask for clarification. In addition, you can visit the following site for more information on our Academic Integrity Policy: [Students Rights, Responsibilities, and Judicial Affairs](https://info.rpi.edu/dean-students/student-rights-responsibilities-and-judicial-affairs).
