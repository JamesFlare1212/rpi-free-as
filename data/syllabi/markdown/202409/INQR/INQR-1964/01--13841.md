## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

This is a contemporary culture course focusing on current political and social issues and their representation in the news media in the United States and in contemporary culture, such as films, exhibitions, and works of art. Although the course’s primary focus is the U.S., course subject matter will be set within historical and global frameworks.

## Course Content

### Content Details:

COURSE TOPICS:  
Topics will be “intersectional” (in the broadest sense of this term, meaning being inter-related) and will include the following topics, and more:  
-- Introduction: Modernity (late 1700s to today), technology, and news media  
--News Media Theory and Art: selected theories of news media; art works (films, etc. ) and artistic-practice related to news media  
--Political Elections: Political Advertisements, Presidential Campaigns, and the News Media  
--War: Internationalism and International Conflict and War --Identities/Communities: Race/Ethnicity/Gender/Women/”Differently abled”/”LGBTQIA….,” Age-related (“generational) difference in the use of news media  
--The Environment and Climate Change  
--Other issues: Immigration, Health, Poverty, Education, Wealth/Poverty, More diversities (both visible and “invisible), AI, “Current Events”…

## Grading Criteria

### Criteria Details:

**Grading/Evaluation of presentations and essays:**  
A: Excellent presentations and essays, distinguished by originality of topic, and depth of research. Essays have good grammar, are spell-checked, and formatting requirements are met.  
B: Good presentations and essays, with solid research. Essays have good grammar, are spell-checked, and formatting requirements met.  
C: Minimal work on presentations and essays. Essays have satisfactory grammar, may have some issues with bibliography, citations, spelling, and formatting requirements.  
D: Poor quality work on presentations and essays with the latter having incorrect content, insufficient length, sloppy bibliographies and citations, and incorrect formatting, spelling, and grammar.  
F: Extremely inadequate work on presentations and essays, or incomplete assignments. Essays with incorrect content, insufficient length, incorrect bibliographies, no citations when necessary, incorrect formatting, spelling, and grammar.  
Students are not permitted to re-do or re-submit papers or assessments. However, if improvements are made during the semester, there will be a strong emphasis on this improved work when determining the final grade.

If you have questions about email formatting or protocols, just ask me. Some of the standard practices of academic/professional emails are as follows: Some of the standard practices of academic/professional emails are as follows:** a) Always sign your name.  
For standard professional/academic contexts, initially sign with your full name. If you are very familiar with your correspondent, then, of course, you can sign with just your first name. (I have too often received an email from a student, with no signature, and  
have no idea who is it from, and I cannot match their email address.)  
b) Addressee’s name and salutation/greeting (optional) of some kind that includes the name of the person you are writing is usually standard in professional/academic contexts—especially when first corresponding. “Formality first” is always the best formula, then as the correspondence/relationship develops, more casual salutations can be appropriate:  
—Traditionally, “Dear” has been the formal standard introductory address, followed by full name (of if they have a title, with title): “Dear Prof. Mary Anne Staniszewski.” In university settings, for example, “Hi" or “Hello Professor,” is good option as well. In the United States usually “Professor” is the standard address. If someone has a PhD, and is not a professor, Dr. is standard. Not all instructors have doctorates, but they may be addressed as professors. “Hey,” for example, is really not appropriate for professional situations, unless these people become your very dear casual friends.  
—In recent years, in many professional contexts, the “Dear” or “Hi” is not included and the email begins with just the person’s name: “Professor Staniszewski,” “Dr. Staniszewski,” or, as suggested for this course, “Prof. Mary Anne.” Just using the name without a formal salutation has become a standard method of address for many professional contexts.  
c) Sign off, closing. This is not as essential, but this does shape and can strongly modify your message. Nothing but your name is fine, but “best regards,” “regards,” “thanks,” sincerely” (I use the latter for recommendations) are some common closings.  
d) Always address the person you are writing according to the way they sign their message to you. If a person signs their initial response to your correspondence with just their first name, for example, then you can follow suit.
