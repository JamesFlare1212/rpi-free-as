## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

This course will provide an introduction to moral philosophy. We will study philosophical views about the nature, scope and foundations of morality, as well as specific contemporary moral questions. Philosophical questions we will address include: What is morality? What is a life lived well? Does morality depend on religion? What is the relationship between morality and self-interest? Do I have a moral duty to help the less fortunate? We will also examine the two major ethical camps in normative ethics: consequentialism and deontological ethics. Finally, we will take a look at morality itself, questioning whether objective moral truths exist at all and whether or not we can ever have moral progress. Emphasis is placed on identifying, evaluating and constructing careful and well thought-out moral arguments.

## Course Text(s)

## Learning Outcomes

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes (CLOs):

- Students should develop an explicit rather than tacit understanding of the ethical issues.
- Students should be able to develop some views about the ethics. While some of these problems are very abstract, others present (almost daily) dilemmas and so students ought to leave the course better prepared to think effectively about these problems.
- Develop the ability to communicate, in both oral and written form, clear and effective argumentation.
- Distinguish and evaluate different philosophical arguments.

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes Assessment Measures:

Regular quizzes on reading assignments. One group oral presentation project. Either two written take-home exams, or one argumentative essay.

## Grading Criteria

### Criteria Details:

Grades will be determined as follows. There are two tracks, or pathways, through which 40% you’re your course grade will be determined.

The first track involves completing two written take-home exams. Each take home exam will be worth 20% of your final grade. This track is designed for students who want to achieve a basic mastery in the subject matter of the course without devoting significant time to developing original thought regarding ethics. This may be desirable for students whose schedules are over-loaded with other difficult courses requiring large amounts of creativity and individual problem solving, or for students who are struggling mentally in these trying times with issues like anxiety, loss of loved ones, or a low sense of wellbeing or motivation. The idea is that for students in this kind of position, more numerous but smaller and structured assignments will help them to be successful.

The second track involves writing an argumentative essay instead of completing take-home exams. There will be two of these papers. Each count as 30% of your final grade. These are two argumentative paper assignments, on a topic of your choice. The paper is 4-6 pages in length. I will provided suggested topics, but you will be free to write on a topic of your choice so long as it is obviously related to our course content. I require that students writing papers meet with me at least once to discuss their topic and how they plan to approach the essay.

Each student will participate in a final group presentation project worth 40% of the final grade. The presentation is 25 minutes, followed by 15 minutes or so of Q&A.

Lastly, exceptional participation with lecture content will count as a bonus, up to 1/3 of a letter grade at the instructor's discretion (for example, a maximum bonus could raise a B+ to an A-).

## Policies

### Attendance Policy:

Attendance is mandatory and will be recorded each class. Each unexcused absence beyond the second will result in the subtraction of three percentage points for the final course grade. For example, if you have a final grade of 90% but have three unexcused absences, then your final grade will be lowered to 87%. Of course, not all absences are unexcused. An absence is excused if (a) you let me know you will be out of class beforehand and we plan accordingly or, if that is not possible, as soon as you can afterward and (b) you are absent for a reason that is either documented or can be documented. I reserve the right to request documentation. For example: Court date, death in the family, student/athlete competition could be excused with proper documentation. Woke up late, extended vacation, didn't feel well, or no documentation would be an unexcused absence. Of course, none of this applies to students who need to take the course asynchronously.

### Academic Policies:

- ### Academic Integrity

  Student-teacher relationships are built on trust. For example, students must trust that teachers have made appropriate decisions about the structure and content of the courses they teach, and teachers must trust that the assignments that students turn in are their own. Acts that violate this trust undermine the educational process. The Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities and The Graduate Student Supplement define various forms of Academic Dishonesty and you should make yourself familiar with these. In this class, all assignments that are turned in for a grade must represent the student’s own work. In cases where help was received, or teamwork was allowed, a notation on the assignment should indicate your collaboration.

  Violations of academic integrity may also be reported to the appropriate Dean (Dean of Students for undergraduate students or the Dean of Graduate Education for graduate students, respectively).

  If you have any question concerning this policy before submitting an assignment, please ask for clarification. In addition, you can visit the following site for more information on our Academic Integrity Policy: [Students Rights, Responsibilities, and Judicial Affairs](https://info.rpi.edu/dean-students/student-rights-responsibilities-and-judicial-affairs).
