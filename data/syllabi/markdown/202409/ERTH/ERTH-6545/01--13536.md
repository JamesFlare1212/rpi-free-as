## Course Text(s)

### Text Details:

This course does not have a required textbook. Organic geochemistry is a diverse and rapidly evolving field, therefore course content and learning materials are derived from primary literature and chapters from several different textbooks. Required readings and other essential resources will be made available in LMS. Major textbook resources include, but are not limited to:

Killops, S., Killops, V. (2005) *Introduction to Organic Geochemistry, Second Edition*

Bianchi, T.S., Canuel, E.A. (2011) *Chemical Biomarkers in Aquatic Systems*

Schwarzenbach, R.P., Gschwend, P.M., Imboden, D.M. (1993) *Environmental Organic Chemistry*

Turekian, K., Holland, H. (2014) *Treatise on Geochemistry, Second Edition*

## Learning Outcomes

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes (CLOs):

- • Assess how organic molecules behave in the environment based on their chemical structure • Identify environmental conditions which favor the production and accumulation of organic matter • Infer conditions under which organic matter was deposited using different biomarkers • Describe factors controlling the long-term storage of organic matter and formation of fossil fuels • Explain how natural and anthropogenic processes control or alter organic matter and carbon cycling • Demonstrate knowledge of different analytical techniques used to quantify/characterize organic matter • Discover how environmental characteristics impact organic matter in aquatic environments

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes Assessment Measures:

**In-class activities (45%)** – Throughout the semester, students will participate in several group activities (denoted in *italics* in the Course Calendar below) that are designed to apply or further explore topics covered during lectures. During each activity, the graduate student will be expected to lead their working group by coming prepared to class, facilitating discussions, answering questions, keeping time, etc. Undergraduate group members are expected to come prepared to class, actively participate in group discussions, and assist in completing the assignments. Note that not all groups will have a graduate student this semester – Prof. Wagner will randomize groups each week. Each group will submit assignments associated with each activity to Gradescope before the end of the class period. All members of each group will receive the same grade. The lowest activity assignment grade per student will be dropped.

**Quizzes (25%)** – There will be three quizzes during the semester. Students will have at least one week to take each quiz and can reference their class notes or any other resources to help them answer the questions. Although the quizzes will not be explicitly cumulative, concepts learned and tested will build upon each other over the course of the semester. The quizzes will be completed via Gradescope and students will work independently to complete each one. The lowest quiz grade per student will be dropped.

**Biomarker molecular tales (8%)** – This project uses illustrated storytelling to tell the tale of a biomarker of your choosing! Biomarkers are like “molecular fossils” and each tell a story about carbon source and/or environmental conditions during/after deposition. Each student will select one biomarker within the first few weeks of the semester. Each student will work independently to create an informative and scientifically accurate illustration that conveys key information on the source, structure, and environmental behavior of the biomarker. You may seek inspiration from the “Molecular Tales of Marine Dissolved Organic Matter” zine we read at the beginning of the course or chart your own creative path. Please note that you do not need to be an artist to tell a compelling visual story! The illustration will be submitted via Gradescope.

**Field study (22%) –** Students will work individually to design and propose a field study to assess organic matter in an environment of their choosing. Undergraduate proposal drafts will undergo peer review by graduate students in mid-November. The written proposal must include the following sections: background, motivation, methodology (including proposed study site description, sample collection and storage, sample preparation and analysis, and data work-up), and expected outcomes. Use of relevant figures and/or tables is also expected. In the last week of class (December 2 and 6), each student will give a “lightning talk” that summarizes the relevance and importance of their field study proposal. The final written field study proposal is due on Friday, December 6 via Gradescope.

## Artificial Intelligence Statement

Students are allowed to use advanced automated tools (artificial intelligence or machine learning

tools such as ChatGPT, Grammarly, or Dall-E 2) on assignments in this course if instructor

permission is obtained in advance. Unless given permission to use those tools, each student is

expected to complete each assignment without substantive assistance from others, including

automated tools. If permission is granted to use advanced automated tools (artificial intelligence

or machine learning tools such as ChatGPT, Grammarly, or Dall-E 2), they must be properly

documented and credited. Text generated using ChatGPT-3 should include a citation such as:

“Chat-GPT-3. (YYYY, Month DD of query). “Text of your prompt.”

You may use AI programs e.g., ChatGPT to help generate ideas and brainstorm. However, you

should note that the material generated by these programs may be inaccurate, incomplete, or

otherwise problematic. Beware that use may also stifle your own independent thinking and

creativity. You may not submit any work generated by an AI program as your own. If you include

material generated by an AI program, it should be cited like any other reference material (with

consideration for the quality of the reference, which may be poor).

Work created by AI tools may not be considered original work and instead, considered automated

plagiarism. It is derived from previously created texts from other sources that the models were

trained on yet does not cite sources. AI models have built-in biases (i.e., they are trained on

limited underlying sources; they reproduce, rather than challenge, errors in the sources). AI

tools have limitations (i.e., they lack critical thinking to evaluate and reflect on criteria; they lack

abductive reasoning to make judgments with incomplete information at hand).
