## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

This course focuses on the co-evolution of life with Earth’s systems and targets students in Earth science, biology, chemistry, and physics. Topics include the emergence and early evolution of life on Earth, the environmental limits of life, microbial and metabolic evolution; the role of life in moderating global elemental cycles; earliest records of life; evolution of photosynthesis and the Great Oxidation Event; fossilization and evolution of complex life; mass radiations, mass extinctions and global change.

## Course Content

### Content Details:

Course Topics

The course topics are divided intro four modules and will cover the following topics:

Module A: The Geosphere and Biosphere

· Earth Introduction – planetary formation & structure

· The rock cycle, the water cycle, weathering and climate

· Biology Introduction – What is life made of? What does it do?

· Biodiversity, evolution and the Tree of Life

· Overview of Global Biogeochemical cycles

Module B: Life and Global Cycles

· The Microbial World; Microbial diversity and life’s limits

· Thermodynamics and microbial redox reactions

· Introduction to redox chemistry; early microbial metabolism

· Microbial energy conservation; chemotrophy & phototrophy

· Microbial elemental cycling and metabolism

Module C: Major Biosphere Events in Earth History

· Evolution of photosynthesis; the Great Oxidation Event

· Glaciations, major extinctions and radiations

· Anthropocene and global change

Module D: Astrobiology, Life’s Origins, Search for Life

· History of Astrobiology

· Life’s Origins

· Biosignatures

## Learning Outcomes

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes (CLOs):

- Explain the interactions between the geosphere and biosphere that affect the emergence of life; global biogeochemical cycles; modern and past biodiversity; evolution of complex life as recorded in the geologic record.
- Identify the environmental and geologic requirements for the emergence of life on Earth and explain the modern hypotheses for the origin and early evolution of life on Earth.
- Interpret the phylogenetic tree of life and apply characteristics of this phylogeny to Earth’s history.
- Interpret, illustrate and explain global biogeochemical cycles and calculate fluxes and residence times of major elements in the biosphere, hydrosphere, geosphere and atmosphere.
- Demonstrate knowledge of major events in the history of the Earth and biosphere, including major extinctions and radiations and global change events.

### Additional Academic Integrity Course Policy and Penalty Information:

Academic Integrity This course will utilize a zero-tolerance policy for any form of cheating or other academic misconduct. All submitted assignments will be subjected to copying and plagiarism checkers. University policy deals with the problem of academic dishonesty, cheating, and plagiarism. The Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities define various forms of Academic Dishonesty, and you should make yourself familiar with these. No violation will be tolerated in this class. The sanctions provided in this policy will be used to deal with any violations.

Academic dishonesty includes, but is not limited to, copying material from another student or use of written notes during an exam, representing others’ work as your own for any assignment, changing answers on an exam paper after the paper has been graded, collaborating with other students or using outside sources during exams, quizzes, and other graded assignments. Any student caught cheating will be assigned an F for the course. Furthermore, violations of academic integrity may also be reported to the appropriate Dean (Dean of Students or the Dean of Graduate Education for undergraduate and graduate students, respectively).

For example, students must trust that teachers have made appropriate decisions about the structure and content of the courses they teach, and teachers must trust that the assignments that students turn in are their own. Acts that violate this trust undermine the educational process.. In this class, all assignments that are turned in for a grade must represent the student’s own work. If you have any question concerning this policy before submitting an assignment, please ask for clarification.

A special note on plagiarism. Please see the definition of plagiarism given below, as outlined in The Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights. To be clear, any time material that is not the students’ own is used, it should be in quotes and contain a citation. If a student paraphrases work from elsewhere, it also requires a citation. Furthermore, even when citations are used, others’ work cannot be used as a student response on an assignment. For example, in this class, if the main conclusions are given in an article, they cannot be used verbatim as a response in the post-discussion evaluation, even if a citation is present.

“Plagiarism Representing the work or words of another as one’s own through the omission of acknowledgment or reference. For example, using sentences verbatim from a published source in a term paper without appropriate referencing, or presenting as one’s own the detailed argument of a published source, or presenting as one’s own electronically or digitally enhanced graphic representations from any form of media.” - ACADEMIC INTEGRITY (2014-2016 Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights & Responsibilities, August 2014)
