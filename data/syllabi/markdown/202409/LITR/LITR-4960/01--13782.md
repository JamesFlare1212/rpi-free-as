## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

Course Description:

This class could be subtitled “Reflecting on Motion Picture History Through Undying Eyes: 200-Year-Old Vampires.” Using a world-building and storytelling framework for learning, students will engage with the history of the moving image. In a format not unlike a table-top roleplaying game, students will create characters that were born sometime in the early 19th century and became vampires shortly after the invention of the photographic image, imagining that small groups of vampires would have come together as covens and then witnessed the evolution of motion pictures up until the present day. From the perspective of their characters, students will encounter various films, TV shows, and streaming digital media, as well as film and media theory--spanning 200 years of image and motion picture history. The semester will culminate in a festival of the history of images and motion pictures, recounting the unlife of images as it relates to living cultural histories.

In other words…200 Year Old Vampires is a roleplaying-game-as-class. You play an individual member of one of several covens of vampires–important here, though, is situating all of this in real-world history (but, with vampires). You trace two centuries of images and motion pictures as your vampires encountered them–delving into those media artifacts and your vampire’s personal relationship to them. You create artifacts of response–diaries, letters, period-style reviews, and potentially images of your own. You imagine how your vampire engaged with each other and with places and cultures they witnessed. The collected covens will plan a festival reflecting on their undying experiences of the first two hundred years of photographic and moving images.

## Learning Outcomes

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes (CLOs):

- -Identify and explain historical and technological trends in photographic images, film and motion pictures.
- -Apply critical and reflective lenses to images and motion picture media texts, and discuss the value and function of those strategies in understanding them from a historical and cultural perspective.
- -Work collaboratively with a group of peers to create stories of a multi-faceted nature, using fictional characters placed in real-world history in order to trace developments in image and motion pictures from an experiential perspective.
- -Collaboratively produce a researched timeline of the development of motion pictures from technological and socio-cultural perspectives.
- -Produce a portfolio of individually-produced written, image, video, and physical artifacts related to image and motion picture history.

## Grading Criteria

### Criteria Details:

ADDITIVE ASSESSMENT:

Rather than an exercise in knowledge absorption, this course should feel like a process of exploration, articulation, reflection, and re-articulation through presentation. You’ll be carefully considering histories of places and cultures, technological developments, and of course images and motion pictures, but you’ll be reacting to them both as yourself but also imagining how your historically situated character might have. This work will be informed by readings on history, readings on film theory, and by interacting with the work of scholars and writers that have come before you, putting you in dialogue with that work as well. Just as importantly, you will be expected to work creatively as well as critically and reflectively. Assessment in this class will primarily be an attempt to measure your depth of engagement with all of these activities.

To those ends, we will use an ‘additive assessment’ strategy where everyone starts with zero points and then accumulates points based on assignments and activities. You won’t receive points based on whether or not you agree with my perspective on the materials, or whether your answers are ‘right’ or ‘wrong’; you’ll receive points based on whether or not you’ve really thought it through. In general, if you follow the guidelines for each assignment and activity and genuinely work towards applying the concepts, you’ll get most or all of the points for the assignments that you undertake. Note that beyond the ‘baseline’ activities there are far more potential points to be accumulated across the semester than you need (and likely far more than you can do); this means that you will need to decide which of them you will undertake, based on your personal interests and comfort level.

## Policies

### Other Course Policies:

Paper Formatting

For most papers, please properly use academic formats, and correctly cite references. As a standard for this class, MLA format will be used. If you have a personal/professional reason for wanting to use a different format—APA, Chicago, etc.—I am perfectly open to that, as long as it’s a standard format. The Purdue University’s OWL website is an excellent resource for both MLA and APA formats and general academic writing practices: \<https://owl.english.purdue.edu/owl/>

HOWEVER…For many of your writings in this class—since they are intended to be period writings of vampires—you will be welcome to use non-traditional format, fonts, etc.. What I’d like you to do is try to ‘sneak in’ (or label in a header or something) the actual information that we need to keep track of your points for the class – i.e., give us your real name and the actual date somewhere, for example. The same goes with citing Images or stills from motion pictures – maybe you can sneak that in at the end.

Use of Images in Assignments

In this class, of course you should include images on any assignments and papers. If you’re using stills from motion pictures or audio, in your references you should give a citation for the media text itself (not the website where you found it), and when possible give a minute/second notation (just as you would give page numbers for a book) so that someone can find the cited material in the original text.

Late assignments\*/\*\*

For most required assignments we need you to do them ahead of class so that we can ‘play the game’ of the class. If you do the assignments late, you will receive half the points (but not have points deducted–see above). For most ‘Open Activities’ there aren’t specific due dates, but rather you are highly encouraged to align when you turn in the activity with the ‘timeline’ that it should correspond to in game.

*NOTE: I am always open to discussing extenuating circumstances—especially in the age of COVID! (which is still with us)—but will be much more lenient if you communicate with me promptly and professionally about what those circumstances are/were.

\*\*NOTE: The Power to Erase My Memory—I realize that we all have crazy and unpredictable lives, and that things come up. So…each of you can also imagine that your vampire used the power of hypnotism to make me forget that you didn’t turn in one required daily assignment; the same goes with 1 absence…let me know when you’d like to use this ability during the semester.

Revisions of Assignments

If you feel that the points weren’t as high as you think you could have achieved on an assignment, we can make arrangements for a resubmission to improve the points attribution of that assignment. Note that this will NOT be possible for the final projects due to time constraints on grading.

Academic integrity

Student-teacher relationships are built on trust. For example, students must trust that teachers have made appropriate decisions about the structure and content of the courses they teach, and teachers must trust that the assignments that students turn in are their own. Acts that violate this trust undermine the educational process. The Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities and The Graduate Student Supplement define various forms of Academic Dishonesty and you should make yourself familiar with these.

In this class, all assignments that are turned in for a grade must represent the student’s own work. In cases where help was received, or teamwork was allowed, a notation on the assignment should indicate your collaboration. Any student who plagiarizes or otherwise cheats, including using text written by a generation system as one’s own (e.g., entering a prompt into an artificial intelligence tool and using the output in a paper), will fail the relevant assignment and is subject to failing the course. Violations of academic integrity may also be reported to the appropriate Dean (Dean of Students for undergraduate students or the Dean of Graduate Education for graduate students, respectively). If you have any question concerning this policy before submitting an assignment, please ask for clarification.
