## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

**Description:**

This course examines race as an ontological fact and epistemological tool—both the target of scholarly inquiry and as a methodological lens—in works pertaining to science, medicine, technology, and society. In addition to exploring concepts like intersectionality, the human genome, and the racial politics of seemingly “neutral” technological forms, students will add their own interests to our course design and shape the areas we study. Throughout the semester, we’ll visit local archives, take part in organizing the racial justice in STS workshop, and envision both traditional and creative ways for disseminating research and scholarship.

## Learning Outcomes

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes (CLOs):

- Recognize and articulate key concepts in the field of race, science, and technology studies.
- Recognize and articulate the various ways race is employed as an analytical or methodological tool in STS scholarship.
- Conceptualize and produce scholarly works exploring the problem of race or employing it as an analytical tool in research pertinent to STS.

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes Assessment Measures:

**Building Portfolios…**

There’s more than one way for us to do this and I’m open to experimenting with different approaches. I like to think of portfolios as a collection of the most meaningful things you’ve done during the semester. Some things you might include are…

- A Research Paper: Write an original research paper (circa 5000 words, ~20 pages) on a topic of interest to you.
- Project Proposals: Based on our archival visits, the materials we study in class, and your own independent interests, craft a viable research plan for a project (or projects) you could pursue in the future.
- Research Blog/Reflective Essay: Based on the materials we study, craft a 500-1500 word essay (or series of essays) that could appear in an STS oriented research blog, essay collection, or journal. For one example of this type of project, follow this link to NursingClio.… [https://nursingclio.org/2021/05/09/a-farewell-but-not-a-goodbye]
- Book Reviews: Choose a scholarly book (published relatively recently) and write an 800-1000 word review appropriate for inclusion in a scholarly journal.
- A Lecture or Pedagogical Exercise: If you’d like to gain experience lecturing, we may be able to create opportunities in my undergraduate seminar on the History of Race, Science, and Medicine. Also, if you’d like to design and module for use in your own classes, this would be an opportunity to do so.
- Alternative Syllabi/Reading List: This would be something akin to an annotated bibliography of works related to the field that you would be interested in reading or could see yourself teaching in a graduate or undergraduate course of your own.
- A Scholarly Translation: Translations are a vital aspect of scholarship in most academic fields, including STS. If there are works you would like to translate and annotate.
- A Podcast Episode
- A Creative Endeavor Rooted in Research and Scholarship: TBD
- Another idea: Have something else in mind? Let’s talk about it.
- End of Semester Reflection: At the end of the semester, please submit a final reflection with your portfolio.

## Grading Criteria

### Criteria Details:

*Assessment*: There is no standardized grading rubric for this course. We’ll meet individually throughout the semester to discuss how you’d like to take the course, including the kind of projects you’d like to work on so that the course speaks to your intellectual needs and the requirements of your program. You’ll receive feedback on your work in the form of questions and comments to build a generative dialogue about your efforts. At the end of the semester, you’ll submit a portfolio and have significant input in determining your final grade.

## Policies

### Academic Policies:

- ### Academic Integrity

  Student-teacher relationships are built on trust. For example, students must trust that teachers have made appropriate decisions about the structure and content of the courses they teach, and teachers must trust that the assignments that students turn in are their own. Acts that violate this trust undermine the educational process. The Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities and The Graduate Student Supplement define various forms of Academic Dishonesty and you should make yourself familiar with these. In this class, all assignments that are turned in for a grade must represent the student’s own work. In cases where help was received, or teamwork was allowed, a notation on the assignment should indicate your collaboration.

  Violations of academic integrity may also be reported to the appropriate Dean (Dean of Students for undergraduate students or the Dean of Graduate Education for graduate students, respectively).

  If you have any question concerning this policy before submitting an assignment, please ask for clarification. In addition, you can visit the following site for more information on our Academic Integrity Policy: [Students Rights, Responsibilities, and Judicial Affairs](https://info.rpi.edu/dean-students/student-rights-responsibilities-and-judicial-affairs).
