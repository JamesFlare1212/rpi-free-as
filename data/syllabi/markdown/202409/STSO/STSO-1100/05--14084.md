### Additional Information for the Instructor Section:

**Section Description**

This section in particular focuses on environmental politics and values in the relationships relevant to our everyday life, experience, and society-wide controversies related to science, technology, environment, and society. In phases 3 and 4 of the course, we will apply critical STS-inflected thinking to environmental topics such as:

- the social construction of soil, water, and air pollution baselines
- knowledge politics of the negative externalities of natural-gas development
- the highly contentious and surprisingly socio-environment contestation related to wind and solar energy development
- the philosophical and pragmatic practices underlying how plastic matter … matters
- the intersection of contaminated brownfields and landfills with racial politics
- the co-constitution of humans, dogs, and capitalism
- the revealingly ambiguous history of mushrooms and human hierarchies
- the planetary politics and labor of microbes
- the equally incredible capacities of beavers and hubris of post-war environmental transplant colonialism
- the intersection of innovative sustainable agriculture and spiritual practices
- the signification of weeds and post-globalization
- wrangling oysters to maintain the socio-political status quo in the face of climate futures
- the relationship between lobsters, whales, and contemporary settler colonial heritage practices
- the geo-enviro-politics fueling the irrational rationality of mitigating climate change with atmospheric geoengineering
- the relationship between the climate crisis and reactionary politics

While other courses, such as Sustainability Debates or Environmental Politics, deal directly with the many genealogies, divisions, and issues in environmentalism, sustainability, regenerative, etc. discourses. However, many similar themes and concepts will be covered in our readings. I will be certain to emphasize these in our post-lecture discussions!

## Course Goals

### Goals:

**Course Description**

What are science and technology in practice? This course is an introduction to the interdisciplinary field of Science and Technology Studies (STS)where we answer that question by looking at how, when, where, and why scientific inquiry and technology innovation occurs—and what it all means. Borrowing from history, philosophy, anthropology, sociology, and others, students will see how science and technology always emerge out of particular contexts, conducted by particular people, and for particular reasons. This is a communications intensive course. In addition to the STS topics discussed in class, we will spend time in class discussing the skills of academic life, writing, and information literacy.

In Phase 1 and 2 of this course, students will gain a robust understanding of some important foundational texts, concepts, and disciplinary history of the field Science and Technology Studies. To do so, we will engage deeply with scholarship on a wide range of topics, such as:

- the structure and experience of scientific, technological, and environmental changes
- so-called pseudoscience through the disciplinary politics of para-psychology
- the industrialization of home technology creating ‘more work for mother’
- Louis Pasteur’s harnessing of microbes and politics
- the co-making of high-energy physics and physicists
- the interrelation of scallops, fishermen, and scientists
- scientific objectivity and feminist science
- the expert-lay politics of radioactive sheep
- ‘normal accidents’ in a risky society
- the relationship between environmental claims and scientific credibility and authority
- scientific and social politics of global climate meteorology

Reading Tips

Obviously, you know how to read. But academic reading is its own strange skill to learn. In this course we are mostly going to be talking about ideas. This means that you should be reading the texts assigned for their arguments. Ask yourself the following questions as you read:

- What is the author’s big argument?
- What is their evidence offered?
- What motivates the author, implicitly and explicitly?
- What does the author see as the consequences of their argument and research? (What are the stakes?)

Pay close attention to the beginnings and ends of chapters or articles, where conclusions and big points usually hide. Additionally, you may want to look at Paul Edward’s guide “How to Read,” which can be found in the “Other Resources” folder in our box. While it is written for graduate students, many of the strategies and tactics discussed are useful for anyone doing academic reading!

*I highly recommend printing readings out and note-taking with a writing utensil and paper if you are interested in achieving the most efficient combination of time usage, effort expenditure, and comprehension.*

Guidelines for Discussion

Class discussions will be extremely important for not only student grades but also comprehension. To ensure these are effective for all, we must all adhere to the following guidelines which apply broadly across STS discussion-based classes:

- The purpose of the class is not to debate or prove a point but to understand more deeply what the authors are trying to express in the texts.
- Talk to each other, not just to the discussion leader or professor.
- Listen and respond to other statements before introducing a new idea. Ask questions if you do not understand what someone has said, or you can paraphrase what another student has said for clarification (“I think you said this; is that right?”).
- Refer to evidence from the text to support your ideas.
- Pay attention to your “airtime”—how much you have spoken in relation to other students.
- Try your absolute best not to interrupt others. But, if you find yourself mid-sentence realizing you have, simply quickly finish your thought, politely apologize, and then explicitly hand it back to the person you interrupted.

- Writing

*There are no midterm or final essays in this class! No final project! No group work or presentations!* Instead, the writing assignments in this class are designed to engage students continually throughout the semester and steadily improve their critical and expressive capacities. This will require diligent and regular work on smaller writing assignments which evolve in the successive phases detailed in the class schedule section. Below, these are explained:

- Phase 2: Micro-Essay x 10 Total — 15%
- In Phase 2, “A Partial Foundation in Science and Technology Studies,” students will turn in a “micro-essay” prior to *every class* on LMS
- We will do a detailed review of Micro-Essays in our second class
- Micro-Essays will be 200-250 words
- Micro-Essays are *not* actual “essays” that need to make an argument, profound point, or demonstrate mastery of the text, nor are they summaries of the text that quote or reference the text
- The Micro-Essay assignments intend to engender consistent reading and note-taking practices that will ensure all students are engaged in the most conceptually important part of the course

- Phase 3: Meso-Reflections x 4 — 15 %
- In Phase 3, “Socio-Environmental Issues of Late Industrialism,” we will shift to *weekly* “Meso-Reflections”
- We will do a detailed review of Meso-Reflections on October 18th
- The Meso-Reflection assignments intend to build on the reading and note-taking practices developed in Phase 1 but further ask students to respond or reflect to the complexities and cases of Phase 2
- Meso-Reflections have a beginning, middle, and end
- Meso-Reflections will be turned in on LMS prior to Tuesday class
- Prompts will be provided by Professor Bucky after Friday classes
- Meso-Reflections will be 400-450 words
- **Writing Intensive Course Note**: As this is a communication intensive class, students will engage in peer review by reading other students meso-reflections and providing comments at least twice, with these two meso-reflections being able to be rewritten by everyone after comments!
- Phase 4: Macro-Rumination x 5 — 40%
- In Phase 4, “Critical Travels, Translations, and Transmutations On a More-than-Human Planet,” we will reach our final evolution of the writing assignment in the form of the “Macro-Rumination”
- We will do a detailed review of Macro-Rumination on November 5th
- The Macro-Rumination will be an extension of the meso-reflection format but with more open prompts that ask students to really display their comprehension through synthesis, application, and imagination
- Open Prompts will be provided by Professor Bucky after Friday classes
- Macro-Ruminations will be 700-850 words

Considering that writing is a central practice in this course, I have provided some free materials in the “Other Resources” folder in the Box. For your everyday English writing things—such as when and how to use a comma—I recommend Strunk and White’s *The Element of Style*. For a general overview of English grammar, I have provided the excellent *English: An Essential Grammar* by Gerald Nelson as well. I **HIGHLY** recommend everyone download and peruse this document not just for this class but your daily life. English is a fundamentally strange language with many fascinating quirks—which I am happy to detail for the class if requested. Viewing it from the oblique angle of a complete grammar is a shockingly educational exercise, even for native speakers. Additionally, I highly encourage students to make use of the Center for Communication and Design (Comm + D). This “free support service … provides one-on-one consultations” for all manner of “written, oral, and visual” projects. A snazzy flyer can be found in the “Other Resources” Box folder.

### Academic Policies:

- ### Academic Integrity

  Student-teacher relationships are built on trust. For example, students must trust that teachers have made appropriate decisions about the structure and content of the courses they teach, and teachers must trust that the assignments that students turn in are their own. Acts that violate this trust undermine the educational process. The Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities and The Graduate Student Supplement define various forms of Academic Dishonesty and you should make yourself familiar with these. In this class, all assignments that are turned in for a grade must represent the student’s own work. In cases where help was received, or teamwork was allowed, a notation on the assignment should indicate your collaboration.

  Violations of academic integrity may also be reported to the appropriate Dean (Dean of Students for undergraduate students or the Dean of Graduate Education for graduate students, respectively).

  If you have any question concerning this policy before submitting an assignment, please ask for clarification. In addition, you can visit the following site for more information on our Academic Integrity Policy: [Students Rights, Responsibilities, and Judicial Affairs](https://info.rpi.edu/dean-students/student-rights-responsibilities-and-judicial-affairs).

- Ø Sexual Misconduct and Title IX

Title IX is a federal law prohibiting sex discrimination. The Title IX section of the Education Amendments Act of 1972 states, “No person in the United States shall, on the basis of sex, be excluded from participation in, be denied the benefits of, or be subjected to discrimination under any education program or activity receiving Federal financial assistance.” Under Title IX, sexual misconduct is considered a form of sex discrimination.

Sexual misconduct includes, but is not limited to sexual harassment, sexual assault, dating and domestic violence, intimate partner violence, and stalking. Sexual misconduct is not tolerated at Rensselaer, and it is a violation of Institute policy, and state and federal law.

Faculty and other employees are considered mandated reporters when it comes to experiences of interpersonal violence (sexual assault, sexual harassment, dating or domestic violence, and stalking). Disclosures of interpersonal violence must be passed along to Rensselaer’s Title IX Coordinators who can help provide support and academic remedies for students who have been impacted. For more information or to report sexual misconduct, see https://sexualviolence.rpi.edu/

- Ø Academic Integrity

Student-teacher relationships are built on trust. Students must trust that teachers have made appropriate decisions about the structure and content of courses they teach, and teachers must trust that assignments that students turn in are their own. Acts that violate this trust undermine the educational process. Any acts of plagiarism will have grave consequences. The Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities defines various forms of academic dishonesty, and you should make yourself familiar with these. In this class, all individual assignments that are turned in for a grade must represent the student’s own work. Any instances of plagiarism will result in a failing grade for the assignment. Repeated instances of academic dishonesty will be grounds for failing the course. Plagiarism includes purchasing term papers; copying or handing in the writing of another student (current or former); using sentences verbatim from a published source without appropriate referencing (when in doubt, cite the source); and presenting as one’s own the detailed argument of a published source. “Recycling” papers written in other courses is also forbidden.

Violations of this policy will be considered academic misconduct.
