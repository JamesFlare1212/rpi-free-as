## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

This course introduces students to harm reduction as a problem space for designing objects, social worlds, infrastructures, and ecologies concretized in physical spaces that shape human and nonhuman social interactions within them. There are myriad ways that harm reduction practices relate to design. We will extend the concept in many directions—towards undoing past harms, living well in the present, or reducing future harms. This course introduces history, theory, and practice of harm reduction in relation to health, well-being, and safety.

Upper-level DIS studios will advance skills as designers and innovators by bringing them to bear on a relevant theme. This one seeks to enable you to promote harm reduction practices in the context of social movements responsive to a decades-long policy crisis that has led to a very high drug-related death rate. This syllabus is experimental in that we will use it to document and bring into being Harm Reduction Design concepts and practices. This course relies on concepts from anthropology, history, public health, and sociology for making sense of how psychoactive drugs have been and are used in historical and contemporary societies.

Harm reduction also includes actions to take legal consequences and criminal penalties out of public health and drug policy, or to reduce the harms of medicalization itself. Decriminalizing these forms of harm require changes in law and policy. We will be looking into political campaigns that work to make these changes on the local, state, and federal levels.

We study how design changes affect specific communities. Who are People Who Use Drugs? Arguably, all of us. We will study how representations of drug use and drug users in popular culture, science, medicine, and social science work. We will trace how public and private values are translated into drug law and policy in ways that become “default settings.” Our goal as designers is to re-set these “default settings” so as to decrease their capacity to harm individuals, communities, and polities. Although we will learn how conventional knowledges generated about drug addiction affect policymaking, and how evidence-based policy works in this arena, we will also attempt to integrate knowledge informed by lived experience wherever possible.

Each studio day will proceed from a mix of in-studio exercises; making and doing sessions; skill shares; and lectures, presentations, and discussions of readings, films, artifacts and objects, and websites. Students will reciprocally receive and give feedback that helps them develop design, reading, writing, discussion, and presentation skills. Students are expected to cite the work of others, including any generative AIs, that they use in their written work and oral presentations.

## Course Goals

### Goals:

Students who successfully complete this course will do a lot of reading, writing, and presenting to situate their design work. They will learn to demonstrate the following:

- \- Ability to think critically about and discuss—in oral and written form—contemporary artifacts, social worlds, and environments relevant to harm reduction’s matrix of factors (economic, technical, neurochemical, biophysical, cultural, historical, etc.) that frame problems and constrain solutions.
- \- Ability to respond to complex design problems that arise in the course of social movements.
- \- Ability to analyze and present findings on particular contexts and with relevant social groups.
- \- Specific knowledge concerning events that led to the current regulatory regime and toxicity crisis governing global drug production, distribution, and consumption.

**Thursday, August 28**

This course defines “harm” depending on the social contexts in which it has been produced, distributed, and used throughout history. Gather your course materials and come to studio having thought about your personal and professional motivations for taking this high-expectations studio course. We look forward to meeting you!

**Presentation: Professor Campbell, “Just Say Know”**

Harm reduction is an approach to helping people who engage in high-risk activities to develop skills and strategies to keep them and their communities safe. This can include provision of sterile equipment, low-threshold and low-barrier care, acceptance of non-abstinence goals in treatment, and radical hospitality. Please start reading *The Harm Reduction Gap,* which discusses the shortcomings of Just Say No messages and abstinence-only treatment approaches. The book introduces harm reduction language, strategies and technologies borne of people who use drugs themselves. The book suggests policy alternatives to current policies that criminalize drugs, drug-using equipment or “paraphernalia,” and settings in which people use drugs. The book aims to destigmatize drug use and support drug policy reform. We will use this approach to guide design projects supporting local efforts to ensure people who use drugs and their communities can stay safe at a time when the drug supply has become uniquely unsafe. This presentation covers a ‘zine called *Junkphood* (@junkie_ephemera).

### Additional Academic Integrity Course Policy and Penalty Information:

Student-teacher relationships are built on trust, honesty, and respect for one another. Students must trust that teachers have made appropriate decisions about course structure and content; teachers that the assignments students turn in are their own. Acts violating trust undermine the educational process. The *Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities* defines various forms of academic dishonesty and procedures for response. Penalties for plagiarism and other forms of cheating can be harsh and their effects long-lasting—they are awful for everyone involved. If you are having difficulty completing assignments, speak to the professors and don’t resort to unethical activities. When you turn in written work under your own name, we trust that you—and you alone—have done this work. Submission of an assignment in violation of this policy will result in failure on the specific assignment, which may result in failure of the course.

**Does this mean I have to write every word? Yes.** Faculty are way more interested in engaging with \*your\* thoughts than with the products of AI. If you use somebody else’s words—or words that someone else or something else have “generated”—you MUST indicate that by putting them in quotation marks and citing where they are from. If you resort to ChatGPT, you MUST indicate that with quotation marks and proper citation. Submission of an assignment in violation of this policy will result in a 0 on the specific assignment, which may result in failure of the course. Violation of this policy may include further disciplinary action. It’s not worth it.

**Safe Space, Brave Space:** I would like for this classroom to be a place where everyone can be their full self, explore new ideas, share unique perspectives, and talk about the ways feelings or “affect” are generated within institutional structures. Things we can do to promote this ethos include: listening to others, valuing divergent perspectives, recognizing your unique insights as well as the partiality of your perspective. We all have limitations and biases. We often talk about bias in this course, and how it has led to distorted, lopsided, or even unethical attitudes, policies, and other outcomes. Please avoid shaming and blaming, using hateful language, name-calling, or misgendering people, all of which prevent people from feeling safe to be themselves.

**Inclusive Language Guidelines:** The Department of Science and Technology Studies aspires to create a learning environment where people of all identities may fully contribute their perspectives. Our guidelines on language and terminology are intended to foster an inclusive and welcoming environment, strengthen our academic writing by making it relevant to broad audiences, enrich our discussions, and reflect best professional practices.

- Gender-inclusive and non-sexist language: Language can be gender-inclusive and non-sexist when words are used to affirm and respect how people describe, express, and experience their gender. Gender-inclusive/non-sexist language acknowledges people of any gender, including stances that are gender-nonconforming and non-binary. Preferred pronouns and names are gender identities and forms of self-expression that should be honored.
- Anti-racist language: Labels and definitions of racial and ethnic categories vary across time and space; these have often been bound up with drug use. In this classroom, we will avoid racial and ethnic slurs, but we will talk about race and ethnicity, racial-ethnic formation, and other historical and sociological languages for how people describe, express, and experience their race, ethnicity, ancestry, or cultural heritage. Students may share the terminology that they prefer to describe their racial and ethnic identities, and these preferences should be respected just as much as pronouns and other identifiers are.
- Non-ableist language: Academia can be a very ableist environment. Ableist language devalues people with physical and mental disabilities—some of which are visible and others less so. Ableist language permeates everyday language so such a degree that it becomes hard to see. Thoughtful alternatives appear in this guide: https://www.autistichoya.com/p/ableist-words-and-terms-to-avoid.html

**All of the above can result in harms that we are seeking to reduce.**
