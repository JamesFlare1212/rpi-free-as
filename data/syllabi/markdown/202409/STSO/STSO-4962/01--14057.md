## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

This seminar surveys the history of Rensselaer Polytechnic Institute, Troy and the Capital Region. By reading scholarly works, performing original research and visiting relevant sites, we will seek to understand the technological, political, social and cultural history of each of these entities, as well as how their histories converge (or don’t). We will also discuss the meanings and methodologies of local history as a sub-discipline of history, and we will contextualize our study of local history by exploring the interplay between local, regional and national themes in history.

## Learning Outcomes

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes (CLOs):

- ● Identify and explain key developments in the history of Rensselaer, Troy and the Capital Region.
- ● Evaluate the ways in which political, social, economic, cultural and technological factors intersected to influence the development of local communities.
- ● Discuss the meaning of local history as a field, and explain common research strategies associated with local history.
- ● Perform original historical research.

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes Assessment Measures:

#### **Attendance and Participation**

**Discussion leadership**

Over the course of the semester, each student will play the role of discussion leader for one of our meetings. On the assigned day, the discussion leader will begin discussion of the assigned reading for that day by providing a fifteen-minute summary of the main **arguments** presented in the reading. Don't just summarize the readings' narrative; instead, focus on the arguments that the authors make. The student will then pose at least three questions related to the reading, which we will answer during class discussion. The student will be responsible for directing and managing discussion, with the course instructor providing support as needed.

Presentation schedules will be coordinated during the first week of the semester. **Except in the case of official excused absences, students who fail to attend class or are unprepared to present on their assigned day will not be able to make up the assignment**.

**Local history wiki**

Over the course of the semester, students will create one article and revise another article on an internal class wiki whose purpose is to document the history of Troy and RPI. Due dates for these assignments are indicated on the course schedule below.

The wiki is hosted at http://christozzi.com/lhwiki Before contributing to the wiki, please create an account that includes your last name so that the instructor can identify your contributions. Editing the wiki requires some basic technical skills, which we will practice in class.

When creating the new wiki articles, follow these guidelines:

- You may create an article about any topic – indeed, choosing a relevant topic is part of the assignment – as long as it relates in some way to the history of Troy and/or RPI.
- The new article should be at least 300 words in length.
- Articles should be modeled on Wikipedia for style, formatting and depth.
- Where appropriate, articles should cite primary and/or secondary sources.
- Avoid writing articles that closely parallel existing content on Wikipedia or other sources. Your articles should be original, unique contributions.

For the article revisions, follow these guidelines:

- Choose any existing article on the wiki to revise, except articles that you wrote.
- You may revise the article by expanding upon their content, improving their writing quality and organization, adding references, clarifying factual errors or ambiguities or any combination of the above. The goal is to revise articles in ways that make them more valuable to readers; determining exactly how to do that is part of the assignment.

**Research project**

Over the course of the semester, students will complete a major research project using original archival sources. Each student is responsible for formulating the topic of the research project; in all cases, however, the goal should be to **answer a question about local history that remains unresolved**. Students should avoid topics that have already been thoroughly researched, unless they believe that additional research can contribute important new perspectives to the topic.

**Students should formulate a research topic by week 4 of the course**, as indicated in the schedule below. Potential topics to research include:

- The history of a local building, street, community or company.
- The life or career of an individual of local significance.
- The ways in which an historical "force" -- such as a political, economic, religious or social idea (like urban renewal or Marxist thought), an environmental condition (such as local geography or geology) or a national or international development (such as World War I or the assassination of Martin Luther King, Jr.) -- impacted RPI, Troy and/or the Capital Region.
- The history of a club or department at RPI.

The above are simply generic suggestions; students should feel free to research any topic that interests them, and creativity in selecting topics will be rewarded.

The research project will be performed on an ongoing basis over the course of the semester. The final product is a 10-15 page (double-spaced) paper that identifies the purpose and relevance of the research project, explains the student's findings and suggests opportunities for future research on the subject.

In order to receive full credit for the research project, students should:

- Ground their work in **archival** sources. Libraries and published books are not typically places to find archival sources. Instead, students should look in physical or digital archives, such as the RPI Archives, the Hart Cluett Museum's archives in downtown Troy or digitized archives of local newspapers. Published materials (such as books and articles) may be used to supplement and contextualize the archival research, but the core of the research project should be focused on original archival work. In certain cases, oral interviews may be useful as well. We will discuss archival research opportunities in class, focusing on materials that are available online during the pandemic.
- Strive not only to narrate an historical development or theme, but also to **explain and interpret it**. In other words, don't simply tell *what* happened; explain as well *why* it happened as it did, and what its significance is for the present.
- Include a clear introduction, thesis statement and conclusion in their final paper.
- Follow the conventions of the *Chicago Manual of Style* for citing sources.

The final paper is due on LMS on the date indicated below.

**Note**: In lieu of completing the research paper described above, students are welcome to consider an alternative endeavor by which they can engage in a substantive and substantial way with local history. Examples may include compiling a database of oral interviews related to local history, building a website or volunteering at a local historical society or museum. Please discuss with the course instructor if you are interested in taking a different approach to the project.

## Policies

### Attendance Policy:

Attendance **and participation** in online class meetings and site visits are required. Participation means posing thoughtful questions and responding to the questions of others in ways that demonstrate completion of and reflection upon course readings and meetings.

**Note on site visits**: As indicated in the schedule of meetings below, on certain days we will travel to sites on or in the vicinity of the RPI campus. We will complete these trips by foot, and students should thus come prepared to walk up to three miles outdoors. If you are unsure of your ability to complete these trips, please contact the course instructor before the first site visit in order to arrange accommodations. On days when we take trips, we will meet in front of Sage Lab (not the Sage dining hall!) on the RPI campus. We will return to campus by the end of our scheduled class period.
