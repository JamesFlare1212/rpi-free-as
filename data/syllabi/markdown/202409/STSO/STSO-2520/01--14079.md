## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

In addition to providing a comprehensive overview of the main theories, concepts, and subject areas within sociology, this course aims to provide you with an understanding of various aspects of medicine, health, and illness from a sociological standpoint. Throughout the duration of the course, sociological research will be utilized to emphasize the methods and perspectives employed in medical sociology. Recognizing that a person's health and life expectancy are more strongly influenced by their zip code than their genetic makeup, it becomes evident that health and illness cannot be comprehended solely by examining biological factors. It is essential to also consider a range of social, political, economic, and cultural factors that contribute to the production and interpretation of health and illness. Throughout the semester, we will explore the key subjects in this field, with a particular focus on understanding the following themes: the structural and cultural dimensions of health, health disparities, the medical profession, the experience of illness, and efforts to reform healthcare. An overarching theme that will be addressed throughout the semester is social inequality and its connection to health disparities, which are shaped by factors such as social class, race, gender, culture, and environment, ultimately impacting access to healthcare. We will also conduct cross-national comparisons of healthcare systems. Additionally, we will examine the historical and socio-political factors that shape the current healthcare delivery system in the United States, critically analyzing and questioning its framework. Lastly, we will explore the quality and cost of healthcare and discuss potential approaches to healthcare reform.

The primary objective of this course is to support undergraduate students who are interested in pursuing careers in medicine, public health, health sciences, and epidemiology. The Association of American Medical Colleges recognizes the significance of sociology in comprehending health, to the extent that 30% of the social and behavioral section of the MCAT now includes sociological content. With pre- medical students in mind, the course incorporates carefully selected readings, lectures, and assignments covering topics such as social relationships, cultures, institutions, stratification, inequalities, demographics, and social change. It is accessible to students from all majors and has no prerequisites.

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes Assessment Measures:

Office Hours Meeting (5%): You are expected to come to my office hours at least one time during the first part of the semester to chat with me about your experience and/or your personal process and engagement in the class. If my office hours do not work with your schedule, we can work together to find a time that works for us both. This one-on- one meeting is a requirement of the course, because I believe that the best learning happens when we are all personally invested in the material and the class as a learning community. The better I can understand your personal process and experience in the class, the better I can help facilitate your learning and success. To earn credit, you must

schedule this required meeting before December.

Attendance and active participation (10%): In a discussion-based course, student attendance and active participation is required and crucial to creating a successful learning community. This means coming to class ready to discuss the assigned material with observations, questions, and comments, as well as participating fully in any small and large group activities. Our class time is specially designed to prepare you for successful completion of the other graded components of the course. Thus, your full engagement and participation during our class time will directly and positively influence your grade more broadly. I expect each of you to come to class having finished all the readings and assignments for the day. I acknowledge some of you may be

uncomfortable in large group discussions, and so I recognize engaged listening and note taking as valid forms of participation. Still, consider preparing one specific point about the readings to address in class each day, or challenge yourself to speak up in class once a week in some way.

During class discussion, you should be intentionally aware of your participation, if you

find you are speaking repeatedly, step back and allow others the chance to speak up; conversely, if you notice that you are not speaking during class discussions, find ways to contribute to the conversation e.g. draw upon your notes for questions or previously formed thoughts.

Weekly Quizzes (20%): On Mondays, we will dedicate part of the class to administering quizzes. The question format will vary but it will mostly be multiple-choice questions on the topics covered the previous week. You will complete these quizzes individually and they will be evaluated on a pass/fail basis. If you answer more than half of the questions correct, you will pass. Rather than testing you, these quizzes are meant to provide you a list of keywords and concepts you should have learned that week. There will be 10 quizzes in total and there won’t be quizzes on Week 1, 9, 11, 13, and 15.

Social Determinants of Health Infographic (15%): Imagine you are a health provider or a public health expert and you are about to attend a meeting with different stakeholders to discuss an ongoing outbreak. Because the schedule is tight, you are only given 5 minutes to explain your case and you decide to use visual help that condenses all the points you wanted to make in a single representation. This assignment will allow you to apply the information and concepts you learn in the first half of the course on an actual public health outbreak. You are expected to approach a public health crisis from the perspectives of social epidemiology, public health, and medical sociology/anthropology. For this, you need to identify an ongoing epidemic or a similar public health phenomenon that affects a particular community either in the US or anywhere else in the globe. Some of the examples are the epidemics of opioid, suicide, depression, hepatitis, tuberculosis, HIV, avian influenza etc. Once you identify the location you will be looking at and the pathogen you will explore, you need to make a comprehensive list of all the social, political, economic, and historical factors that gave way to the particular epidemic you are investigating. While class readings will guide your analysis, you will also need to do your own research to access the most accurate and up-to-date information. You can use CDC or NHI databases as well as academic and journalistic sources. When you complete your research, you need to represent your findings visually—I am happy to offer alternative forms of representation should you need accommodation. Your purpose is to summarize your research in the form of an infographic or a similar format. You can use free online sources to create the infographic.

Midterm Exam (25%): On October 20th, you will take an in-class midterm exam. This will predominantly consist of multiple-choice questions with the possibility of several short answer questions. The exam will include all the topics we have covered so far, including that of the Week 8. You should be prepared to answer questions based on textbooks, academic articles, and lectures.

Final Exam (25%): The final exam will be similar to the midterm in format with the exception that you will be responsible for all the topics we covered since the beginning of the semester.

### Academic Policies:

- ### Academic Integrity

  Student-teacher relationships are built on trust. For example, students must trust that teachers have made appropriate decisions about the structure and content of the courses they teach, and teachers must trust that the assignments that students turn in are their own. Acts that violate this trust undermine the educational process. The Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities and The Graduate Student Supplement define various forms of Academic Dishonesty and you should make yourself familiar with these. In this class, all assignments that are turned in for a grade must represent the student’s own work. In cases where help was received, or teamwork was allowed, a notation on the assignment should indicate your collaboration.

  Violations of academic integrity may also be reported to the appropriate Dean (Dean of Students for undergraduate students or the Dean of Graduate Education for graduate students, respectively).

  If you have any question concerning this policy before submitting an assignment, please ask for clarification. In addition, you can visit the following site for more information on our Academic Integrity Policy: [Students Rights, Responsibilities, and Judicial Affairs](https://info.rpi.edu/dean-students/student-rights-responsibilities-and-judicial-affairs).
