## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

This course serves as an introduction to key concerns and theoretical frameworks in Science and Technology Studies, with an emphasis on canonical readings in philosophy, the social sciences, and the critical humanities. Across those areas, we focus on three clusters of concerns: political economy, subjectivity, and justice. These concerns have animated Science and Technology Studies scholars over the history of the inter-discipline and continue to animate them still. In each of these clusters, we will encounter key historical readings as well as contemporary authors who are engaged with earlier material, largely drawing from scholars working in the North Atlantic. Across the readings, we will engage with scholars across critical fields—cultural studies, ethnic studies, Indigenous studies, disability studies—as they seek to open the parameters of foundational theories that constitute the subjects of political economy, subjectivity, and justice as obligatorily white, cisgendered, heterosexual, ablebodied, and of the Global North. Instead, by diversifying the genealogical bases of the foundations of our concepts, we can constitute Science and Technology studies as a more inclusive, polyvocal inter-discipline that reworks the disciplinary basis of knowledge production and expertise.

We begin and end with key readings in Science and Technology Studies (Haraway, Latour, Shapin & Schaffer, Daston & Galison) to familiarize students with central concerns in the field. We then turn, in sequence, to discussions of political economy (particularly Marx and the Marxians), subjectivity (Freud and the post-psychoanalytic tradition, e.g., Butler), and questions of justice, democracy, and community. Throughout, we address canonical readings, their interpretations by close adherents who widen the application of the original theory, and then turn to critics from outside of their genealogies, often focusing on positions taken by Black, Indigenous, and disabled scholars. In so doing, the aim is both to demonstrate the relevance to each of these areas of concern to Science and Technology Studies and to demonstrate how Science and Technology Studies can become more inclusive in its conceptualization of the discipline, its objects, its participants, and its practices. We conclude the course by returning to key thinkers in Science and Technology Studies and consider their applicability for forwarding the inter-discipline as a radical (or, at least, anti-disciplinary) practice.

Students will complete a number of writing projects in the course that aim to help the student integrate their understanding of course material while also furthering their individual research. This includes three “position” papers that ask students to synthesize readings in relation to their research project as well as a grant application to support their research.

## Learning Outcomes

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes (CLOs):

- 1\) Acquire an understanding of key theoretical and methodological concerns in Science and Technology Studies,
- 2\) Acquire knowledge of the philosophical bases of Science and Technology Studies, with particular reference to ideas about political economy, subjectivity, and justice,
- 3\) Develop writing projects that further their understandings of their research projects in line with the above areas of concern,
- 4\) Develop a grant application for submission to a relevant funding agency.

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes Assessment Measures:

**A Sketch of a Project (10 pts)**

In 2 pages (~500 words), answer the following questions (likely in a paragraph each): What is your dissertation project? If you had to begin the project today, what would you focus on and how would you go about collected data about it? What kinds of questions are you asking, and with what theoretical bases? What methods will you employ? What’s at stake in your project? And how does it relate to concerns in Science and Technology Studies?

**Grant Application, Draft and Completed (20 pts each, 40 pts total)**

The default model for this assignment is NSF’s GRFP solicitation; if you are ineligible or would like to apply for a different grant, please discuss it with me as soon as possible, as it will shape the expectations of the assignment for you. (One possible alternative is the Wenner Gren’s Wadsworth fellowship [if you are doing “anthropological” work]). The GRFP comes in two parts, a 3 page “Personal, Relevant Background and Future Goals Statement” and a 2 page “Graduate Research Plan Statement.” They should each be formatted to fit 1” margins on all sides, in single-spaced 11 pt Times New Roman. These page counts are inclusive of all materials (references, figures, etc.). Please read the solicitation for details on the content and format of the application materials.

**Peer Review of Grant Applications (four at 5 pts each, 20 pts total)**

You will read the draft grant application of four of your peers and provide written assessments (~1 page, 300 words) of their merits. We will discuss the details of this assignment in more depth in class.

**Three Position Papers (three at 10 pts each, 30 pts total)**

At three points in the term, you will be asked to reflect on the contents of the previous section of the course in the context of your developing research project. In 5-7 pages (\~1500-2000 words), answer the following questions: (in \~600 words) What are the key concerns in this conceptual area? How are key ideas defined, refined, and contested?; (in \~300 words, based on empirical evidence) Describe an element of your research that is related to these key concerns; (in \~600 words) How does your research expand or challenge these key ideas? What are the limits of these ideas in relation to your research topic?

  Student-teacher relationships are built on trust. For example, students must trust that teachers have made appropriate decisions about the structure and content of the courses they teach, and teachers must trust that the assignments that students turn in are their own.
