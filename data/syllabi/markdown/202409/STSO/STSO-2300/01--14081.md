## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

It’s hot out there! The water is rising, there’s plastic in the fish, the pipes keep breaking, and the storms are only getting stronger. Our communities face a growing number of challenges, from the extreme (surviving an oil spill, a hurricane, a factory closure, or a heat wave) to the mundane (repairing a roof, turning on the air conditioning, finding an apartment, taking a shower, breathing outside). For sustainability minded people - or for anyone paying attention - it’s hard to ignore the distressing daily signals of ecological, social, and political crises that shape our lives and futures. Why is it like this, what should we do, and who decides?

No matter our role in the organization of resources and power for sustaining and nurturing life on this planet, we are members of complex and interdependent human political ecologies. We reproduce these ecologies all the time, as we make choices about what to buy, to do, to advocate for. We even reproduce these ecological systems just by virtue of reproducing the cells of our own bodies - as we eat, drink, and excrete, globally scaled and intimately experienced flows of resources and capital weave through our lives, metabolisms of socionature. At every scale, in our selves, our communities, and our cultures, ideologies of nature and society are used to obscure the roots of serious problems, lay blame on scapegoats, and normalize profound violence. Yet, time and time again, people find or create opportunities for rupture, to shift the structure, and to “pierce the future for hope.” This course provides students with a wide-ranging understanding of the relationships between humans, nature, power and difference. Drawing primarily on concepts and research from critical geography, political ecology, critical urban studies, post-colonial studies, and decolonial feminisms, the class will use land, labor, and history as tools for examining structures of power and inequality in the economic and ecological scales of our lives.

**How to read *actually read* the required materials:**

All readings for this class will be provided as PDFs. Students are responsible for saving their own copies of these documents and reading the assigned texts in advance of the class meetings in which they will be discussed. Sometimes we will assign podcasts or films.

Here’s the deal - you need to do your best to encounter the *whole* reading or assigned material before class. Skimming 20 minutes before class is a great pathway to a low grade. If you set aside at least 90 minutes to work through the reading, if you take some notes on what you notice, what you glean, and what you’re finding to be impenetrable, you are on the right track. Give yourself room to focus, and don’t rob yourself of the chance to be well prepared for the exams.

Many readings will have *some* dense theory sections, but every reading will be full of stories and details that are *very* *interesting and* every reading will have at least one sentence that blows your mind. So, if David Harvey’s paragraph on the theory of neoliberalism is too dense for you, do your best to keep moving so you can *actually* read the paragraph about the Iraq war or the NYC fiscal crisis.

Finally, don’t get intimidated or overwhelmed by a bunch of big words - keep track of the ones you keep seeing, and let’s make sure we go over them in class!

### Additional Course Specific DEI Information:

The Department of Science and Technology Studies aspires to create a learning environment in which people of all identities are encouraged to contribute their perspectives to academic discourse. This course has guidelines on language and terminology that aim to foster an

inclusive and welcoming environment, strengthen academic writing, enrich discussion, and reflect best professional practices.

Gender-inclusive and non-sexist language: Language is gender-inclusive and non-sexist when we use words that affirm and respect how people describe, express, and experience their gender. Gender-inclusive/non-sexist language acknowledges people of any gender (e.g. first year student versus freshman, chair versus chairman, humankind versus mankind). It also affirms non-binary gender identifications, and recognizes the difference between biological sex and gender expression. Students may share their preferred pronouns and names, and these gender identities and gender expressions should be honored.

Non-racist language: The labels and definitions of racial and ethnic categories vary across history, societies, and even situations. In this classroom, we will avoid racial and ethnic slurs and strive to use words that affirm and respect how people describe, express, and experience their race, ethnicity, ancestry, or cultural heritage. Please review this website for an overview of racial category terms, which discusses why some terms are offensive and others are preferred: https://raceandpedagogy.ssc.wisc.edu/getting-started/terminology/. Please note that this website is already somewhat outdated because it does not capitalize Black. Today, in the US context, Black and White should be capitalized, just like Latino/Latina/Latinx, Asian, Polish, Navajo, or any other term referring to a racial or ethnic category, Of course, there are many more racial and ethnic categories than those discussed on this website, and our language is always changing. Students may share the terminology that they prefer to describe their racial and ethnic identities, and these preferences should be honored.

Non-ableist language: Ableist language, which devalues people with physical and mental disabilities, permeates everyday language. However, there are many options for replacing common ableist rhetoric with more thoughtful alternatives. You are encouraged to read this guide and reflect on what you are communicating when you use ableist terms. https://www.autistichoya.com/p/ableist-words-and-terms-to-avoid.html

### Additional Course Information:

**Sexual Misconduct and Title IX:**

Title IX is a federal law prohibiting sex discrimination. The Title IX section of the Education Amendments Act of 1972 states, “No person in the United States shall, on the basis of sex, be excluded from participation in, be denied the benefits of, or be subjected to discrimination under any education program or activity receiving Federal financial assistance.” Under Title IX, sexual misconduct is considered a form of sex discrimination.

Sexual misconduct includes, but is not limited to: sexual harassment, sexual assault, dating and domestic violence, intimate partner violence, and stalking. Sexual misconduct is not tolerated at Rensselaer, and it is a violation of Institute policy, and state and federal law.

Faculty and other employees are considered mandated reporters when it comes to experiences of interpersonal violence (sexual assault, sexual harassment, dating or domestic violence, and stalking). Disclosures of interpersonal violence must be passed along to Rensselaer’s Title IX Coordinators who can help provide support and academic remedies for students who have been impacted. For more information or to report sexual misconduct, see

https://sexualviolence.rpi.edu/.
