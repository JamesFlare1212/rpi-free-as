## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

Integrates theory from psychology, neuroscience, and other social sciences into traditional economic models of human behavior. By relaxing the standard assumptions of perfect rationality and selfishness, more realistic modeling features such as loss aversion, present-bias, social norms, and habit formation improve economic analyses. These techniques are used to analyze individuals’ health behaviors, health insurance markets, and healthcare policy.

## Learning Outcomes

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes (CLOs):

- • demonstrate in-depth knowledge of the history of behavioral economics and its application;
- • compare and contrast the approaches of behavioral economics and standard economics to modern issues of human behavior;
- • construct a meaningful new research question in the field of behavioral economics; and
- • design an empirical method of assessing said research question

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes Assessment Measures:

You will be assessed on the above learning outcomes using a combination of quizzes, written exercises, and a research project. Specifically, you’re going to be asked to complete the following:

- Participation, Quizzes, and Miscellaneous Items: you are required to participate in all course discussions.
  - o **Quizzes:** each Tuesday, there will be a Quiz on the video lecture material covered for that week’s topic.
  - o **Discussions**: you can satisfy this part of your grade by contributing meaningfully to “in-class” discussion on CE and Paper Days. For CE days, this includes i) asking follow-up questions on video lecture material and ii) meaningfully participating in the communication exercise for that day (e.g. asking questions, contributing responses, engaging in group discussion). For Paper days, this includes i) signaling that you have read your assigned working paper, ii) asking engaging questions of your peers who read a different working paper, and iii) engaging in the student-led discussion for that day.
- Paper Discussions: you, along with 1 – 2 other students, will be responsible for leading a discussion of papers once throughout the semester. You are responsible for i) finding two papers on the module topic, ii) submit these papers to me for approval, iii) read your paper on your assigned day, and iv) lead discussion of your paper on your Paper day. I will lead discussion on Reference Dependence to give you a model of what this should look like.
- Research Project: since this course is Communication Intensive, there are several things required of you in this vein. To satisfy these requirements, you’ll be working in small groups (alone if you are registered for the 6000-level course). You will pick a topic and write several items, most due at the end of the semester, but present your work-in-progress along the way. We’ll talk more about this at a later date.

## Guidelines

Here I provide some additional guidelines for some graded items.

Paper Days

1. If you are not the discussant:
   1. Read the assigned papers
   2. Participate in the discussion of the papers
      1. Answer questions/provide comments about the paper that you were assigned
      2. Ask questions/ask for clarity about the paper that you were not assigned – these can be answered by your peers who did read that paper
   3. If you are the discussant:
      1. Find two additional papers on that week’s topic and submit to the me on LMS by the assigned due date.
      2. Read your assigned paper for the discussion.
      3. Prepare to guide/moderate the discussion of your paper
         1. Moderate the discussion – make sure that we hear from lots of voices, not just the same student talking all the time. Part of your grade will depend on how well you moderate the discussion.
         2. Suggestion: to politely “cut-off” someone who has been talking a lot, feel free to interject with “I’m so sorry for interrupting, but for the sake of time and to hear from other voices, could you wrap up your thoughts briefly?” Then give them a few sentences to wrap up their thoughts and then move on to other questions/topics.

Note: a given student talking a lot is not inherently bad – they probably have lots of great ideas to talk through! But it can make for a lack of diversity in thoughts/ideas discussed and we want to hear from everyone.

### Other Course Policies:

The overall structure of this course is that of a “flipped” classroom. This is where the vast majority of traditional lecture material is handled asynchronously through pre-recorded video lectures and normal lecture time is used for a combination of exercises, discussion, and presentations.

**Pre-Recorded Lectures:** each week/topic module you are responsible for viewing a pre-recorded video lecture uploaded to LMS. These will mostly be split into two sub-lectures (because does anyone really want to listen to me drone on for an hour at a time?) and the overall length will be about 1 hr. These will replace traditional in-person lecture.

**In-Class Exercises (Mondays):** (most) Mondays will be dedicated to a number of “Communication Exercises” (CE) and discussion of lecture material. These may take the form of short presentations (e.g. progress updates on the final project), writing exercises (e.g. working on project writing and getting feedback from peers), project labs (e.g. group discussion work to assist with constructing literature reviews), etc. On some CE days we will also discuss readings on the topic of the writing process in economics (i.e. Economical Writing). CE days will also be used for any necessary discussion of pre-recorded lecture material for the module so that you have a chance to ask clarifying questions of me and your peers.

**Paper Discussions (Thursdays):** Thursdays will be dedicated to “Paper Discussions” (PD). Each week/topic module you will be responsible for reading **one** paper on the theme of the week. I will lead the first Paper day, but otherwise Paper days will be lead by students (more on this later).
