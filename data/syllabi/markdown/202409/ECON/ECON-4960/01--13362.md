### Additional Information for Course Information Section:

This course applies economic analysis to explore the intersection of energy systems and societal values, investigating global energy consumption, climate change impacts, energy poverty and inequality, and strategies for decarbonization. We will explore the dynamics of nonrenewable and renewable energy markets, the implications of market failures, the role of policy interventions, and the evaluation of energy policies through cost-benefit analyses.

## Learning Outcomes

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes (CLOs):

- Participate in meaningful and respectful discussions on topics in energy policy.
- Recognize and explain market failures that warrant energy policy interventions.
- Describe the economic and societal impacts of using markets to supply and allocate energy.
- Develop the skills to evaluate the benefits and costs of different energy policies.

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes Assessment Measures:

- **Midterm and Final Exams (60%)**: The midterm and final exams each contribute 30% to your final grade. The exams will draw heavily from the homework questions and lecture notes, ensuring you are well-prepared through your ongoing coursework.
- **Homework Assignments (30%)**: Throughout the course, you will submit six homework assignments, each worth 5% of your final grade. These assignments are designed to help you consolidate your understanding of the course material and prepare for the exams.
- **In-Class Presentation (5%)**: You will deliver a 15-minute presentation on an energy policy of your choice. This presentation is an opportunity to explore a specific energy-related issue in-depth and will account for 5% of your total grade.
- **Participation (5%)**: Active participation is crucial and will make up the remaining 5% of your final grade. You are expected to engage in meaningful discussions during class, with the necessary readings provided at least one week in advance.

- ### Academic Integrity

  Student-teacher relationships are built on trust. For example, students must trust that teachers have made appropriate decisions about the structure and content of the courses they teach, and teachers must trust that the assignments that students turn in are their own. Acts that violate this trust undermine the educational process. The Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities and The Graduate Student Supplement define various forms of Academic Dishonesty and you should make yourself familiar with these.
