### Additional Information for Course Information Section:

In-class discussion

Proper and effective scientific discourse requires clear communication of ideas followed by open discussion amongst many scientists. This is especially true for multidisciplinary areas of science, like astrobiology, where people have different specializations and perspectives. It is important to acknowledge these differences (and the fact that no one person can know everything) and respect the specialization or expertise of those involved. Progress in multidisciplinary sciences is made by sharing knowledge and experience through asking and answering questions in diverse groups (like this class). Students should feel free to ask questions throughout in-class discussions (even though the answer might not be known) and, in fact, part of the course grade will depend on this. The only type of question that is unacceptable in this class is one that comes a lack of preparation for the day’s discussion (e.g., article was not read, or expertise of a guest lecturer was not looked into in advance).

Everyone in the class is expected to read any articles provided and to participate in the discussions. Student’s involvement will be evaluated by the instructors each week. Note that discussion participation must be meaningful to count toward your grade, and that vague or uninformed comments (e.g. “I didn’t understand the paper”) will not suffice.

NOTE: If it becomes apparent that students are not reading the literature prior to class, we reserve the right to enforce this by the addition of graded written assignments.

## Course Goals

### Goals:

Course Goals / Objectives This is an interactive readings and seminar course in which we will discuss current topics in astrobiology and the origins of life through the view of earth sciences, chemistry, biology, and astrophysics. Each week will consist either of a guest speaker or discussion of papers related to a previous speaker. Students are required to come prepared to seminar having read assigned papers, and be ready to fully participate in Q&A and discussion. The goal of the course is to provide broad exposure to current topics in Astrobiology, and hone reading, discussion, and synthesis skills.

Course Logistics

Course Structure: Each course meeting will consist either of a guest speaker presentation followed by Q&A, or by a general class discussion that focuses on a particular topic and several associated peer-reviewed journal articles.

Course Meetings: Class meetings will be held in Darrin 232. Attendance and participation are mandatory and will be included in assessment measures.

## Learning Outcomes

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes (CLOs):

- Synthesize and assess oral presentations and participate in oral discourse
- Critically read and evaluate scientific literature from multiple disciplines
- Effectively communicate and discuss scientific experiments and outcomes
- Recognize gaps in our current understanding of Astrobiology and the origins of life
- Critically evaluate the scientific literature with regard to topic
- Formulate research ideas and trajectories based on relevant literature in the field and findings in the student’s own research
- Manage and effectively stimulate class discussion
- Contextualize new research results, from oral presentation or published journal articles, into the broader astrobiology and origins-of-life research umbrella

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes Assessment Measures:

Course Assessment Measures (ISCI 4510)

This course is designed to be a readings, seminar, and discussion course in which students develop the skills to read, understand, and discuss the scientific literature as well as emerging scientific results presented orally. Students enrolled in ISCI 4510 will be responsible for (1) regular and meaningful participation in each class period (2) responding briefly in LMS to assess seminar presentations. As such, student participation is a critical part of achieving these outcomes. Students are expected to read articles prior to each class meeting, and come prepared to discuss the material. Therefore, active participation and attendance comprise more than half of the student’s final grade in the course. Instructors reserve the right to introduce graded assignments if students do not engage in discussion or come to class unprepared.

- In-class discussion/involvement (60%); Attendance (40%)

Course Assessment Measures (ISCI 6510)

This graduate level course is designed not only to enhance students’ comprehension and discourse skills, but also to help students develop the skills to synthesize new results and contextualize new research within their broader knowledge of Astrobiology. Graduate students enrolled in ISCI 6510 will each be in charge of introducing the speaker and leading the post-seminar discussion. A student’s final grade will depend on the quality and extent of participation in class discussions. Instructors reserve the right to introduce graded assignments if students do not engage in discussion or come to class unprepared.

- In-class discussion/involvement (50%); Discussion lead (20%); Attendance (30%)

### Additional Academic Integrity Course Policy and Penalty Information:

Academic Integrity This course will utilize a zero-tolerance policy for any form of cheating or other academic misconduct. All submitted assignments will be subjected to copying and plagiarism checkers. University policy deals with the problem of academic dishonesty, cheating, and plagiarism. The Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities and The Graduate Student Supplement define various forms of Academic Dishonesty and you should make yourself familiar with these. No violation will be tolerated in this class. The sanctions provided in this policy will be used to deal with any violations.

Academic dishonesty includes, but is not limited to, copying material from another student or use of written notes during an exam, representing others’ work as your own, changing answers on an exam paper after the paper has been graded, etc. Any student caught cheating will be assigned an F for the course. Furthermore, violations of academic integrity may also be reported to the appropriate Dean (Dean of Students or the Dean of Graduate Education for undergraduate and graduate students, respectively).

Student-teacher relationships are built on trust. For example, students must trust that teachers have made appropriate decisions about the structure and content of the courses they teach, and teachers must trust that the assignments that students turn in are their own. Acts that violate this trust undermine the educational process. In this class, all assignments that are turned in for a grade must represent the student’s own work. In cases where help was received, or teamwork was allowed, a notation on the assignment should indicate your collaboration.

If you have any question concerning this policy before submitting an assignment, please ask for clarification.

A special note on plagiarism. Please see the definition of plagiarism given below, as outlined in The Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights. To be clear, any time material that is not the students’ own is used, it should be in quotes and contain a citation. If a student paraphrases work from elsewhere, it also requires a citation. Furthermore, even when citations are used, others’ work cannot be used as a student response on an assignment. For example, in this class, if the main conclusions are given in an article, they cannot be used verbatim as a response in the post-discussion evaluation, even if a citation is present.

“Plagiarism Representing the work or words of another as one’s own through the omission of acknowledgment or reference. For example, using sentences verbatim from a published source in a term paper without appropriate referencing, or presenting as one’s own the detailed argument of a published source, or presenting as one’s own electronically or digitally enhanced graphic representations from any form of media.” – ACADEMIC INTEGRITY (2014-2016 Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights & Responsibilities, August 2014)
