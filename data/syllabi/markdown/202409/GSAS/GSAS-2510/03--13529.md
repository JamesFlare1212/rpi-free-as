## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

Intro to Game Design is a course that explores the basic theory and practical aspects of game design. This exploration extends to various forms of games and their cultural significance. The core methodology in this class involves crafting playable games through a rapid prototyping and iterative design process. While the course focuses on the creation of non-digital games, discussions about digital games will also take place. The coursework comprises several short assignments along with a final project. Each assignment requires students to write a design statement, explaining the game’s concept, gameplay mechanics, and overall design approach. For the final project, students will select and further develop a project that was previously completed in the class by refining its design and presentation.

## Course Goals

### Goals:

- Cultivate an understanding of what games are, both digital and analogue, as well as their mechanics that contribute to the formation of meaningful player experiences.
- Be familiar with the basic methodologies and conceptual skills of game design, including system and economic design, prototyping, playtesting, design iteration, and critique.
- Develop a collaborative mindset marked by a positive attitude, effective communication abilities, and a receptive mind for engaging with feedback.
- Forge connections between the course concepts and broader contexts, incorporating the dynamics of professional game industries as well as media and culture that are beyond games.

## Learning Outcomes

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes (CLOs):

## Grading Criteria

### Criteria Details:

Game Modification  
Group Size: 2  
Due: 09/06  
Each group will receive a simple card game for modification. The group should play and analyze the game, identify potential areas for enhancement, and subsequently iterate on the design to create an improved version.  
Mechanics Game  
Group Size: 3 - 4  
Due: 09/20  
Each group will receive a theme and a gameplay structure for making a tabletop game. The goal is to design a game that integrates the theme and mechanics, resulting in a meaningful and balanced gameplay experience.  
Narrative Game  
Group Size: 3 - 4  
Due: 10/04  
Each group will receive a brief narrative and a list of gameplay structures. Groups can choose a structure based on their own preferences. The goal is to explore the intersection of storytelling and games, with a specific focus on investigating how procedural representation can work with embedded narrative elements to produce a dramatic player experience.  
Scavenger Hunt  
Group Size: 4 - 5  
Due: 10/18  
The entire class will work together to make a scavenger hunt game! It will be divided into small groups, each responsible for designing a mini-game in a specific RPI building or location. The game should introduce players to RPI history and culture through engaging mechanics. Each group will use a digital tool provided by the instructor to design their portion of the game, which will be played by approximately 200 students in Intro to Psychological Science. The goal of this assignment is to explore the challenges of designing games for a large audience and enhancing public engagement.  
Digital Design  
Group Size: 2 - 3  
Due: 10/25  
Each group will use Machinations to design and simulate an interactive system. The goal is to use Machinations to model the game flow and mechanics, allowing the designer to refine the game's balance and overall design.  
GDD (Game Design Document)  
Group Size: 1 - 2  
Due: 11/01  
Working alone or in a pair, write a document (about 5 or less pages) to pitch a game design concept. Keep in mind that this is a documentation exercise, so you don’t need to make the game. The goal is to present your idea in an effective and professional manner.  
Final Project  
Group Size: 3 - 4  
Due: 12/10  
Each group can select one game that was previously made in this class and use the remaining semester to amplify and polish the visuals, design, mechanics, and player experience. It is noteworthy that every group must work on a different game. Also, groups can make a new game from scratch but it is not recommended. Students can join groups working on projects they were not initially involved in creating. The goal is to experience polished game design through the processes of research, project management, and production.

Plagiarism is defined as representing the work or words of another as one's own through the omission of acknowledgement or reference.
