### Additional Information for Course Information Section:

**Course Description**

This multidisciplinary course provides theoretical and methodological orientation to critical games research, and seeks to address both the broad and pragmatic implications of the question, “What does it mean to do research via games?” Through a hybrid lecture/workshop class format, students will gain experience with methodological research techniques in various disciplines game design draws from--visual arts, humanities and media studies, social science, and computer science--as well as independent research design and proposal writing.

As a core part of the “Critical Game Design” graduate curriculum, students will be introduced to standard research practices in conjunction with critical deconstructions of those research practices. Students will grapple with questions of power, construction of knowledge, positionality, and epistemic justice in research while developing their identity as researchers.

As a “Jam” course, assignments will be largely shaped by individual student interest, but will also feature collaborative planning and skills-sharing. Students will develop games-related research projects that employ diverse methodologies. Ideally, the proposals developed in the class will directly contribute to the student’s independent thesis project.

While the course readings, discussions, and design exercises will be the same, the design of this class also recognizes the different needs and timelines of Master’s students and Ph.D. students. Master’s students will be working closely with their advisors and each other to develop the methodology section of their Master’s projects, while Ph.D. students will be working more speculatively to survey the field of game design research methods, and develop first drafts of a research proposal.

## Learning Outcomes

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes (CLOs):

- Distinguish between methodology and methods.
- Articulate the methodological formations and practices of several disciplines that contribute to game design and game studies.
- Identify a research site (as broadly defined by diverse fields), and derive a research question from that site.
- Apply a research methodology to a research question.
- Develop research methods, derived from their selected methodology, appropriate to their research question.
- Contextualize their research within larger structures of academic institutions, knowledge structures, and power structures.
- Articulate their own positions as researchers within disciplines and systems of power.

**Project Descriptions**

# **Brownbag Exercise**

Due Date: Varies per student, Week 9 through Week 12

Beginning in Week 9, two-three students each week will present a one-hour brownbag session of their current work. Brown bags are not polished research presentations; rather, they are work-in-progress talks where students present their current research interest, potential research questions, ideal methods, and literature backgrounds. Most importantly, student presenters bring questions and other issues they are currently facing with their project to the class,

Each student in the class will participate in the brownbag activity once during the semester, The students presenting brownbags that week will act as a co-instructor with the faculty instructor of the course for the week of their reading assignment. During their brownbag cycle, the student will:

- Prepare three (3) readings that provide conceptual, empirical, or methodological background for their project. The student presenter will select one of these readings as a required reading for that week’s class, while the other two readings will serve as supplementary material. As such, each brownbag week will have 3 required readings (one from each presenter) and 6 supplemental, non-required readings. The three readings will be posted on the class Discord page the Tuesday before (the week before) their presentation.

- Present for 15 minutes on their research topic. This presentation will be informal, but should have some visual aids to help guide the class along. The presenter can assume that the other students in the class have read the required reading. Each presentation should highlight:
  - o The background of the project
  - o The potential research question
  - o Some potential methods for exploring that question
- Work with the class instructor to run a 30-minute discussion of their topic/the reading/their project. The goal of this discussion is to generate feedback on their project and to further develop some new research directions and methods for their project. Each student will be given 30 minutes, with 15 minutes of flex/break time between each brownbag session.

# **Peer Review Workshops**

Each student will be responsible for peer-reviewing outline drafts. You will return your peer review feedback documents to your peer review partner and submit a copy to me. For each peer review, the student will be responsible for assessing:

- The presence or absence of the major components of the project proposal outlined
- The quality of writing and of ideas in each of those sections
- Intellectual unity and consistency throughout the proposal document
- Appropriateness of the methodology for the research question
- Appropriateness of the methods derived from the methodology
- The writer’s understanding of positionality in research
- Overall scope of the project

# **Final Project: The Research Document and Lightning Talk**

Every student will be responsible for submitting a research proposal by the end of class. This research proposal, ideally, will serve as the backbone of the student’s dissertation project proposal and external funding proposals, or will contribute to the student’s analysis of their project’s findings, successes, and failures.

The Research Proposal will be due in three steps: an outline of each half of the proposal, and a final submission, each respectively due over the final three weeks of class. In the final class you will give a 15 min lightning talk on your research project accompanied by a slide deck.

# **The Research Proposal**

Length: Draft: 7 pages, single spaced, 1” margins, 12-point Times font (in accordance with HASS Fellowship guidelines)

**Due Date:** December 12, 2023

Every student will be responsible for submitting a research proposal by the end of class. This research proposal, ideally, will serve as the backbone of the student’s dissertation project proposal and external funding proposals.

The structure of the Research Proposal draft is derived from the HASS Fellowship model, ideally to make application for internal funding easier for the student:

1. **Background.** Summarize your field of study and the focus of your dissertation research. Include a description of your background, prior experience, and preparation for this inquiry. Situate your work relative to current and historical research in the field, providing citations for relevant publications and sources.

2. **Research Questions/Creative Objectives.** List your research questions; the issues, hypothesis, or problems that you will address.

3. **Methodology.** Detail the methodological procedures you will follow to address your questions. How is this approach appropriate for your topic and consistent with best practices in your field?

4. **Timeline.** Provide a timeline for the completion of your research and dissertation, listing deadlines by which you hope to accomplish major goals.

5. **Impact**. Describe the larger cultural significance of your research. How will your work impact critical issues across disciplinary boundaries?

6. **Assessment.** Describe the strengths and limitations of your work. How will you assess your results?

7. **Future Goals.** What are your future research and career goals?

The structure of the final Research Proposal retains the structure of the draft, but expands upon the Background, Methodology, and Impact sections. Students will be expected to incorporate critical and deconstructive insights from course readings and discussions into those sections. In particular, students will more clearly articulate the epistemic and political implications, limitations, strengths, and dangers of their research project, as well as their own personal and structural position in relation to their research. Students must also discuss their plans for mitigating, where possible, some of the larger structural and epistemic limitations and dangers of their project.

### Additional Academic Integrity Course Policy and Penalty Information:

**Attendance**

Students are expected always to be present during class and recitations. Attendance will be taken at the beginning of each class. Excellence in submitted work will not make up for delinquency in attendance. More than two unexcused absences will result in a lowering of your final course grade by one mark for each unexcused absence after 3. More than five absences will result in the failure of the course. Three late arrivals will equal one missed class. If you must miss a class, assignments are due before the class period begins. Excusable absences include illness, family emergencies, and scheduled Rensselaer athletic events. All excused absences must be delivered to the professor via the Office of Student Life.

**Academic Integrity**

Student-teacher relationships should be built on trust. Students should be able to trust that teachers have made responsible decisions about the structure and content of the courses they teach, and teachers must trust that the assignments students turn in are their own. Acts that violate this trust undermine the educational enterprise and contradict the very reason for your being at Rensselaer. *The Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities* defines various forms of academic dishonesty and procedures for responding to them. The policies laid out in the *Handbook* are intended to maintain a community of trust and will be strictly enforced. Please review these policies.

For this course, the following penalties will apply:

- Significant acts of plagiarism (e.g., text copied verbatim from an unidentified source): Failure of the course and a written judgment in the student’s official record,
- Minor acts of plagiarism (e.g., referencing the findings of others without appropriate citations): Failure of the assignment, plus reduction of final course grade by one letter grade.

- Other acts of academic dishonesty: Penalties range from a warning to reduction of final grade by one letter grade to failure of the course, depending on the severity of the violation as determined by the instructor. As is evident above, penalties for plagiarism are significant. All direct use of another person’s words must be placed inside quotation marks. You must also indicate where you paraphrase another’s work and where you borrow another’s specific ideas or interpretations. If you have questions regarding proper citation practices, see the instructor for clarification *before* the assignment is submitted. While collaboration is encouraged throughout the course, others cannot do work for you. All assignment activities must be carried out by the individual or team members submitting the assignment for a grade. Other people may show you *how* to do something (say, when using computer software), but you must follow up by doing the work yourself. *The Rensselaer Handbook* provides specific procedures by which a student may appeal a grade. You should speak to the professor before initiating an appeal. If this does not lead to satisfactory resolution, you have the option of appealing your grade by writing to the head of the Department no later than 10 days after your grade has been posted.
