## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

“This course is designed to provide an overview of the art of effective story development. Students will come away with a heightened awareness of the structures and principles that master storytellers apply to their craft in games and other popular media. It is intended for all GSAS majors and other interested students, as well as the prerequisite for the GSAS concentration in Writing.”

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes Assessment Measures:

# 2. Criteria Demonstration

This section provides suggested means for students to demonstrate fulfillment of each assessment criterion. The provided list is not intended to be comprehensive. Students are encouraged to collaborate with the class community to identify other possible means of demonstration. The instructor must approve any alternative means of demonstrating fulfillment of assessment criteria.

## 2.1 Course Skill Proficiencies

For this course students will generally demonstrate proficiency in course skills through a set of key deliverables. In some cases the suggestion allows choice between a few possibilities. Explanations of suggested proficiency demonstration for each skill are presented below. Individual course proficiencies can be assigned + or – ratings, leading to an overall + or – for the criterion as a whole, the same way that criteria demonstrations affect the final grade.

### 2.1.1 Write Short Narrative Fiction

The student can write a cohesive short story that demonstrates:

- Consistent worldbuilding
- Cohesive plot structure
- Robust characterization
- Integration of theme
- Effective, grammatical use of prose

**Key deliverables****:**

- Write a 1,000 – 3,000 word short story
- Revise the story at least once

### 2.1.2 Analyze narrative in games

For a game that they have played, the student can comment intelligently on:

- The relationship between gameplay and narrative
- Characterization and character development
- Plot structures (e.g., acts and scenes, plot arc, climax, hero’s journey)
- Literary and narrative techniques (e.g., motif, foreshadowing)
- Representation

AND use at least one concept in existing game narrative scholarship to describe how those elements contribute to (or distract from) the themes and/or messages of the game’s narrative

**Key deliverables:**

- Write a polished hierarchical outline for a game narrative analysis
- Write a Game Narrative Review following the format used by the Game Developer’s Conference

### 2.1.3 Engage with game narrative analysis

The student can accurately explain the argument of a piece of game narrative scholarship, including the general theoretical approach employed. Additionally, they can offer a balanced critique of an existing analysis of a game narrative, scholarly or otherwise.

**Key deliverables:**

- Create a polished hierarchical outline of an existing scholarly analysis of a game narrative
- Write a one paragraph summary of an existing scholarly analysis of an existing game narrative
- Write a short response (300-500 words) to an existing analysis of a game narrative, scholarly or otherwise

### 2.1.4 Explain key terms and concepts in game storytelling

The student can effectively explain at least 50 terms and concepts from the course vocabulary list, which will be provided online and collaboratively edited as the course progresses.

**Students can demonstrate effective explanation in a variety of ways, including:**

- Correctly using and explaining it in the context of any other deliverable for the course

- Giving a 5 minute presentation on it during a session
- Verbally explaining your understanding of the concept to the instructor, teacher’s assistant, or certified class member and answering any follow-up questions
- Correctly responding to a question about the term on the final exam

### 2.1.5 Manage your time

The student will be able to develop and adapt a plan for completing the work of the course in the context of their other duties and commitments, including their duty to take care of their own well-being. They will be able to make regular progress on course work and submit work in a timely manner that respects the time of the instructor, teaching, assistant, and other students. *Note that this skill m**ay not be used for criterion 3, demonstrating excellence in a course skill.*

**Demonstration of effective time management calls for a specific set of actions:**

- **Plan****:** By the fourth session of the course, student will develop a formal plan for managing their time that semester, including their planned task management approach and a time allocation plan that takes into account their other courses and obligations.
- **Adapt:** The student will provide evidence of having adapted their plan at least once per round.
- **Systematize:** The student will provide evidence of utilizing a task management system, either idiosyncratic or following an established system such as GTD, Kanban, or Time Blocking
- **Execute:** The student will submit deliverables and revisions before their deadlines and complete expected preparations before course sessions, with an 80% success rate and no key deliverable grossly behind schedule

## 2.3 Excellence in a Course Skill

This criterion calls on each student to determine which course skill best fits their interests, goals, and aptitude. Having determined that, the student will focus extra attention and effort on developing that skill, with the goal of producing work that they can be truly proud of. In many cases, this means continuing to revise and/or expand a portfolio piece well beyond the level of proficiency. In some cases, this means producing one or more additional portfolio pieces that demonstrate the skill. In all cases, a few guidelines apply:

- The student must demonstrate proficiency in a course skill before demonstrating excellence
- The student must coordinate with the instructor so that they can collaborate to develop an effective plan for the work
- Excellence is about process; the student should seek regular feedback from the instructor, who will explain what needs to be done to bring the work to the expected standard of excellence
- Whereas some proficiency demonstrations take ephemeral forms, like an oral defense, excellence demonstrations will usually take enduring forms
- A larger-scale use of a skill to benefit community outside the class is a very fitting way to demonstrate excellence

Work demonstrating excellence should take a form that builds on the student’s expertise. This is an opportunity to creatively bring one’s unique skills to bear on the content of the course. As such, the work can take a wide variety of forms, as long as clear expectations are worked out with the instructor.

## 2.4 Benefit Another Community

Like all communities, our classroom community is connected to and nested within a number of other communities. A foundational principle of the course is that our work together is for the benefit of communities outside the classroom. If our work together has no potential to benefit others, we should change what we are doing. To fulfill this criterion, a student must apply a course skill in taking an action that benefits community outside the course in some way. Examples will be discussed in class, but the following guidelines apply:

- “Community,” here, should be a physical rather than a virtual community, and should be distinct from either the student’s family or friend group
- The student must coordinate with the instructor so that they can collaborate to develop an effective plan for the work

## 2.5 Passion

This criterion is, admittedly, the most subjective. In most cases, however, it will not require any additional work to be submitted by the student. Instead, as the student participates in the course and submits work, their enthusiasm for at least one aspect of the course should become clear. In the end, if a student’s passion for anything in the course is difficult for them to identify or the instructor to observe, the student and instructor will communicate about this to help uncover points of connection with the course.

# 4. Deliverables

Students submit a variety of deliverables throughout the semester, and each deliverable contributes to the portfolio of work demonstrating fulfillment of the assessment criteria. **Deliverables are due by the beginning of the class session.** *(See Section 2.1.5, “Manage your time” for information on deadlines and assessment)*

Deliverables are divided into three categories: *Basic, Key, and Expertise*.

## 4.1 Basic Deliverables

Although not flashy, and generally not polished, the basic deliverable is the workhorse of the course, providing students with essential practice on course skills, opportunities for immediate feedback, and potential seed projects that can grow into key deliverables

- Small-scale work, emergent from the work of an individual session
- Generally submitted in a single draft and not revised
- Deadlines and expectations finalized during the closing period of a session
- Generally be the sort of work that can be completed before the next session
- At times, they might be smaller tasks related to progress on a key deliverable

## 4.2 Key Deliverables

The larger scope and relative independence offered by key deliverables make them essential for demonstrating proficiency in course skills.

- The work of a round, taking multiple weeks to complete
- Polished work, often revised for a second draft
- Initial submission due at the penultimate session of each round
- Revision deadlines set by instructor based on necessary work

## 4.3 Expertise Deliverables

Expertise deliverables should be the most polished work submitted by the student. A good rule of thumb for what makes a successful expertise deliverable is that it should be something that the student could include as part of their wider portfolio and/or resumé outside of the course.

- The work of a semester
- Highly polished work, revised multiple times
- Initial submission due at the end of the third round
- Final revisions due by the penultimate session

## Grading Criteria

### Criteria Details:

# 1. Assessment Criteria

Students will be assigned grades in the course based on demonstrating fulfillment of five basic criteria:

**Passing Criteria**

1. Proficiency in each of the course skills
2. Notable contribution to the class community

**Other Criteria**

3. Excellence in one of the course skills
4. Use of a course skill to benefit a community outside the class
5. Passion for at least one aspect of the course

## 1.1 Passing Criteria

The first two criteria (proficiency in course skills and contribution to class community) are designated as *passing criteria*. In order to be assigned grade D in the course, the student must demonstrate fulfillment of the *passing criteria*. Even if all other criteria are fulfilled, if the *passing criteria* are not fulfilled, the student will fail the course.

## 1.2 Other Grades

Other grades will be assigned according to the following table:

| **Grade** | **Criteria** |
| --- | --- |
| D | Passing criteria only |
| C | Passing criteria + 1 other |
| B | Passing criteria + 2 others |
| A | All 5 criteria met |

### 1.3.1 Pluses and Minuses

Each criteria can be met minimally (-), at expectation, or exceptionally (+). If the number of minuses exceeds the number of pluses, the grade will be lowered to a minus. If the number of pluses exceeds the number of minuses, the grade will be raised to a plus.

## 3.2 Round

The course divides the semester into five self-contained periods called rounds. In some ways they are equivalent to "sprints" in agile project management. Each round will be defined by a starting question, one or more games, one or more analytical approaches, and some key deliverables. Games and analytical approaches will be determined collaboratively as the semester progresses. They will generally last 5 sessions, but may be adapted to meet the needs of the course.

| **Round** | **Dates** | **Starting Question** | **Key Deliverables** |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 8/30 - 9/24 | What is the relationship between games and stories? | Short story first draft Existing analysis outline Existing analysis summary Existing analysis response |
| 2 | 9/27 - 10/11 | How do we create and analyze characters? | New Analysis Outline GNR Elements & Highlight GNR Characters |
| 3 | 10-15 - 10/28 | How do we structure and analyze plots? | GNR Lessons GNR Breakdown |
| 4 | 10/31 - 11/15 | How do we build and think about secondary worlds? | Short story final draft GNR complete first draft |
| 5 | 11/19 - 12/6 | Where do we go from here? | GNR final draft |

### Round Structure

Rounds 2-4 will tend to have a similar basic structure, although the details will adapt to the questions and goals that emerge.

#### Session 1

Introduce and engage with the starting question of the round; identify possible directions and means of exploration, including existing scholarship and games to engage

#### Session 2

Deep dive on concepts and techniques relevant to the round; practice related skills

#### Session 3

Discuss existing scholarship and games

#### Session 4

Explore themes of the round through shared play experiences

#### Session 5

Debrief and tie up loose ends, begin transition to question and goal for next round

## 3.3 Session

The course is composed of 28 sessions, each of which is 110 minutes long. As with the semester and the round, the dynamic middle section is framed by a relatively stable beginning and end. Although a planned schedule is provided below, it should be noted that this schedule is intended to flexibly structure, not to rigidly restrict, the flow of a session.

Following the opening, students will generally workshop either work in progress or work newly submitted.
