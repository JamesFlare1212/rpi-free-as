## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

EGD meets once per week, as an intensive 3.5-hour intensive studio class. The expectation is a *minimum* of 10 hours per week (including class time). Usually it ends up being significantly more. Students will be given a thematic “prompt” every three weeks, which they must respond to with a creative project. The goal is to break rules, push boundaries, and do a series of works that are conceptually compelling. The medium and method is being left completely open. Creative use of AI is strongly encouraged. You could choose to do:

- A playable prototype similar to what you might do in an extended game jam
- A game design document that includes concept art, diagrams, code snippets, etc.
- A purely analog game (no computer required to play)
- A mixed-media analog/digital game (computer combined with physical media)
- An ARG/LARP (documented live-action role play)
- A sound art/composed piece of music
- An art installation or performance piece

Experimentation is the name of the game! Work can be either solo or collaborative. The flow will be roughly as follows:

- Week 1: Project pitch (either to class, if recruiting, or one-on-one)
- Week 2: Project progress update (either to class or one-on-one)
- Week 3: Project presentation (to class, play/view session as appropriate)

## Learning Outcomes

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes (CLOs):

- ● Successfully deliver refined concepts and prototypes on a short deadline
- ● Hone ability to judge appropriate project scope
- ● Work independently or in pairs, exercising a wide breadth of production skills
- ● Find or sharpen a creative identity or personal style

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes Assessment Measures:

Over the course of the semester, you will start and finish (accounting for breaks, etc.) **4 projects** In total. As a result of this intensive assignment structure, the prototypes represent the entirety of your submitted assessment. There will be no assigned readings, no additional homework outside of these weekly projects, and no written assessment.

**Tools**

You can choose to do all projects with the same tool/platform (e.g. Unity, Unreal, Roblox, etc.), or mix and match. It’s up to you.

**Groups**

The course was originally designed with the idea that students would work independently, or in groups of no more than two. This was to push your individual creativity in response to each week’s prompt, and avoid situations where in larger collaborative groups there is often a very unequal division of labor. Such an approach assumes a certain level of comfort with prototyping/building interactive systems, which typically in GSAS means using a “game engine” of some sort. I put that in quotes because I like to think broadly about what constitutes a game engine. It could be anything that inspires play, and introduces some sort of “mechanic” and interaction - experimental writing, video, object making, etc.. Thus, even for those of you that identify primarily as writers, or 2D/3D artists, or sound designers, there should be plenty of creative leeway for you to come up with viable responses to prompts if/when working solo.

**Submissions**

Projects must be submitted by **MIDNIGHT** on the night before the completed project presentation to the class (i.e. Tuesday nights),and linked to the class Discord assignment channel. This gives me a chance to look at things prior to class presentations. If a digital game, please zip your project before submission and use the following filename format:

**Project [Num] [Last Name(s) of creator(s)]**

Example: *Project 1 Doe*

Example: *Project 2 Doe Smith Park*

You should also bring your own copy of the game to class (either on your own computer or downloaded and available on one of the university’s computers) for demonstration purposes. If you are unable to attend class due to illness or other extraordinary circumstances, let the professor and/or TA know beforehand.

## Grading Criteria

### Criteria Details:

To achieve a high grade, a project must be successful in its aims and demonstrate a sense of style and inventiveness. The point of making these projects/prototypes is to test the value of new, original ideas. These ideas can be mechanical, technical, audiovisual, or conceptual in nature — experimentation and originality are the most important outcomes here.

You will **not** be graded on “polish” or the sheer amount of content you produce. Not every submission needs to be “amazing.” The main goal is to demonstrate an interesting experimental approach to the topic. Failed experiments can be as or more “educational” than “successful” ones.

Reuse of elements from previous projects is fine, especially if you decide you would like to continue developing a project in relation to a subsequent prompt.

Each project will be worth 25 points. Your final grade for the semester will be based on the cumulative points awarded for the four projects. Being a good citizen, participating in discussions, and going above and beyond expectation on a given prompt will help push you over a grading threshold.

**Grade Breakpoints**

- A 100 – 93 Excellent: outstanding effort
- A - 092 – 90
- B + 089 – 87
- B 086 – 83 Above Average: notable effort
- B - 082 – 80
- C + 079 – 77
- C 076 – 73 Average: marginal effort
- C - 072 – 70
- D + 069 – 67
- D 066 – 60 Passable: minimal effort
- F 059 – 0 Failure: major issues with assignment completion

**Grading Criteria**

Your grade will be based on concept and function. Is the concept clear and provocative? Can you articulate what makes it experimental? It will also take into account “style.” To what degree have you honed your own creative identity? Are your projects memorable in the way they look, sound, feel, read, or play? If your project is late, fails to run during class, or demonstrates a noticeable lack of effort, your overall grade will take a significant hit.

  Student-teacher relationships are built on trust. For example, students must trust that teachers have made appropriate decisions about the structure and content of the courses they teach, and teachers must trust that the assignments that students turn in are their own. ### Additional Academic Integrity Course Policy and Penalty Information:

**Attribution & Permission**

Don’t take credit for work you didn’t produce! (e.g. images, music, etc). Clearly indicate the origin of any materials you source externally and where exactly you got them, so it’s clear which parts of your submission are originally authored/created, and which have been appropriated, reworked, and how.

For any materials you share publicly (e.g. putting your game on itch.io) you need to consider **copyright** obligations. For example: do you have permission to use a particular tune? Did you ask the creator? Is it public domain or Creative Commons?

For any materials you share publicly you also need to consider **privacy** obligations. For example: for anybody identifiable in a photo or video clip you take, do you have their permission to show them?
