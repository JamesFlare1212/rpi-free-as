## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

This course will introduce students to the genre of the literary short story. The primary objectives of this course are to write thoughtful and effective short fictions in this genre, engage meaningfully in an artistic community of peers, practice listening and nuanced thinking, and become more creatively fearless. Through reading and discussion, short writing exercises, and participation in a writing workshop, students will learn to recognize diverse elements of effective short story writing and be given plenty of opportunities to practice. Every student will leave the course with a toolkit of ideas for fiction and a final portfolio of creative writing, including two polished stories.

## Learning Outcomes

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes (CLOs):

- Students will demonstrate knowledge of fiction writing in general and the short story in particular by utilizing appropriate terminology to analyze and discuss published fiction.
- Students will demonstrate craft-based reading strategies through annotations, short analytical responses, and discussions.
- Students will establish a consistent creative practice by regularly producing short creative writings.
- Students will demonstrate engagement in the writing process through observing, drafting, peer critique, and revision.
- Students will actively engage in a creative community of peers through participation in workshop and discussion.
- Students will synthesize learning by constructing a learning portfolio that showcases their skills and development in the field of fiction writing over the course of the semester.

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes Assessment Measures:

**Reading.** In this course, you will learn to read like a writer, which is somewhat different than the reading you may be used to in English classes. The expectation is that as you engage with these texts you are “looking behind the curtain” to determine how the artistic effects were achieved and preparing to discuss your theories with others. Reading should be an active endeavor.

**Short Exercises (SE).** These are short creative writing prompts designed to give you practice in one particular skill. There is no minimum length so long as the prompt is thoughtfully addressed. Maximum length is one double-spaced page. Short exercises should be posted in your SE journal.

**Craft Discussion Boards.** The purpose of these posts is to help you organize your thoughts before class and for us to get discussions started. You can think of these as brief educated reading reflections (as opposed to informal opinions or book reviews, for example) that set the tone for fruitful in-class discussions. These are not essays and no thesis is required. The length requirements here are flexible; I am simply interested in seeing you engage with the texts using course terminology and frameworks and hearing your questions and ideas. The writing style need not be formal, but it must engage with the reading by using specific evidence and end with a question that will help open class discussions. See rubric at the end of this syllabus.  
**Workshop.** Instruction in this course follows the guidelines established by the Association of Writers and Writing Programs (AWP) for the teaching of creative writing, which include a “challenging writers’ workshop” as a hallmark.  
**End Comments.** Good feedback can be short or long, so I won't impose an arbitrary length on yours, but you must include all components.  
**Observation Journal.** This is simply a place for your to record your observations about the world around you, which includes your internal landscape.  
**Creative Portfolio:** Rather than producing a major new work at semester’s end, you will instead compile and revise a portfolio of work that includes the components listed in the rubric and anything else you feel represents your learning this semester.

- ### Academic Integrity

  Student-teacher relationships are built on trust. For example, students must trust that teachers have made appropriate decisions about the structure and content of the courses they teach, and teachers must trust that the assignments that students turn in are their own. Acts that violate this trust undermine the educational process. The Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities and The Graduate Student Supplement define various forms of Academic Dishonesty and you should make yourself familiar with these.
