## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

To develop an understanding of strategies and practices involved in marketing technologically oriented products and services. To see if and where these strategies differ from marketing of non-technical products/ services, and to determine how and why they vary. The purpose of this course is NOT to give you AN answer or a perspective, because there is none. The objective of this course is to follow a more broad-based strategy by exposing you to different schools of thought in this area along with their respective advantages and limitations.

In general, this course will focus on honing your market analysis skills to leverage decision-making in the high-tech context. I hope that this course will enhance your skills in analyzing industry trends, identifying threats and opportunities, designing suitable products and marketing strategies to best suit market/ environmental conditions, customer segmentation and analysis, and in assessing/monitoring a firm’s relative advantage via competitive intelligence.

The attached course outline should be used as a reference, but will change as the semester proceeds. I will announce any changes for the upcoming week before the end of each class meeting.

## Course Goals

### Goals:

OUTCOMES

- Students will be able to understand the principles of marketing of high technology products. This will include product planning, pricing, segmentation, distribution, advertising, sales promotion, selling and sales force management, legal issues, research and product service.
- Students will understand how marketing high technology products differs from marketing products that are less technology intensive.
- Students will be able to apply the principles to business situations and demonstrate mastery of decision making through the study of business cases and videos.
- Students will learn how to analyze complex marketing situations in the marketing of high technology products and be able to make recommendations.

Think about it. High-tech products intimidate many customers where other products wouldn't. And technology is a market relatively open to competition; one new technical innovation, and you're next biggest threat is in the game. As if those weren't challenges enough, the speed of technological change can make your current marketing strategy obsolete overnight.

High-tech products and services are introduced in turbulent, chaotic environments, where the odds of success are often difficult to ascertain at best and stacked against success at worst. Although many more marketing issues require high-tech adaptations, the keys are: (1) to systematically examine what are the critical distinctions between high-tech environments and more traditional environments, and (b) to logically modify decision-making to account for those distinctions.

The cases we will study deal primarily with large business companies. I hope to give a blend of personal insights as well from my own experience in business marketing and sales. Using these approaches, we will give you a broad exposure to business marketing challenges and concepts.

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes Assessment Measures:

**Cases** - we will generally study one case each week. All written case reports should be individual work, not the work of a team, unless you are part of the group leading the discussion.

This course is taught using cases – and I have found that many students have limited experience with case teaching and might feel a little bit apprehensive about what is going to happen. This section contains some advice on how to prepare for a case class (as a student) and how to conduct yourself during the classroom discussion.

Case teaching differs from regular lectures in that the bulk of the speaking is done by the students, who discuss a case (a written description of a business problem) bringing their individual experience and their understanding of the relevant literature as presented in the course. The teaching method is used in business studies, but also in law and medicine – disciplines where students after graduation will be faced with problems that require understanding a complicated situation, applying relevant theory and common sense to it, and formulating and testing a solution. Much like in real life, cases are written so that there is no correct solution – you will find that you and your classmates may have radically different ideas about the nature of the described situation and the best way to resolve it. That’s fun, and that is where the learning is.

I use case teaching because it allows me to make teaching relevant, lets the students apply theory and judgment to real problems, and enables students to learn from each other and not just from the teacher. (For a great article on the history of the case method by a great case teacher, see David Garvin’s "Making the case: Professional education for the world of practice." Harvard Magazine 106(1): 56ff. To see why understanding theory is important in terms of practice, we recommend the Clayton M. Christensen and Michael E. Raynor’s "Why Hard-nosed Executives Should Care about Management Theory." Harvard Business Review September 2003): 67-74.)

One group of 4 to 5 students will be chosen at the beginning of the semester to lead each discussion by briefly summarizing the facts of the case, then highlighting the key issues that management in the case must resolve, and why. These introductory remarks should take about 5 minutes. The students will lead the class analyzing the case in depth and propose alternatives, solutions and recommendations with appropriate rationale. In many cases, careful analysis of the numbers (e.g. analyzing break-even) in the case may help identify the most promising approach for management to take. The cases will generally deal with issues related to the assigned readings in the Armstrong text and should help illustrate the theory in the text. The student(s) leading the discussion will be required to turn in a copy of their PowerPoint presentation through the LMS by the beginning of class but will **not** be required write an analysis. **Discussion leaders should encourage student participation by both in-person and online students, for example by building a few surveys/polls of the students into the case analysis.**

***Class participation in the case discussions whether during class or in the online discussion will form a significant portion of your grade!***

Students will turn in a detailed analysis of the key issues and the student’s recommendations by the beginning of each class. Papers should be approximately three pages typed, double-spaced, 12 point font, 1 inch margins on all sides, plus any tables and appendices. Written cases submitted up to 48 hours late will be marked down 5 points. Papers will be accepted over 48 hours late at with a larger mark-down.

The format of the detailed analysis should be similar to our class discussions: a very short (a couple of sentences) summary of the situation as you see it; identification of the key issues facing management and why you think they are the key issues; and your recommendation of the proper course(s) of action for management and the supporting analysis. There are questions in the syllabus and in some of the cases that should be explicitly answered in the papers.

It is important to connect your analysis to the content of the text and lectures (PowerPoints). Please footnote 2 connections per paper in January, 4 per paper in February and 6 per paper in March and after. You should not include any footnotes of the case itself.

**Written assignments must be submitted through Safe Assign** in the Blackboard Learning Management System (LMS).

**Marketing Project** – Identify in writing 8 specific instance of marketing in your daily life. The marketing instances have to be reflective of 8 points in the PowerPoint lectures or the textbook. Please create a 2-minute PowerPoint presentation to present to class. Identify the slide or page number in the PowerPoint slide notes that the 8 points refer to. Include at least 2 specific examples of segmentation and 2 specific examples of pricing theory. Your PowerPoint will be due by the beginning of class **Tuesday April 19th** and you will present your PowerPoint the next week.

**Exams** – There will be two exams for this course. The exam questions will cover material from the Armstrong text and class lectures. The second exam will cover the second half of the course and will not be cumulative. If we move to online the exams might be replaced by an alternate assignment.
