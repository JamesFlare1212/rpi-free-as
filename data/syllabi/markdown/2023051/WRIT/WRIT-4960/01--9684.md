## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

Writing About Diversity aims to introduce students to major conversations centering around race and diversity in the United States, focusing in particular on Critical Race Theory as well as intersectionality between race and other forms of identity, such as class, gender, and sexuality. It is also a process-driven writing class that is centered around student writing projects and research, and that asks students to create original works of knowledge building off of existing scholarly research. Drafts are submitted in multiple stages that allow students to receive feedback from both peers and the instructor multiple times throughout the semester. The first half of the semester will be focused on texts from the memoir/personal narrative genre dealing primarily with race but also touching on issues of class, gender, and sexuality. During this unit, each student will give a presentation illuminating one aspect of the background or context of one of the readings. This will be a good starting point for the Analysis Paper, which asks students to choose a personal narrative dealing with issues of diversity and identity, and analyze it in relation to its context. The second half of the semester will explore major contributions to discussions about race in the United States. During this unit, students will be responsible for conducting a research project where they will write a paper that contributes to an ongoing conversation about diversity and identity in the United States. As part of this process, students will give a second Presentation which will inform us about your research and how it fits into an existing conversation.

## Course Goals

### Goals:

In this course, students will:

- Gain an understanding of major conversations involving race, class, gender, sexuality and other forms of identity in the United States.
- Learn to read critically and respond to scholarly texts and to use them as a starting point for one’s own research.
- Contribute original knowledge to ongoing conversations about race, class, gender, sexuality and other forms of identity in the United States based on scholarly research.
- Improve their understanding of the conventions of academic writing and research, including how to set up an academic research project and follow it through to completion.
- Learn how to assess the reputability and reliability of various information sources, both in print and online.

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes Assessment Measures:

**DESCRIPTIONS OF MAJOR ASSIGNMENTS**

**Analysis Paper:** Based on our discussions during the first half of class, find an example of a memoir or personal narrative that was published as a contribution to a conversation about diversity and identity in the United States other than the ones discussed in class. This can either be a recent example, or a historical one. Find 3-4 scholarly, peer-reviewed sources that help you understand the context that the memoir/personal narrative you chose is responding to as well as the issues involved. The first set of student presentations, which dive into the background of some of the texts that we are reading for class are a good model here. Then, write an analysis paper explaining how the memoir/personal narrative you chose responds to a specific context in order to make an argument, for one or more audiences, at a specific moment in time, and making an assessment of whether or not the argument is effective. **Analysis papers should draw on 3-4 scholarly, peer-reviewed sources, and should be at least 6-8 pages, double spaced, with 1” margins, in 12 point Times New Roman or a similar sized font. Rough drafts of your Analysis Paper will be due on Mon 6/26 at the beginning of class and should be at least 5 pages. Final drafts will be due on Mon 7/10 at the beginning of class.**

**Research Paper:** During the second half of the semester, students will be responsible for producing an original work of research about a topic related to diversity and identity in the United States. The topics discussed in the textbook by Delgado and Stefancic as well as the chapters from books on specific topics relating to race and its intersections with other marginalized identities are a good place to start here in defining your topic. Just like the chapters that we will be reading all deal with specific topics, a good research paper will be focused on a narrowly focused topics and will aim to make an original contribution to an ongoing conversation that goes beyond what has already been said in existing research. **Research papers should draw on at least 8-10 reputable, scholarly sources, and should be at least 12-15 pages, double spaced, with 1” margins, in 12 point Times New Roman or a similar sized font. Topic proposals will be due on Mon 7/24. Rough drafts will be due on Mon 8/7 and should be at least 8-10 pages. Final drafts will be due on Mon 8/14.**

**Presentation 1:** For the first set of Presentations, each student will choose a day to present during the first half of the class. The student should read ahead for the readings that will be due when they have signed up to present, and choose one element of the background or context that the author is responding to, or a relevant issue. Students should schedule a time to meet with me during my office hours or at another time in order to get my approval on your topic no less than one week before you are scheduled to present. For this presentation, you should find at least 2 reputable, scholarly sources that help us understand a specific aspect of the day’s readings. A good format for the presentations is to start by telling us about something in the readings that caught your attention and relates to your topic, then tell us about the information in the sources you found, and tell us how this relates to and helps us better understand the days readings after you discuss each source. You should also come prepared with 3-5 discussion questions to present to the class. Good discussion questions are open-ended, allow opportunities to make connections with other readings or with knowledge from outside the course, and either don’t have a clear or obvious answer, or allow for multiple responses. Plan to have a PowerPoint or other visual presentation, and for presentations to take roughly 12-15 minutes, not including discussion questions, which will be incorporated into the class discussion.

**Presentation 2:**

The second set of Presentations should inform us about your topics for the Research Paper. These presentations should give us a brief overview of the conversation that you are responding to that touches on the major sources and what they say, and give us an overview of the thesis and major points that you will be arguing in your final paper. Plan for these presentations to take about 15 minutes, with another 12-15 minutes for questions and comments.

**Weekly Response Journals:**

Each Thursday, students should submit a Response Journal that responds to one aspect of the readings for that week. Good response journals do not attempt to summarize every point of the readings, but focus on one or two specific things that you found interesting, puzzling, challenging, engaging, or that you would like to explore further. Feel free to bring in outside information and sources, as these Response Journals can naturally lead into the type of research that you will be doing for the two major papers this semester. It is also helpful to make note of any questions you have, as well as points you would like to discuss in class, as these can be a good jumping off point for class discussions. Response Journals should be around 1.5-2 pages, double spaced.

**Homework and Participation:**

This is a process-driven, writing intensive class, and so it is expected that students will attend class regularly and make insightful and relevant contributions to class discussions, and during the typical class session, participation grades will be assigned based on participation in that day's discussion and/or activities. Each class session, in preparation for the day's discussion, I will be asking you to submit a series of discussion points based on that day's readings. Discussion points can either consist of questions you wanted to talk about with regard to the readings, or specific passages or points in the text that you wanted to talk about. Aim for at least 5 discussion points per class, and they will be due at the beginning of each class session through LMS. Homework assignments, rough drafts, revision activities, and in-class group work will also count toward the homework and participation part of your grade. In general, so long as the student meets all the requirements of the assignment, homework assignments, in-class activities, and process work such as rough drafts are graded on a pass/fail basis.

## Policies

### Attendance Policy:

This is a small group seminar-style class, so students are expected to attend class regularly and to actively participate in all class sessions. As such, students will get three unexcused absences for the semester with no questions asked. Student athletes should inform me at the beginning of the term regarding any classes they will have to miss during the semester. Other reasons for an excused absence can be submitted through the Office for Student Success. Unexcused absences beyond the third will result in the student’s final grade being lowered by a third of a letter grade (so a B would become a B-, and an A- would become a B+, and so on).

### Other Course Policies:

***LATE WORK POLICY***

Students are expected to submit work on time and to communicate with me in advance if you need an extension for any assignments. No extensions will be granted once the due date for the assignment has passed. Homework assignments and rough drafts will be due at the beginning of class on the day for which they assigned. After that, no late homework assignments will be accepted. Final drafts of major writing assignments will be due at the beginning of class on the day for which they are assigned. Starting the following day, they will be marked down a third of a letter grade for each day they are late (so a B would become a B-, an A- becomes a B+, and so on). If you need an extension for an assignment, contact me before the due date. If you need an extension on a more regular basis, accommodations can be worked out with the Office of Disabled Student Services or with the instructor.

***INCLUSION POLICY***

Our classroom and my office hours are spaces where all are welcome. Please note that discrimination or harassment in any form will not be tolerated in this learning community. Beyond simply avoiding discriminating against others, your presence in this class indicates that you agree to actively promote a welcoming and inclusive learning environment for all, including but not limited to taking the following actions: using inclusive language, such as referring to people by their preferred personal pronouns; understanding that it is your responsibility in class discussions to shape your comments for a diverse audience that may include native and non-native English speakers, people of various genders and sexual orientations, people from various cultural and religious backgrounds, and other identifiers that may differ from your own; listening and not defending when someone from another background takes the time to explain their position; understanding that everyone comes to the classroom with differing levels of access to the dominant culture.

***ACADEMIC INTEGRITY***

All work submitted for this class should be the student’s original work, and should have been written for this class. When students include the ideas of others in their writing, they should make it clear in the text and through in-text citations which ideas and quotes are from outside sources. In cases where group work is assigned, the names of all members in the group must be listed, and the submission must contain only work by those students. Submission of any assignment in violation of this policy will result in a zero on the assignment. A second violation will result in automatic failure of the course and potential disciplinary action by the institution.

### Academic Policies:

- ### Academic Integrity

  Student-teacher relationships are built on trust. For example, students must trust that teachers have made appropriate decisions about the structure and content of the courses they teach, and teachers must trust that the assignments that students turn in are their own. Acts that violate this trust undermine the educational process. The Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities and The Graduate Student Supplement define various forms of Academic Dishonesty and you should make yourself familiar with these. In this class, all assignments that are turned in for a grade must represent the student’s own work. In cases where help was received, or teamwork was allowed, a notation on the assignment should indicate your collaboration.

  Violations of academic integrity may also be reported to the appropriate Dean (Dean of Students for undergraduate students or the Dean of Graduate Education for graduate students, respectively).

  If you have any question concerning this policy before submitting an assignment, please ask for clarification.
