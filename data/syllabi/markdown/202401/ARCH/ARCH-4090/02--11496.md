## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

**INTRODUCTION**

Buildings are testament to the will and forces that affect their conception, realization, use and experience. They bear cultural and professional significance and possess within them, their constituent components and context, important lessons for a student wanting to discover what a work of architecture is in its larger context; what brought it about, and how it contributes to the discipline and professional knowledge and practice. Buildings are both manifestos of intention and the manifestation of habitations and range in their influence from reinforcing obsolete meanings and patterns at one extreme, to innovating and provoking yet unconsidered ones, at the other.

The purpose of this seminar is to introduce students on the investigation of buildings in their larger physical, social, cultural and professional contexts “in order to reveal the cultural [and architectural] knowledge that is embedded within them.”[1] It is intended to contribute to students’ ability to critically observe and analyze, to raise awareness regarding the state of the profession at its most progressive frontier and their relation to it, and to improve architectural knowledge and scholarship with respect to innovation and progress in contemporary practice.

Teams of three students each will be assigned one building for a 8-weeks long case method investigation. Using primary and secondary source materials each team will analyze its building formally and technically including the production means, methods and materials employed to design and realize it, and seek to understand and describe it in its professional, socio-cultural, historical, and natural contexts. Where possible, post-occupancy evaluation examining performance relative to project objectives is also expected. Teams will be required to document their building with diagrams, drawings, and digital models for use in a booklet format case study.

By examining and discussing not one building, but many from a select group of progressive contemporary projects, this seminar will draw from the in-depth investigation to broaden our collective understanding of the state of the discipline, including its manifold motivations, preoccupations, and enabling technologies that affect best practices. We will employ case study methods to systematically examine, critically analyze, deepen, and disseminate architectural knowledge with respect to how buildings come to be, what their significance is, and whether [and how] they contribute to progressive and meaningful change or are prey to stylistic concern(s).

*Case Study* is a methodology of inquiry that investigates a phenomenon within its context and on which multiple sources of evidence are used.[2]As a methodology is neither new nor consistent across disciplines, and while it has been extensively employed in Law, Business, and to a lesser extent in Science, Social Sciences, and Engineering education, Architecture has only recently adopted case study methods in a substantial way. While many in architectural education have employed rudimentary case studies for years – to reveal synoptic comparative data about buildings, or to “show them” to others, the more recent *AIA Case Study Initiative* has resulted in a substantial initiative geared toward professional practice and the delivery mechanisms in the process of design through construction and post occupancy evaluation, where available.[3]

We, however, will not be following the AIA Case Study Initiative format. Our case study seminar is focused on a different set of issues, namely, to examine and analyze buildings in their larger cultural and professional context and how they have managed to substantially innovate and/or lead in changing the way we design, practice, build and inhabit buildings.

**Analysis and Investigation:** Analysis, research, and investigation – all under the broader term ‘study’, are aimed at understanding the architectural artifact, how it was conceived, derived, realized; how it has been inhabited, responded to and what cultural and professional (disciplinary significance) it has. Methods will be both qualitative, based on observation, and quantitative based on measurable aspects of buildings that may be used for comparative analysis.

Systematic analysis and investigation will include an examination of the project’s environmental, cultural, and professional context, critical questioning, deconstruction (‘disassembly’) and analysis of the artifact and its constituent components. Subjects of investigation include:

1. the complex physical, social and disciplinary context within which they were imagined, realized, and now exist,
2. the building program and performance criteria relative to bioclimatic and other environmental principles, and the project brief,
3. the technologies they employ and integrate, and
4. the respective architectural practices and their relation to key consultants, clients, and users.

The intent of the seminar is to build an acute awareness of the vast and critical knowledge embedded within architectural [cultural] artifacts, and to understand architectural precedent[s] not as things to be quickly understood, or to superficially aspire to, but from which lessons and trajectories can be cast, against and beyond ones own work.

**COURSE FORMAT**

The course format consists of a combination of seminar presentations, discussion, collaborative case-study investigation, and student team / individual presentations. Each student team will analyze, document and present what has discovered for discussion and comment at various stages throughout the semester. Each student is responsible to participate in discussion concerning and understand the case studies of the other students.

Roughly one-half day of each weekly meeting will be dedicated to student’s presentations, the other half will be dedicated to short seminars. The last meetings of the semester will be dedicated to individual and team meetings with the instructor.

The deliverables will include submissions associated with the assigned topics, as well as a review of the development of drawings, diagrams, and model(s) of student’s study project. All documents must be original and developed to show what students believe is critical. There will be a coordination of the case study booklet format and layout for use in developing the final submissions. At the end of the course, the material will be presented on a summary poster and presented on a poster session, where each student individually will do a oral presentation of his/her research to a rotating jury of invited faculty and advanced students.

The course final deliverables include a component to be developed by each student individually, an illustrated essay (using the graphic material developed as a team) where students will be able to articulate his/her personal critical take on the assigned project. Each student will be assigned a topic or *lens* to frame their writing.

**Methods:** Examination of bibliography and secondary references (images and commentary text) will be employed to understand the project’ intentions, framework and various contexts. When practical, this should be complemented with firsthand observation.

Deconstruction - the disassembly and breakdown of the building into its various components systems and parts through diagramming, modeling, and drawing will be employed to discover and represent (make visible) the most significant aspects and innovations of the project or its components.

Documentation - the re-presentation of the project in a booklet formatted case study and poster session presentation will be employed to place the work in a larger context of best practices and to disseminate it. Documentation will be formatted according to the Rensselaer Case Study Project and will include those diagrams, drawings and model(s) – (physical and digital) appropriate to the principle (most significant) story(ies) of the project.

Each team is expected to develop and employ clear and appropriate representational techniques for case study presentation in a poster session and for dissemination through a booklet-formatted case study document. The graphic material developed by the team for the booklet, accompanied by a minimum of one additional individually developed drawing will serve to illustrate a critical essay to be developed by each student individually.

**Teamwork and Collaboration**: Teamwork requires commitment, communication, and trust between several collaborators who each contribute equally to the task, complement one another and are willing to test the thoughts and work of the other, and to be tested. Focus on this task and the development of productive team approaches are important. Though one team member, having done the bulk of the research on a particular topic may present more substantially on that topic than the other, it is expected that each team member participate in and know the work of the others before it is presented. To accomplish this, regular joint team meetings are necessary and should be scheduled outside of class hours. Although the project is submitted as a team Individual contribution to team work should be identified. ## Course Goals

### Goals:

1. To gain substantive knowledge of a significant contemporary building, its innovations, styles, preoccupations, and trends as they relate to contemporary architectural practice, particularly those claiming innovation with respect to the program, environment and technology.
2. To raise awareness of contemporary influences on architecture and to develop the capacity to form a critical view on innovation and change. To understand one’s own and others’ cultural and professional context, both at its center and at its progressive margins.
3. To develop an awareness of design as a mode of inquiry and/or research and enable students to make the distinction between innovation, invention, and creative design through an investigation of the work of best practices.
4. To develop introductory architectural knowledge and scholarship with respect to innovation and progress in contemporary practice.

## Learning Outcomes

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes (CLOs):

- Outcome 1 ARCHITECTURAL CASE STUDY - RESEARCH AND DOCUMENTATION: The student will have the ability to work with others to research a case study. Student will have the ability to conduct library research, assess, record and evaluate information. Students will be able to assemble researched information in a clear, compelling and succinct document documenting the precedent in two dimensional orthogonal projections (Plans, sections, site plans) and three dimensional drawings, as well as in writing. Note: When working in teams student work will be identified, each student will be required to develop at least one building section/plan demonstrating control of scale, line weight and graphic legibility.
- Outcome 2 ARCHITECTURAL CASE STUDY - ECOLOGICAL KNOWLEDGE AND RESPONSIBILITY: Students will be introduced to the dynamic between built and natural environments. They will be able to developed quantitative analysis in Climate Studio software to assess illumination, solar and wind forces affecting their case study throughout the year. Note: When working in teams student work will be individually identified, each student will be required to develop a portion of environmental analysis.
- Outcome 3 ARCHITECTURAL CASE STUDY - DESIGN ANALYSIS: Students will have the ability to develop an analysis of synthesizing different aspects of an architectural artifact such as program, material systems and circulation. Students will demonstrate this ability through diagrams and in writing, investigating their larger physical, social, and professional contexts. Note: When working in teams student work will be individually identified, each student will be required to develop a portion of spatial analysis.
- Outcome 4 ARCHITECTURAL CASE STUDY - HISTORY AND THEORY: Students will have the ability to identify and analyze the case study Student will analyze the case study in relationship to an assigned research topic and related scholarship. In their analysis they will be able to address to the affect the case study's physical, spatial, and material form has on the experience of its users, its program and the discipline of architecture.
- Outcome 5 INFORMATION LITERACY : Students will be able to use Chicago Style Citations written correctly and understand their use as acknowledgment in scholarly writing.

### Criteria Details:

Grades would be established according the following grading criteria:

- A (EXCELLENT): Demonstrates a high level of curiosity, independent motivation, and a thorough, nuanced understanding of the topic at hand. Concepts are applied accurately; data are interpreted correctly; drawings are abundant, clear, and effective; content shows depth of thought and resourcefulness; written language is thoughtful, reflecting careful attention to the material, as well as clear, concise, and free of grammatical and spelling errors; oral presentations are coherent, logically-sequenced, and well-practiced; all assertions are supported with cited evidence; all work is clearly organized and visually compelling.
- B (GOOD): Demonstrates moderate curiosity, fairly consistent motivation, and a good understanding of the topic at hand. Concepts are applied fairly well; data are interpreted correctly; drawings are generally good but at times less clear or effective than desirable; content is substantial; written language is generally clear but less concise than ideal and contains minor grammatical or spelling errors; oral presentations are competent; all work is fairly well-organized and visually appealing.
- C (AVERAGE): Demonstrates limited curiosity, limited motivation, and an adequate understanding of the topic at hand. Concepts are sometimes mis-applied; data are interpreted with minor errors; drawings are limited in number, extent, and clarity; written language contains adequate content but is difficult to follow in places and/or contains significant grammatical and spelling errors; oral presentations are mildly disorganized and somewhat challenging to follow in places; work is in general mildly disorganized and visually inadequate.
- D (INFERIOR): Demonstrates minimal curiosity, motivation, and understanding of the topic at hand. Concepts are mis-applied; data are interpreted with significant errors; drawings are careless, unclear, or ineffective; written language contains little content, is thoroughly difficult to follow, and/or contains numerous grammatical and spelling errors; oral presentations are limited in scope, unpracticed, and extremely difficult to follow; most work is presented carelessly.
- F (FAIL): Fails to meet minimal expectations
