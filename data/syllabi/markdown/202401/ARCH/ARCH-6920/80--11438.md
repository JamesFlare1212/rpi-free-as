## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

The PhD Qualifying Examination is the prerequisite for writing a dissertation. It is designed to examine the knowledge acquired by the student in their proposed field of inquiry. In this context, knowledge of the field not only entails a mastery of the facts related to the field, it also requires the ability to formulate and elaborate on both theoretical and practical problems related to the chosen field of inquiry. Both aspects will be tested with the oral and written formats of the qualifying examination. In anticipation of the qualification exam, the Doctoral Seminar 2 prepares students for a level of knowledge and expertise such as would be required to construct and teach a course on the subject, and to be able to autonomously conduct leading scholarship in the field.

## Course Goals

### Goals:

The Doctoral Seminar 2, as an extension of the Doctoral Seminar 1 is a preparatory seminar for second year PhD students of the Built Ecologies Program. The goal of this seminar is to prepare students for their Qualification Exams that will take place at the end of the semester.

## Course Content

### Content Details:

From the previous “Doctoral Seminar 1”, each student has already chosen 3 broad areas of expertise that constitute his/her areas of examination in their qualification exam. At least one was chosen from within the discipline of “origin” and at least was chosen from a discipline outside of the discipline of “origin”. In the doctoral seminar 2 course, the same 3 areas will be further elaborated and crystalized as areas of expertise for each student. The semester is divided into 3 sections corresponding to the 3 areas of inquiry identified by the students in conjunction with their advisors. For each area, the students will propose a revised and extended version of the previously defined bibliography, will update the comprehensive diagram that illustrates key ideas and figures as they have evolved in time. At the end of the semester students will answer 3 questions proposed by their prospective dissertation committee members.

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes Assessment Measures:

All Course Objectives and Outcomes will be assessed as 3 hand-in projects + 3 oral presentations that will be evaluated by the course instructor at the end of each semester section.  
1\. Assessment of learning outcome "The ability to find the boundary between established knowledge and outstanding scientific questions" : this outcome will be evaluated using the document provided by each student, especially referencing the quality of the question that was formulated within it.  
2\. Assessment of learning outcome "The ability to summarize established knowledge, explain the context of new questions, and express the importance of such questions" : this outcome will be evaluated using the document provided by each student, especially referencing the quality of the written answers to the questions.  
3\. Assessment of learning outcome "The ability to respond to questions in a concise rigorous way - in an oral format using systems diagrams and other forms of graphic representation": This outcome will be evaluated using the documents provided by each student, specifically focusing on the graphic representations - diagrams provided within them. Those would also be evaluated during the oral presentations of the same questions.

### Additional Academic Integrity Course Policy and Penalty Information:

The Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities defines various forms of Academic Dishonesty and procedures for responding to them. Academic dishonesty violates the trust between students and teachers. Students must trust that teachers have made appropriate decisions about the structure and content of the courses they teach.

Teachers must trust that the assignments students turn in are their own performance. Violations of this trust undermines the educational process. Cheating, plagiarism (defined as the lack of attribution or citation of the work of others), willful damage or theft of another person’s work violate academic integrity. The first instance of the breach of this trust will merit a failing grade in this course and the violator(s) will be reported to the Dean of Students for disciplinary action.

The definitions and examples presented below are samples of types of academic dishonesty and are not to be construed as an exhaustive or exclusive list. The academic dishonesty policy applies to all students, undergraduate and graduate, and to scholarly pursuits and research. Additionally, attempts to commit academic dishonesty or to assist in the commission or attempt of such an act are also violations of this policy.

- Academic Fraud: The alteration of documentation relating to the grading process. For example, changing exam solutions to negotiate for a higher grade or tampering with an instructor’s grade book.
- Collaboration: Deliberate facilitation of academic dishonesty in any form. For example, allowing another student to observe an exam paper or allowing another student to “recycle” one’s old term paper or using one another’s work in a paper or lab report without citing it as another’s work.
- Copying: Obtaining information pertaining to a graded exercise by deliberately observing the paper of another student. For example, noting which alternative a neighboring student has circled on a multiple- choice exam.
- Cribbing: Use or attempted use of prohibited materials, information, or study aids in an academic exercise. For example, using an unauthorized formal sheet during an exam.
- Fabrication: Unauthorized falsification or invention of any information in an academic exercise. For example, use of “bought” or “ready-made” term papers, or falsifying lab records or reports.
- Plagiarism: Representing the work or words of another as one’s own through the omission of acknowledgment or reference. For example, using sentences verbatim from a published source in a term paper without appropriate referencing, or presenting as one’s own the detailed argument of a published source, or presenting as one’s own electronically or digitally enhanced graphic representations from any form of media.
- Sabotage: Destruction of another student’s work. For example, destroying a model, lab experiment, computer program, or term paper developed by another student.
- Substitution: Utilizing a proxy, or acting as a proxy, in any academic exercise. For example, taking an exam for another student or having a homework assignment done by someone else.
