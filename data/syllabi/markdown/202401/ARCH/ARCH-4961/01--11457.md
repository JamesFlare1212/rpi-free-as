## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

*‘The set in Hitchcock is more than just a mere set, rather, it is a labyrinth in which everyone – characters, director and audience – loses and finds themselves, in the intensity of their emotions.’*

-Pascal Bonnitzer, from *Notorious,* published in Slavov Zizek’s *Everything You Always Wanted to Know About Lacan But Were Afraid to Ask Hitchcock,* p. 152

*‘There is violence that all individuals inflict on spaces by their very presence, by their intrusion into the controlled order of architecture. Entering a building may be a delicate act, but it violates the balance of a precisely controlled geometry (do architectural photographs ever include runners, fighters, lovers?).’*

-Bernard Tschumi, architect, theorist, *Violence of Architecture,* 1981

*‘How do we get from the guy being pushed off the Verrazano-Narrows Bridge to the same guy at a 5th Avenue café?’ ‘The crew goes there,’ said Hitchcock without cracking a smile. ‘Wait a minute. How do we get the audience there, is what I mean.’ ‘Mr. Gardner,’ said Hitchcock. ‘The audience will go wherever I take them and they’ll be very glad to go there, I assure you.’* -Alfred Hitchcock, director (1899-1980)

**Overview** A semester long course in viewing: how to be an active viewer, a rested viewer, a curious viewer. A viewer willing to be immersed, overwhelmed, alert and a reader. A course for architects and makers who are eager to connect to cinema as a driving force behind modern architecture, an engine of spatial ideas, time and place. This course utilizes frequent viewing of the cinema of Alfred Hitchcock in a big screen setting, with relevant readings and discussions, at least one field trip and projects in writing, story-boarding and film analysis.

**On Cinema** What does it mean to participate in cinema in 2024? We are now deeply entrenched in “the era of the small screen”. But for a medium, industry and art that began small (Edison Videolas, private viewing booths and small-scale projections) cinema quickly became a group effort, both in the making and the viewing. As vaudeville theaters converted to projected cinema, screens grew, formats increased and changed proportions and the experience of the medium became about being in a group, in a grand space, alone in a crowd. In other words, there was a social aspect of watching moving pictures, aided and abetted by architecture.

Carrying certain traditions over from theater, organists accompanied silent films, as did quartets and the occasional orchestra. Acoustics for cinemas advanced quickly when Al Jolson’s “the Jazz Singer” was introduced in 1928. These advances in acoustics accompanied newly proportioned formats like VISTAVISON (between 1.66 and 2.00:1) CINERAMA (from 2.59:1 and 2.65:1) and later IMAX. To see a film with a periphery-filling screen and an advanced sound system (*Dolby* was introduced in 196*5, THX* in 1983, *DTS* in 1990) makes for a sensory overload; IMAX puts you in the eyepiece of the DP.

Media has shrunk to smaller and smaller screens, a more personalized, portable experience. Cinema still means social encounter, not only with the content but with the possibility of post-screening dialogue. Of course, there are hundreds of excellent on-line forums for film, but the experience of the big screen means literally seeing MORE. This semester we will try to understand why architecture is so essential to the experience, and why so many directors (and especially Hitchcock) value the role of architecture as something essential to the human story, be it comedy, tragedy or drama.

**Acknowledgement/Honoring** This semester sees a change in title for the course, the first time in 15 years. In honor of my former HASS colleague, Professor Pauline Oliveros, the course now has the prefix “Deep Viewing”. Oliveros (1932-2016) was a pioneering American composer accordionist and electronic musician who founded the Center for Deep Listening (CDL) in Kingston, NY and taught her Deep Listening course here at the Institute. The principle behind the course was that anyone could become a more sensitive listener—not only of music but of the world surrounding oneself.

“The first concern of all music in one way or another is to shatter the indifference of hearing, the callousness of sensibility, to create that moment of solution we call poetry, our rigidity dissolved when we occur reborn—in a sense hearing for the first time.” —Lucia Dlugoszewski, http://www.newmusicbox.org/news/may00/obit_ldlugoszewski.html, http://www.artofthestates.org/cgi-bin/piece.pl?pid=15, and http://musicmavericks.publicradio.org/features/interview_dlugoszewski.html

Similarly, this course—while using film as a kind of perceptual door—shares many of the same aims, to lessen the “indifference of viewing, or the indifference of looking.” We will spend a brief period at the start of each class using silent breathing techniques taken from various meditation practices and adapted for this class. We will focus on our breathing in the light, breathing in the dark and will acclimate our vision to the circumstance of cinematic viewing. One of the goals is to become a more aware viewer, moving away from the days’ previous activities into a state of calmness, both relaxed and alert. Phones will be silenced and may be collected at the beginning of each class, if necessary.

**Course Outline** It may seem ironic to invoke processes of meditation, in order to study the cinema of one who has been branded (including by himself) as the Master of Suspense. It seems a complete contradiction. And yet when asked about his idea of perfect happiness, Hitchcock replied “Open skies. Not a problem on the horizon. I’m free to work on my creative process.” In the spirit of both Hitchcock’s and Oliveros to create extraordinary experiences for their audiences, we too will learn from their traditions, policies and innovations. Building audience is a matter of patience, practice and sacrifice. As Hitchcock once said, “Style is self-plagiarism.”

**Why Architecture? Why Hitchcock?** In this course I argue for Hitchcock’s cinema as a kind of architectural practice. Each Monday we will watch a film, followed by discussion. There will be introductory lectures and readings, usually every other week. Some readings will include annotation; others will just be discussed. There is a midterm paper; a final project; a story boarding assignment’ and a “close viewing” assignment, where each student works with a film of their choice that is NOT part of the Monday viewing regimen. Hitchcock’s major filmography is on the last page and should be used as both a checklist and an “inquiry” list.

This seminar will explore the art of Alfred Hitchcock via the urban condition, modes of architecture and technical advances that make for this most “architectural” of cinematic practices. The narrative structures of Hitchcock’s films often move the characters from pastoral settings to urban contexts, and vice versa. These allegories track naive or innocent characters as they move into self-awareness, a transition always reflected in the costumes, music, lighting, editing and direction. Famously averse to shooting on location, Hitchcock invented and refined techniques for controlling shifts in scale, perspective and space - all part of his reliance on the studio for a kind of ‘world building’. For example, as a way to save on location costs, Hitchcock developed backlit film transparencies at the scale of architecture.

His techniques of sonic and visual abstraction, defamiliarization, continuous takes, color saturation and disorienting perspectives all have analogs in the operations of the modern city. His themes of voyeurism, doubling, mistaken identity and paranoia are hallmarks of the modern human condition. He made the first film to address psychoanalysis as a subject (*Spellbound,* 1945), shot four “single set” films (*Lifeboat,* 1944; *Rope,* 1945; *Dial M for Murder,* 1950; and *Rear Window,* 1956), and his dark comedy *Frenzy*(1972) looked at the urban phenomenon of serial murder. From the *39 Steps* to *I Confess* to *Psycho,* Hitchcock torqued the city grid as a symbol for both freedom (anonymity) and oppression (chaos).

The Master of Suspense has also been seen as a misogynist, sadist, humorist and cultural critic. We will critically engage his works via screenings, writing and our own attempts at storyboarding and set design.

In *The Poetics of Space (*1957) Gaston Bachelard asserts that “if one would ask us what is the most precious benefaction of the house, we would say: the house accommodates dreaming, the house protects the dreamer, the house allows us to dream in peace”. The claim can easily be made that the primary role of housing is safety – including protection from the elements, but also as an affront, literally, to society. It is an emblem of the culture but can also be seen as being apart from it. This is Hitchcock’s main premise: that the most sinister thing is not a far off, foreign threat, but is actually next door.

## Course Goals

### Goals:

**Student Learning Outcomes** Students who take this course will be able to:

**Outcome 1 Research** Apply fundamental critical thinking to an advanced topic of study

Demonstrate a working knowledge of Hitchcock’s timeline and filmography that can be utilized to analyze his cinema—and other works of cinema

## Policies

### Attendance Policy:

**Participation**

Students will arrive to class on time and be engaged with the presented films, discussions and lectures. Phones will be OFF during class. Because we devote so much time to viewing, students will learn to form opinions on the fly, often following a screening. Note-taking is encouraged.

**Attendance**

As we have placed an emphasis on viewing films at a large scale and in a group setting, attendance is essential to the process. Attendance will be taken at each class. On one or two occasions (such as with the “close viewing” assignment), films will be screened in a location at a time and choosing of the student. The course has a least one field trip, and all students are required to view at least one other work of cinema outside of class (this could include a museum installation, film festival or the students’  
participation in making a short film or commercial).

### Additional Academic Integrity Course Policy and Penalty Information:

As a member of the SoA Artificial Intelligence Subcommittee, we are currently in the process of examining the reasonable use of A.I. in conducting research and the subsequent use of A.I. in creating scholarly papers and design strategies. The policy of this instructor is to pursue penalties for outright plagiarism, that is, "old-fashioned" plagiarism, and to allow for the acknowledged use of A.I.in generating new insights into cinema, particularly that of Alfred Hitchcock. Each aper that utilized A.I. will be subject to full citations of the prompts used and a critical analysis of content. What is accurate? What is false? What has been invented?  
A grade of 0 (zero) will be given (for that assignment) for the first violation of the plagiarism policy. The student will receive a grade of F for subsequent violations. However, the use of A.I. as a research/writing tool is allowed, if acknowledge within the paper, and does not constitute the bulk of the intellectual content.  
Group work is encouraged in at least one assignment and the students will qualitatively and qualitatively assess each others' participation.
