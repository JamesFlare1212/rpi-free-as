### ARCH 4770/4780 - Architectural Design Studio 5/6

---

This is an upper division architectural design studio that explores topics of contemporary interest to the discipline. Students apply for this studio based on their personal interest and professional objectives. Three such studios Architectural Design Studio 5, Architectural Design Studio 6, and Architectural Design Studio 7, are required for the degree. This course is offered in the fall and spring semesters and is required of all architecture undergraduates in the B.Arch. program.

## Course Goals

### Goals:

This Vertical Studio will explore the coupling of two fields of design practice: urban mining and regenerative building. Working at an architectural scale with an urban scope and terrestrial impact, the studio’s inquiry will combine speculative design thinking with pragmatic material-based construction practices that are oriented towards a creative exploration of architectural “leftovers” - buildings *as* waste and building *from* waste - and the new kind of material, ecological, and social worlds this engenders. The success of an architectural project is often measured by what is added to the world rather than what is repaired, removed, or left unchanged. Indeed, although it has been said that everything has already been “made” in our anthropocenic era, architecture still remains largely in the business of expanding footprints. Making buildings – especially new ones – is both an engine for, and measure of, such growth. As such, architecture is linked to the resource-intensive activities of the building construction industry and to the broader market-based systems of capitol, development, and urbanization that drive it. This perpetual growth machine is based upon a model of production and consumption in which a linear, one-way flow of resources is formed into buildings which, when no longer considered useful, beautiful, etc., are discarded. This predicament seems to be particularly prescient as our contemporary moment is shaped by an existential reckoning with a climactic tipping point and limited distributed resources. This advanced design studio will consider various ways that we might challenge this carbon-hungry growth paradigm in favor of a regenerative one that reimagines the value and significance of architectural leftovers.

## Learning Outcomes

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes (CLOs):

- OUTCOME 1: Research 1a. ability to use / develop specialized knowledge - Students will conduct and implement discipline-specific research on the field of adaptive reuse (principles and practices) and circular building construction methods (deconstruction and salvage technologies).
- OUTCOME 1: Research 1b. ability to use / develop broad and integrative knowledge - Students will conduct and implement interdisciplinary research on the urban transformation of the city of Albany, historically and today, identifying social and ecological factors, architectural and landscape typologies, and spatial and material conditions.
- OUTCOME 2: Intellectual Design Skills 2a. ability to design - Students will learn to differentiate between, and demonstrate the ability to design for, different types of architectural change (use, configuration, material, iconography, etc.), different durations/frequencies of change (temporary/permenent, continuous/incremential, etc.), and various forms of displacement (displaced peoples, uses, building components, etc.)
- OUTCOME 2: Intellectual Design Skills 2b. ability to design using information resources - Students will learn to identify and analyze existing site conditions through the use of base documentation and demonstrate the ability to use this technical information in the design of strategic interventions into an existing built fabric of a large building and complex urban site with precision, clarity, and impact.
- OUTCOME 2: Intellectual Design Skills 2c. ability to design engaging diverse perspectives - Students will learn to identify the diverse perspectives of project stakeholders and demonstrate the ability to integrate alternative worldviews and lived experiences into a design project: the conversion of an abandoned food storage warehouse into a hybrid building type consisting of a material "bank", job training facility, and workforce hostel.
- OUTCOME 2: Intellectual Design Skills 2d. ability to design with ethical reasoning - Students will learn to make informed value judgements regarding the appropriate allocation of limited resources (material, spatial, ecological, financial, etc.). Students will demonstrate the ability to articulate the ethical dilemmas inherent in the choices of where, when, and for whom these resources will be distributed and how they are integrated into a project design.
- OUTCOME 2: Intellectual Design Skills 2e. ability to design with quantitative fluency - Students will learn to evaluate a building's constructive anatomy and quantify it as a material stockpile for reuse. Students will demonstrate the ability to interpret and synthesize different types of information (written/numerical/visual), establish and calculate numerical areas/space allocations, analyze multiple "test-fit" scenarios, and determine the project's scope of work, including the types, amounts, and extents of the design changes needed to be made to yield maximum impact.
- OUTCOME 2: Intellectual Design Skills 2f. ability to design with communicative fluency - Students will learn to differentiate between different constituencies/audiences (architectural, client, and general public) and demonstrate the ability to articulate a design problem, problem-solving methods, and solution in these different contexts.
- OUTCOME 3 Collaboration 3a ability to collaborate with team - Students will demonstrate the ability to work effectively in 2-3 person teams using collaborative methods and interpersonal skills. Students will demonstrate the ability to develop a strategy for group work (knowledge/info sharing, ideation, workflow, project management, etc.) that recognizes different group member’s strengths, documents the strategy so that others may understand it, implements the strategy, communicates and evaluates the results, and modifies the strategy as needed.
- OUTCOME 4 Civic and Global Engagement 4a. ability to engage the civic realm - Students will demonstrate the ability to engage issues of public interest/concern, specifically the urban redevelopment of Albany and workforce development in the Capitol Region. Students will develop skills of thoughtful listening, engaged dialogue, and critical thinking to develop designs aimed at making positive change for the common good.
- OUTCOME 4 Civic and Global Engagement 4b. ability to engage the global realm - Students will demonstrate the ability to engage complex public issues associated with sustainable, regenerative design aimed at transforming cities into active parts of a global carbon sink.
- OUTCOME 5 Design Execution 5a. Midterm Review - Students will demonstrate the ability to implement their learning in Outcomes #1-4 in a clear, complete, and compelling way in a presentation that includes diagrams, mappings, orthographic drawings, axonometrics, and renderings.
- OUTCOME 5 Design Execution 5b. Final Review - Students will demonstrate the ability to implement their learning in Outcomes #1-4 in a clear, complete, and compelling way in a presentation that includes diagrams, mappings, orthographic drawings, axonometrics, and renderings.

### Academic Policies:

- ### Academic Integrity

  Student-teacher relationships are built on trust. For example, students must trust that teachers have made appropriate decisions about the structure and content of the courses they teach, and teachers must trust that the assignments that students turn in are their own. Acts that violate this trust undermine the educational process. The Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities and The Graduate Student Supplement define various forms of Academic Dishonesty and you should make yourself familiar with these. In this class, all assignments that are turned in for a grade must represent the student’s own work. In cases where help was received, or teamwork was allowed, a notation on the assignment should indicate your collaboration.

  Violations of academic integrity may also be reported to the appropriate Dean (Dean of Students for undergraduate students or the Dean of Graduate Education for graduate students, respectively).

  If you have any question concerning this policy before submitting an assignment, please ask for clarification. In addition, you can visit the following site for more information on our Academic Integrity Policy: [Students Rights, Responsibilities, and Judicial Affairs](https://info.rpi.edu/dean-students/student-rights-responsibilities-and-judicial-affairs).

### Additional Academic Integrity Course Policy and Penalty Information:

**STATEMENT OF ACADEMINC INTEGRITY**

The Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities defines various forms of Academic Dishonesty and procedures for responding to them. Academic dishonesty violates the trust between students and teachers. Students must trust that teachers have made appropriate decisions about the structure and content of the courses they teach. Teachers must trust that the assignments students turn in are their own performance. Violations of this trust undermines the educational process. Cheating, plagiarism (defined as the lack of attribution or citation of the work of others), willful damage or theft of another person’s work violate academic integrity. The first instance of the breach of this trust will merit a failing grade in this course and the violator(s) will be reported to the Dean of Students for disciplinary action.
