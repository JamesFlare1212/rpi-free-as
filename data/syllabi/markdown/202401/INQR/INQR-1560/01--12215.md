## Learning Outcomes

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes (CLOs):

- More clearly see the media environment in which you are embedded and use the tools and techniques of media studies to address it critically
- Articulate the major concepts, theories and practices of media studies, and interrogate, amplify or challenge those theories in an academically valid form
- Deconstruct media messages, particularly those intended to persuade and/or short-circuit critical thinking, to discover hidden motivations and intentions
- Cultivate and apply the tools, techniques and vocabulary of media and communication for personal growth and professional gain through ethical application
- Contextualize contemporary media-driven public debates, controversies and moral panics within an historical context
- Appreciate the deep interconnections between technology and communication and how one’s view of reality and one’s belief in the scope of possible futures can be constricted or liberated, advanced or exploited, by the geography of one’s media environment
- Research, create and publicly present academically valid analyses on topics related to contemporary media studies
- Decide if further studies in media and communication would be a personally rewarding and life-enhancing pursuit

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes Assessment Measures:

PRESENTATIONS + CLASS PARTICIPATION (weekly short uploaded ppt presentations, with occasional public presentations, plus in-class discussion and mandatory attendance)

RESEARCH PROJECT & QUIZ (short multi-part analysis and presentation project + 10-14 question end of term quiz)

MIDTERM EXAM (30-35 multiple choice questions)

TERM PROJECT (multi-part project culminating in a 5 to 7-minute end of term class presentation) 25%

### Academic Policies:

- ### Academic Integrity

  Student-teacher relationships are built on trust. For example, students must trust that teachers have made appropriate decisions about the structure and content of the courses they teach, and teachers must trust that the assignments that students turn in are their own. Acts that violate this trust undermine the educational process. The Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities and The Graduate Student Supplement define various forms of Academic Dishonesty and you should make yourself familiar with these. In this class, all assignments that are turned in for a grade must represent the student’s own work. In cases where help was received, or teamwork was allowed, a notation on the assignment should indicate your collaboration.

  Violations of academic integrity may also be reported to the appropriate Dean (Dean of Students for undergraduate students or the Dean of Graduate Education for graduate students, respectively).

  If you have any question concerning this policy before submitting an assignment, please ask for clarification. In addition, you can visit the following site for more information on our Academic Integrity Policy: [Students Rights, Responsibilities, and Judicial Affairs](https://info.rpi.edu/dean-students/student-rights-responsibilities-and-judicial-affairs).

### Additional Academic Integrity Course Policy and Penalty Information:

Unlike most other courses, Media and Society encourages the review, use and presentation of work by media scholars and amateurs, in fact encourages such use ... as long as such work is clearly cited. Failure to cite work or to pass the work of other off as original will result in a grade of zero for weekly assignments and, depending on the extent of un-cited appropriation, will negatively affect the grade on the required research project and final project. Collaboration is encouraged for all assignments and project, though not required. Midterm exam and final quiz must be taken in class.
