## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

This course invites you into the world of philosophical ideas and reasoning -- to join a great conversation that has unfolded over the past 2,500 years since philosophy began in Ancient Greece. We will explore such issues as the nature of Philosophy, whether reason can allow us to make better judgments about right and wrong and about better and worse ways of living, the relationship that exists between mind and body, and whether philosophy has anything to contribute to discussions about the existence and nature of God. This course will aim to help you develop your skills in reading philosophical texts and in discussing and evaluating philosophical concepts.

## Learning Outcomes

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes (CLOs):

- 1\. Students will be able to explain and communicate the essential characteristics of Philosophy and the uses and value of Philosophy.
- 2\. Students will be able to identify the main types of arguments and explain how arguments are used in Philosophy.
- 3\. Students will be able to articulate and explain major approaches within philosophical ethics, including Utilitarianism, Deontology (duty-based ethics), Virtue Ethics, and Ethics of Care.
- 4\. In the context of ethics, students will be able to explain and utilize the distinction between intrinsic value and instrumental value.
- 5\. Students will be able to articulate such concepts and categories within Philosophy of Mind as Substance Dualism, Property Dualism, and Physicalism (e.g., Type- and Token-Identity Theories, and Eliminativism).
- 6\. Students will be able to identify and discuss some of the most influential issues and arguments within Philosophy of Religion having to do with religious experience, arguments for the existence of God, and the prevalence of evil and suffering in the world.

## Course Calendar

### Calendar Details:

The course calendar has been provided to you in a separate document which is available on the LMS site for the course. However, a condensed version of the Calendar is as follows:  
Week of 01/08: Overview of course; Questions asked in Philosophy; Beginnings of Philosophy; Basics of arguments, necessary and sufficient conditions.  
Week of 01/15: Knowledge and how it might be acquired, difference between knowing and thinking.  
Week of 01/22: Value and benefits of Philosophy; Plato's Allegory of the Cave as classic image of the role of Philosophy.  
Week of 01/29: Intro to Ethics; Intrinsic vs. Instrumental Value; Principle of Impartiality; Bentham's version of Utilitarianism.  
Week of 02/05: J.S. Mill's version of Utilitarianism; Strengths and criticisms of Utilitarianism; Review for Exam 1.  
Week of 02/12: **EXAM 1**; Kant and duty-based ethics, hypothetical vs. categorical imperatives, autonomous vs. heteronymous will, 1st and 2nd formulations of C.I.  
Week of 02/19: Trolley Problem and ethics settings for autonomous vehicles; Virtue ethics and ethics of care.  
Week of 02/26: Applied ethical issue: What do we owe to those in desperate need; Begin Mind & Brain unit; Survey of positions on mind-body relationship.  
Week of 03/04: NO CLASSES (Spring Break).  
Week of 03/11: Catch-up; Review for Exam 2.  
Week of 03/18: **EXAM 2**; Descartes' Meditations as classic statement of interactive dualism.  
Week of 03/25: Neuroscience, Physicalism, and disorders of mind and perception; Nagel's "Knowledge Argument" and Jackson's "Qualia Argument" against Physicalism, and David Lewis' response.  
Week of 04/01: Overview of topics & positions in Philosophy of Religion: Epistemic and pragmatic arguments for God; Epistemological issues concerning religious experience.  
Week of 04/08: Problem of Evil and proposed responses; Buddhism as non-Western philosophical and religious tradition; Buddhism on removal of suffering.  
Week of 04/15: Buddhism, continued. Review for Exam 3.  
Week of 04/22: **EXAM 3**.

### Academic Policies:

- ### Academic Integrity

  Student-teacher relationships are built on trust. For example, students must trust that teachers have made appropriate decisions about the structure and content of the courses they teach, and teachers must trust that the assignments that students turn in are their own. Acts that violate this trust undermine the educational process. The Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities and The Graduate Student Supplement define various forms of Academic Dishonesty and you should make yourself familiar with these. In this class, all assignments that are turned in for a grade must represent the student’s own work. In cases where help was received, or teamwork was allowed, a notation on the assignment should indicate your collaboration.

  Violations of academic integrity may also be reported to the appropriate Dean (Dean of Students for undergraduate students or the Dean of Graduate Education for graduate students, respectively).

  If you have any question concerning this policy before submitting an assignment, please ask for clarification. In addition, you can visit the following site for more information on our Academic Integrity Policy: [Students Rights, Responsibilities, and Judicial Affairs](https://info.rpi.edu/dean-students/student-rights-responsibilities-and-judicial-affairs).
