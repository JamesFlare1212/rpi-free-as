## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

This course will explore what it means to do community-engaged research about science and technology, and how the field of Science and Technology Studies (STS) can help to improve community engagement in other areas of research and practice, such as medicine or engineering. In addition to learning about these research practices through readings and classroom discussions, students will gain hands-on experience in a community engagement project and will develop their own project proposals.

## Learning Outcomes

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes (CLOs):

- Identify a wide range of community-engagement activities that are used in a variety of research fields.
- Grapple with the ethical and political implications of community-engaged scholarship.
- Compare and evaluate examples of community-engaged research.
- Develop original ideas and plans for community engagement in their own scholarly and professional practices.

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes Assessment Measures:

Final grades will be based on evaluations of the following assignments. Details about each assignment will be provided separately.

Contribution to a faculty-led community-engagement project (approximately 20 hours total). The purpose of this assignment is to gain hands-on experience with the practicalities and intellectual challenges of community engagement. You will learn about an STS faculty member’s ongoing community-engagement practices and assist them with tasks that are necessary to success of their projects. Through this brief collaboration, you will put course texts into action, and, in turn, use your experience to reflect on and critique those texts in class discussions. The collaborating faculty member will be consulted when assigning a grade for this assignment. (25%)

Literature review essay focusing on one significant approach to community engagement in STS, science and technology policymaking, or a field commonly studied by STS scholars, such as ecology, public health, or information technology. The purpose of this assignment is to pursue, in greater depth than in possible through weekly readings, understanding of a particular approach or method of community engagement, such as science shops, participatory technology assessment, community-based participatory research, participatory design, consensus conferences, citizen juries, citizen science, participatory action research, etc. At least three books and seven articles should be discussed. Target length is 5000 words. Effective strategies for writing literature reviews will be discussed in class. (25%)

“Guest lecture” to the class in which you use examples from your literature review to illustrate an idea from the course readings. In consultation with the course instructor, you will choose an appropriate day to lead the class for 90 minutes, sharing the knowledge you gained in the course of writing your literature review. (15%)

Proposal for a research project involving community engagement. The final writing assignment is a proposal for a research project (relevant to each student's professional aspirations) that involves some form of community engagement. It does not necessarily need to be immediately implemented, as in a dissertation project. Consider this an opportunity to design a project that you aspire to carry out when the time is right. This will be written incrementally throughout the semester, with opportunities for feedback in class meetings. Each increment (total of 10 weekly writings) is worth a point toward the total 25 points that can be earned for this assignment. The final proposal is worth the remaining 15 points. (25%)

Contributions to classroom community (10%): All students are required to make regular, sustained, thoughtful contributions to class discussions and to contribute to building a classroom culture that is respectful, stimulating, and supportive.

- ### Academic Integrity

  Student-teacher relationships are built on trust. For example, students must trust that teachers have made appropriate decisions about the structure and content of the courses they teach, and teachers must trust that the assignments that students turn in are their own. Acts that violate this trust undermine the educational process. The Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities and The Graduate Student Supplement define various forms of Academic Dishonesty and you should make yourself familiar with these. ### Additional Academic Integrity Course Policy and Penalty Information:

In this class, all individual assignments that are turned in for a grade must represent the student’s own work. Any instances of plagiarism will result in a failing grade for the assignment. Repeated instances of academic dishonesty will be grounds for failing the course. Plagiarism includes purchasing term papers; copying or handing in the writing of another student (current or former); using sentences verbatim from a published source without appropriate referencing (when in doubt, cite the source); and presenting as one’s own the detailed argument of a published source. “Recycling” papers written in other courses is also forbidden.
