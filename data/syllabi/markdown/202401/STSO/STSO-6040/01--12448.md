## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

# This PhD-level course delves into the intricate intersections of technology, feminism, and queer theory, placing a strong emphasis on their relationships with issues of racial and social justice. Through a critical examination of foundational texts and contemporary scholarship, students will explore the ways in which technology shapes and is shaped by societal structures, power dynamics, and identities. The course is designed to foster a nuanced understanding of how technology can be both a tool for liberation and a conduit for oppression. Students will engage with a diverse range of literature, from classic works in feminist and queer theory to cutting-edge research in science and technology studies. Discussions will revolve around key themes such as agency, embodiment, surveillance, and access, enabling students to develop a comprehensive understanding of how technology intersects with broader social and political contexts. By the end of the course, students will not only have a solid foundation in feminist and queer approaches to technology but will also be equipped to critically analyze and contribute to discussions surrounding justice within the realm of science and technology studies. The course aims to empower students to question existing norms, challenge biases, and propose innovative solutions that foster inclusivity and equity in the ever-evolving landscape of technological advancements.

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes Assessment Measures:

# 1. Critical Analysis: Develop the ability to critically analyze and assess the impact of technology on various aspects of society, with a specific focus on feminist and queer perspectives, while considering intersections with racial and social justice.

# 2. Theoretical Understanding: Gain a comprehensive understanding of key theories within feminism, queer theory, and technology studies, and apply these theoretical frameworks to analyze technological advancements and their implications.

# 3. Intersectionality: Explore the intersections of gender, sexuality, race, and social class in the context of technology, recognizing the importance of intersectionality in shaping individuals' experiences and access to technological resources.

# 4. Ethical Considerations: Examine the ethical dimensions of technology through the lens of feminist and queer theories, addressing issues such as privacy, consent, and the unintended consequences of technological innovation.

# 5. Historical Perspectives: Trace the historical evolution of technology and its relationship with gender, sexuality, and marginalized communities, understanding how past developments continue to influence contemporary technological landscapes.

# 6. Research Skills: Enhance research skills by critically engaging with scholarly articles, books, and other academic sources that explore the multifaceted connections between technology and issues of justice.

# 7. Communication and Collaboration: Develop effective communication skills in both written and oral formats, and foster collaborative discussions that integrate diverse perspectives on technology, justice, and societal impacts.

# 8. Innovative Solutions: Encourage creative thinking and the proposal of innovative solutions to address challenges related to gender, sexuality, racial justice, and social justice within the realm of science and technology.

# 9. Awareness of Bias and Inclusivity: Cultivate an awareness of biases embedded in technological systems and design, and explore strategies to promote inclusivity and diversity in technological development and implementation.

# 10. Critical Reflection: Engage in reflective practices to assess personal biases and assumptions, fostering a continuous commitment to questioning and challenging normative practices within technology studies.

## Grading Criteria

### Criteria Details:

**Participation** (25%)

In a discussion-based seminar, student attendance and active participation is required and crucial to creating a successful learning community. This means coming to class ready to discuss the assigned material with observations, questions, and comments, as well as participating fully in any small and large group activities. Please be present not only physically but also mentally. I expect each of you to come to class having finished all the readings and assignments for the day. I acknowledge some of you may be uncomfortable in large group discussions, and so I recognize engaged listening and note taking as valid forms of participation. Still, consider preparing one specific point about the readings to address in class each day, or challenge yourself to speak up in class once a week in some way.

During class discussion, you should be intentionally aware of your participation, if you find you are speaking repeatedly, step back and allow others the chance to speak up; conversely, if you notice that you are not speaking during class discussions, find ways to contribute to the conversation e.g. draw upon your notes for questions or previously formed thoughts.

**Facilitation** (25%)

You will each sign up to for two weeks as facilitators. You must demonstrate thorough knowledge of the week’s readings and facilitate the discussion via a handout, questions, and/or a creative exercise. You are especially encouraged to incorporate relevant artistic work, video clips, art, blog posts, and current events. The facilitation should take between 10-20 minutes and you are free to use this time as you see fit. During the facilitation exercise, you should consider yourself

in the shoes of a course instructor and lead the discussion in a way that you would if you were a teacher. For this exercise, you should avoid simply summarizing the readings; instead, you should share what you what find interesting, challenging, or confusing. Don’t forget to

- allow & encourage everyone to speak
- ensure that others are respectful in listening and responding even if there is

disagreement

- generate important and debatable claims and conclusions about the

reading

- present back to the class the group’s best points with adequate

explanation/context

- pose questions or solicit comments on specific points/issues

**QCQs** (25%)

Each week, you will be responsible for posting a critical reading response of at least 300 words on Blackboard a total of 10 times during the semester. The responses should be posted no later than 7 am on Thursday. The QCQs are designed to prepare you better for class discussions. Think of these as your notes and reflections on the readings you will use for the discussion. These are informal writing opportunities to demonstrate your critical engagement with the class material and concepts. Your responses must be submitted as a “QCQ” or Quote / Comment / Question in which you: (1) identify a significant passage from the text; (2) write a paragraph explaining its significance and how it relates to the author’s main claims; (3) pose a question for discussion that arose for you from the text. *When we have multiple readings, your main quote can be from a single text, yet you should try to establish a discussion across different readings in your comment and question.*

**Final Paper** (25%)

You have complete freedom with the format and content of your final paper, as long as it incorporates the topics and readings we will cover in this class. Although you might think feminist, queer, crip, and black onto-epistemologies of technology are not relevant to your work, I would like you to push yourself out of your comfort zone and consider how every academic investigation can immensely benefit from these perspectives and their take on social justice. Your final can be a portion of your portfolio, a paper you would like to submit for publication, a dissertation chapter, literature review etc. As you can note on the syllabus, the last two weeks will be dedicated to workshopping your papers. So, think of what you’d like to get extensive feedback for. Everyone will read others’ final papers and provide detailed feedback for them.

- ### Academic Integrity

  Student-teacher relationships are built on trust. For example, students must trust that teachers have made appropriate decisions about the structure and content of the courses they teach, and teachers must trust that the assignments that students turn in are their own. Acts that violate this trust undermine the educational process. The Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities and The Graduate Student Supplement define various forms of Academic Dishonesty and you should make yourself familiar with these. In this class, all assignments that are turned in for a grade must represent the student’s own work. In cases where help was received, or teamwork was allowed, a notation on the assignment should indicate your collaboration.

  Violations of academic integrity may also be reported to the appropriate Dean (Dean of Students for undergraduate students or the Dean of Graduate Education for graduate students, respectively).

  If you have any question concerning this policy before submitting an assignment, please ask for clarification. In addition, you can visit the following site for more information on our Academic Integrity Policy: [Students Rights, Responsibilities, and Judicial Affairs](https://info.rpi.edu/dean-students/student-rights-responsibilities-and-judicial-affairs).
