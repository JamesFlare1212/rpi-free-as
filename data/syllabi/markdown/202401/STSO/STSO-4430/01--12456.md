## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

This course teaches basic historical, anthropological, and sociological concepts for making sense of how psychoactive drugs have been used in historical and contemporary societies. We analyze how licit and illicit drugs serve as “technologies” in specific settings and subcultures; how these technologies are produced, distributed, and consumed; what meanings are attached to them and how these change over time; how drug policy governs them; what these technologies and their use tell us about society and culture in different times and places; and impacts of biomedical knowledge on drug users, including persons with addictions.

We focus on representation of drug use and drug users in popular culture, science, medicine, and social science, tracing how public and private values are translated into drug law and policy. We study how social and cultural change affects specific communities of drug users. You will learn how scientific knowledge generated about drug addiction affects policymaking, and how evidence-based policy works within this arena. Policy is defined as state management of populations defined in response to social problems.

As an upper-level interdisciplinary humanities and social sciences course, this course stimulates critical thinking by making ethical and political relations visible in public policy texts, court cases, the policymaking process, and social outcomes. Although we center U.S. drug policy because it has greatly influenced regulation of the global drug trade, we discuss the “new drug history” emerging all over the world. Meanings attributed to drug use vary historically and culturally; comparative examples abound and each student is asked to present a comparative case in addition to a US-based presentation as a way to build awareness of drugs into understandings of global citizenship. As a Communication Intensive course, students will receive and give feedback that helps them develop reading, writing, discussion, and presentation skills.

## Course Goals

### Goals:

**COURSE LEARNING OUTCOMES**

Students who successfully complete this course will demonstrate the following:

- \- Ability to think critically about and discuss—in oral and written form—contemporary social problems related to drugs and drug policy, recognizing the matrix of factors (economic, technical, biophysical, cultural, etc.) that frame problems and constrain solutions.
- \- Ability to respond to complex, content-related questions concerning historical events in the regulation and control of legal and illegal drugs
- \- Ability to analyze and present findings on particular historical, cultural, and social contexts
- \- Specific knowledge concerning events that led to the current regulatory regime governing global drug production, distribution, and consumption.

**Tuesday, January 10**

**Reading required for today’s class:**

1. Howard S. Becker, “Drugs: What Are They? and come to class prepared to discuss it. The short essay is on LMS and at: https://www.howardsbecker.com/articles/drugs.html

**Friday,** **January 13**

Drug policy debates evolve into media panics that mark boundary conflicts between social groups—some “normal,” others “deviant.” Lines are drawn between “legal” and “illegal” substances, behaviors, and activities. Howard S. Becker likens drugs to pharmacological weeds in which boundaries between ‘weeds’ and ‘not weeds’ are ‘socially constructed.’ We study drugs as “boundary objects” in Science and Technology Studies (STS), an interdisciplinary field combining history, sociology, and anthropology of science and technology. Sociologist Craig Reinarman considers what drug ‘scares’ (or ‘moral panics’) have to do with boundaries between social order and disorder, and the interest groups that police these boundaries **frame** drugs in society. **Frames** are a concept basic to this course: please attend to current examples of **how drugs and drug scares are framed.** We will do an in-class exercise using them. . . . an activity that will be as exciting and interesting as **your** examples.

**Reading required for today’s class:**

1. “The Social Construction of Drug Scares,” *Craig Reinarman,* LMS

**WRITING PROJECT 2**

**Situating Drugs**

**Due February 17, 2023**

As you did for the implosion paper, choose a *specific* drug as the artifact around which a *specific* subculture of “socially problematic” drug use has grown (the “context of use” that you are situating in this assignment). This assignment does not work well if you choose “cocaine,” but works better if you choose “upper and middle-class suburban cocaine users in southern California in the 1980s.” There are two parts: an annotated bibliography that shows the sources on which you relied, and how you used them. You should locate 5-7 newspaper, magazine, and peer-reviewed scholarly articles focused on that drug, that specific subculture, and the specific geographic area in which it has taken root. The goal is to produce your own analysis of the cultural materials you find. You may also use visual materials or artifactual materials (Patient Product Inserts, drug company giveaways or DTC advertisements) if relevant. Write a short (2-3 sentences) summary of each article or artifact you are using and then use these summaries as the basis for writing a paper that answers the following questions. Your summaries should be attached as the bibliography of the main paper, which should be double-spaced. Feel free to attach visual materials if relevant.

**Drug Subcultures and Contexts of Use**

**DUE March 31, 2023**

You may write your paper according to whatever organizational structure you feel is appropriate. To get you started, I suggest that you first describe how, where, and when the subculture formed. Then use the following questions as guidelines—only some of which will be relevant to your subculture; just answer those that are. This is **not** an outline of the paper, but a set of guiding questions that can help you get started.

- What population is affected by the drug problem you are examining?
- When and where was the drug use of this group first defined as “problem”?
- Has this drug problem been addressed through law or policy? How did it get onto the political agenda?
- How has this drug problem represented in the media?
- When and where was this drug introduced to the market? Is it currently legal or illegal; OTC or prescription only? Has it always been? Does it have a medical use? For what conditions? Does it have a nonmedical use? Is it “diverted” (illegal) or used “off-label” (legal)?

- Do the articles refer to governing bodies like the WHO, the U.S. Congress, CDC, NIDA, or FDA? How are these entities portrayed? Is the author biased for or against regulation?
- How are users of this drug portrayed? Are they considered to have a “disease,” or not? Are they represented as “criminals”? What implications are represented for those who use this drug?
- By what “routes of administration” is this substance ingested? Are there any rituals or paraphernalia specific to this drug?
- What claims do the articles make about the cultural or subcultural practices of users? How would you characterize these practices and/or rituals if you were an anthropologist?
- What does this drug and the contexts in which it is talked about tell us about contemporary social relations? At the broadest level, what would you say about the “politics” of this drug?

**WRITING PROJECT 3**

**RECONSTRUCTING DRUG POLICY**

**DUE April 25, 2023**

**This paper consists of 3 parts:**

**Introduction (2-3 pages):** include Background on Findings from Prior Work on Drug Subculture Paper; Statement of the Drug Policy Problem (as you see it—see below for concrete suggestions on problem definition, proposed solutions, etc.)

**State of Global Drug Policy Reports (5 pages):** Each student will choose a country such as Afghanistan; Australia; Brazil; British Columbia/Canada; Burma/Myunmar; China; Colombia; Denmark; Finland; Hungary; India; Indonesia; Japan; Mexico; The Netherlands; Pakistan; Poland; Portugal; Scotland; Singapore; Spain; Sweden; United Kingdom. You may choose a state within the United States: California; Colorado; New Mexico; or a region (Northern European countries; the Golden Triangle; coca-producing regions; Latin America; etc.); Questions to guide you:

- What is the overall goal and philosophy of this state/country/region’s drug policy system?
- How does this state/country/region put this goal/philosophy into action?
- What drug problems has this state/country/region faced historically? Which drugs were considered problematic, and which non-problematic?
- Under what political economic, or cultural conditions did these considerations change?
- Has this state/country/region shifted drug policy gears recently, and, if so, how so?
- How would you characterize this state/country/region’s overall approach to drug policy?

**Discussion (2-3 pages):** What would make a difference for the health and well-being of individuals who comprise your drug subculture? You will present these findings to the class in the succinct form that we used for the Drug Subcultures Discussion.

# Problem definition

- Who was responsible for getting this social problem onto the political agenda?
- What competing claims were made about how the problem should be defined? Were there people or groups who didn’t consider this a problem?
- Were there legal cases relating to the policy?

# Proposed solutions

- Who advanced proposals to solve the problem?
- What alternatives were advanced and why? Who advanced alternative accounts?
- Which solutions prevailed and why do you think they did so?
- What policy outcomes were developed at each level of government?

**Implementation**

- What policies were implemented? If they haven’t yet been implemented, how could they be?
- Who was held accountable for implementation?
- How was the implementation evaluated?

# Conclusion

- Do you think that these solutions were the “best” we could do?
- Can you think of any alternative proposals that might do better?
