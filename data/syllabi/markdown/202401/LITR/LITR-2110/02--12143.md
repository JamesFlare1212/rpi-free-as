## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

We can probably agree that reading literature is at least somewhat important. But why do you suppose so? When you think about it, literature is an incredibly inefficient way to convey meaning. And literature doesn’t necessarily make us more ethical or empathetic people (case in point: Jeff Bezos was inspired to start Amazon by Kazuo Ishiguro’s novel The Remains of the Day). So why do we—and why should we—read literature?

While reading literature is often understood as a solitary, contemplative, or escapist act—something we do privately, quietly, even secretly—we will see in this class that literature is also a profoundly social act. When read with an openness and willingness to examine ourselves and our society, the texts in this course offer unexpected ways of reflecting on our own values and grasping how we are implicated in the lives of others, how we intersect with and mutually constitute each other. Reading helps us narrate, and therefore make thinkable, the often-invisible relationships and systems that structure our lives.

Though reading can encompass any encounter with “texts” of any genre or medium, this course focuses on a very small selection of distinctly literary texts—specifically, four Anglo-American novels, spanning 200 years—to introduce you to some key authors, ideas, and aesthetic developments in the history of the modern novel.

This class begins with two presuppositions:

1. Vital to an education in any discipline is the creative act of reading. In addition to technical training, a genuine education includes developing attentiveness, curiosity, and critical thinking that allow you to examine yourself and the broader culture and society in which we all participate.
2. It is your right to be able to read and study literary texts and their history, which is also to say the history of human thought.

This process of reading attentively is not always easy. It involves developing skills and methods of intellection of imagining that are often undervalued in contemporary society; that can seem distant from the urgencies of everyday life; and that sometimes require uncomfortable, and even painful, self-reflection. With this warning out of the way, this course will be of interest to students wanting to become conscious readers of all sorts of literary and cultural texts and to better understand how art and culture contribute to the creation of social life, in ways both seen and unseen.

## Learning Outcomes

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes (CLOs):

- Reading and Interpretation: Read select literary texts from a range of periods—Romantic, modernist, contemporary—that represent a range of perspectives while building core skills in reading, interpretation, and argumentation appropriate to the humanities.
- Written/Oral Communication: Articulate thoughts and ideas clearly and effectively in written and oral forms to academic and general audiences; demonstrate ability to listen effectively, speak publicly, and express ideas to others; recognize potential communication barriers and adjust as needed; and write, revise, edit, and proofread.
- Critical Thinking/Problem Solving: Exercise sound reasoning to analyze texts, interpret new ideas, formulate effective questions, and overcome problems. Obtain, interpret, and use knowledge, facts, and qualitative data and demonstrate originality and inventiveness. Identify strengths and weaknesses of solutions, conclusions, or approaches to problems and texts.
- Intellectual Curiosity, Creativity, and Maturity: Demonstrate a desire to know and learn more (about self, others, and society) and approach ideas and conversation with imagination, creativity, and intellectual good faith.
- Capacity for Improvement: Understand intellectual inquiry as an ongoing process, approach assignments and projects as occasions for continuous improvement and learning, practice self-reflection and meta-cognition, engage in intellectual conversation with others, provide and receive constructive feedback from others.

## Policies

### Attendance Policy:

This course focuses on in-class discussion and collaborative learning, anchored in textual analysis. Aside from absences for illness, religious holidays, and documented absences for university-related activities, your physical and active intellectual presence at each class meeting is essential. Your success, and that of your peers, depends on your preparedness for class and your readiness to discuss our course materials (see Appendix 5 for more information on what constitutes such active presence).

Each of you will have an allotment of three (3) unexcused absences that do not fall under any of the categories above. Any further absences will incur a penalty of half a letter on your final grade.

If you miss any class meetings because of the Add/Drop period, you still must complete all reading and writing assignments from that time.

### Additional Academic Integrity Course Policy and Penalty Information:

Student-teacher relationships are built on trust. For example, students must trust that teachers have made appropriate decisions about the structure and content of the courses they teach, and teachers must trust that the assignments that students turn in are their own. Acts that violate this trust undermine the educational process. The Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities and The Graduate Student Supplement define various forms of Academic Dishonesty and you should make yourself familiar with these. In this class, all assignments that are turned in for a grade must represent the student’s own work. In cases where help was received, or teamwork was allowed, a notation on the assignment should indicate your collaboration.

I take plagiarism and other forms of academic dishonesty seriously. Anyone engaging in academic dishonesty, including using chatbots to write assignments (unless permitted for specific class activities), will be referred to the Dean of Students.
