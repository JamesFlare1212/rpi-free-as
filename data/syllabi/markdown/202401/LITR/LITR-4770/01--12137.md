## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

What does it mean to be a woman writer, and what is the social function of this designation? Is women’s literature necessarily *feminist* writing? Women Writers explores these questions through close reading of diverse literature produced by women in conjunction with feminist theories. The emphasis will be on women writers’ strategies for articulating gendered experiences and on the role of literature as cultural mirror and catalyst for social and political change. Avoiding any claims to a fixed “universal woman,” the course includes a cross-section of women’s experiences as women, authors, and members of diverse social groups. Although gender will serve as the primary category of analysis, attention will be paid to themes of love and sexuality, embodiment, trauma and memory, social class, race and ethnicity, selfhood, and family dynamics. This course emphasizes intellectual rigor, critical thinking and writing, creativity, collaboration, and student-centered learning.

## Course Goals

### Goals:

- To foster enjoyment of literature.
- To model close reading and interpretation as a means to improve critical thinking.
- To help students recognize that literature does not occur in isolation but is part of a complex dialogue within cultural and historical contexts.

- To help students hone their voices as scholars who enters critical conversations about literature and social issues.
- To encourage students to think intersectionally about the relationships among gender, race, class, sexuality, ability, and other vectors of identity.
- To showcase the writing process as a place for intellectual and emotional growth by emphasizing importance of revision and engaging meaningfully with feedback.

- To facilitate team building, small group-dynamics, and interpersonal communication skills by fostering a learning community.

## Learning Outcomes

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes (CLOs):

- • Interpret and critique significant literary works.
- Produce strong analyses of literary texts, supported by scholarly evidence, in relation to the material, economic, social, and political, and (trans)national conditions of textual production.
- Utilize theoretical knowledge from literary and cultural studies to situate discussions of women’s literature.
- Demonstrate oral communication skills through class participation and presentations.
- Develop supported viewpoints on how social constructions of identity shape contemporary literature.
- Recognize, interrogate, and evaluate personal and social values.
- Practice alternative methods of critical response, including but not limited to creative writing, graphic arts, and digital storytelling.

### Other Course Policies:

**Participation:** This is not a course in which you will read for information and pass an exam. Instead, the primary mode of learning will be dependent on consistent, active preparation and participation—discussion, interrogation, visits to office hours, and written reflections. Active participation demonstrates you recognize your responsibility for your education and reflects common professional tasks beyond the undergraduate classroom. To do well in this category, you must first and foremost maintain a positive attitude and open mind. You must prepare well to fully engage in class activities, including interacting with peers. **At a minimum, you should keep up with all readings, be prepared to engage with peers in small groups, make substantial efforts in peer review, and have at the ready one specific** **question and comment about a specific passage in the assigned reading for every class.** Expect that students will be called upon regularly to do this.

- ### Academic Integrity

  Student-teacher relationships are built on trust. For example, students must trust that teachers have made appropriate decisions about the structure and content of the courses they teach, and teachers must trust that the assignments that students turn in are their own. Acts that violate this trust undermine the educational process. The Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities and The Graduate Student Supplement define various forms of Academic Dishonesty and you should make yourself familiar with these.
