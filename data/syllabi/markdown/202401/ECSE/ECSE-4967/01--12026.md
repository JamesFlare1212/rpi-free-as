## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

As technologies based on quantum phenomena continue to proliferate, an understanding of the  
underlying physics of how these systems operate is critical for work in those fields. This course  
will begin by providing a foundation in the basic theory of quantum mechanics, such as the  
interpretation of wave functions, solutions to the Schrodinger equation for simple potentials, and  
formalism (i.e. Hilbert spaces, Hermitian operators and observables). The course will then focus  
on applying those fundamentals to develop tools for finding solutions to realistic quantum  
systems and to understand the physical models behind modern nanoscale technologies such as  
quantum tunneling-based devices, quantum dots and nanowires, superconductors, and quantum  
computing hardware (qubits, gates, circuits).

## Learning Outcomes

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes (CLOs):

- Explain the physical consequences of the probabilistic nature of quantum physics and how that differs from the deterministic nature of our everyday experiences with classical physics
- Solve the Schrodinger equation for simple potentials and interpret the solutions both in terms of the formalism of quantum mechanics and semi-classical arguments
- Apply basic quantum mechanical theory and approximation methods to model and solve realistic nanoscale systems
- Describe ways in which qubits and quantum gates can be physically implemented and discuss their advantages and disadvantages

10 Homework Assignments – submission through Gradescope  
Quantum mechanics is best learned through practice and homework assignments are  
correspondingly a central part of the learning process for this course. Homework problems will  
emphasize developing a deeper conceptual understanding of quantum mechanics, solving  
problems closely related to the lecture material, and applying tools from the lecture material to  
model and solve realistic quantum systems. Homework will generally be assigned and due  
weekly, except during exam weeks. Homework assignments that are submitted late are subject to  
a penalty of 10% of the total possible points for the assignment per day late.  
2 Midterm Exams  
Two midterm exams will assess students’ learning of the material in the first two units of the  
course. Exams will evaluate both conceptual understanding and the ability to mathematically  
solve problems from the course material. The exams are independent, closed-book assessments  
held during the usual lecture time and space. During the exam, students will be allowed a crib  
sheet that is provided by the instructor. A student may leave the exam session to use the restroom,  
but must leave any communicating devices behind.  
Students are required to communicate with the instructor prior to missing a scheduled assignment  
or exam. Official requests for alternate exam times and/or additional time to complete exams will be accommodated if received two weeks before the scheduled exam time. Requests received at a  
later time may be considered up to two days before the exam, but will be accommodated only if  
arrangements can be made. In cases in which arrangements for a makeup exam cannot be made  
within a reasonable timeframe, the instructor and student will discuss alternatives.  
Final Exam  
The final exam will be a cumulative assessment of the course material and will take place during  
finals week. Students are required to take the final exam. The same policies as outlined under the  
section above also apply to the final exam.  
Class Participation & Attendance  
Ten percent of the students’ grade will be based on participation and attendance. This will include  
attendance and active participation during class. This assessment will be based on the  
observations of the course staff and completing in-class questions and assignments.

### Additional Academic Integrity Course Policy and Penalty Information:

Student-teacher relationships are built on trust. For example, students must trust that teachers  
have made appropriate decisions about the structure and content of the courses they teach, and  
teachers must trust that the assignments that students turn in are their own. Acts that violate this  
trust undermine the educational process. The Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and  
Responsibilities and The Graduate Student Supplement define various forms of Academic  
Dishonesty and you should make yourself familiar with these. In this class, all assignments that  
are turned in for a grade must represent the student’s own work. In cases where help was

Intellectual integrity and credibility are the foundation of all academic work. A violation of  
Academic Integrity policy is, by definition, considered a flagrant offense to the educational  
process. It is taken seriously by students, faculty, and Rensselaer and will be addressed in an  
effective manner. If found responsible for committing academic dishonesty, a student may be  
subject to one or both types of penalties: an academic (grade) penalty administered by the  
professor and/or disciplinary action through the Rensselaer judicial process described in this  
handbook. Three relevant academic integrity violations to emphasize include:  
Collaboration: Collaboration is defined as deliberately facilitating an act of academic dishonesty  
in any way or form; for example, allowing another student to observe an exam paper or allowing  
another student to “recycle” one’s old term paper or using one another’s work in a paper or lab  
report without citing it as another’s work.  
Copying: Copying is defined as obtaining information pertaining to a graded exercise by  
deliberately observing the paper of another student; for example, noting which alternative a  
neighboring student has circled on a multiple-choice exam.  
Plagiarism: Plagiarism is defined as representing the work or words of another as one’s own  
through the omission of acknowledgment or reference. Examples include using sentences  
verbatim from a published source in a term paper without appropriate referencing, or presenting  
as one’s own the detailed argument of a published source, or presenting as one’s own  
electronically or digitally enhanced graphic representations from any form of media.  
The Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities defines the full list of forms of  
Academic Dishonesty and you should make yourself familiar with these. In this class, all  
assignments that are turned in for a grade must represent the student’s own work. In cases where  
help was received, or teamwork was allowed, a notation on the assignment should indicate your  
collaboration. If you have any questions concerning this policy before submitting an assignment,  
please ask for clarification. Students in this course should be aware that the items emphasized  
above also apply to the experimental/simulation data, Matlab code generated by each student or  
student team towards the completion of the experiment report. Tools exist to detect similarities between files and the staff reserves the right to employ such tools to deter code based academic  
dishonesty.
