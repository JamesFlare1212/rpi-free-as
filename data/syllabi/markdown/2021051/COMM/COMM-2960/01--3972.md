## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

This course engages students in a critical examination of television and its historical and present status as a dominant popular culture medium. Television in this context is broadly understood as an evolving medium for motion pictures in the age of technological convergence. Using a variety of analytical and theoretical approaches, we’ll consider the significant influence television has on individual, social, and cultural experiences—addressing questions of representation, identity, economics, and ideology. The class uses a collaborative learning process, allowing students to influence the content and emphasis of the class and reflecting the democratic nature of popular culture media.

## Course Goals

### Goals:

1\) You will develop a faculty for critical media engagement with television, learning how to apply aesthetic, historical, and critical frameworks as tools towards deepening your understanding of the medium. Watching numerous genres and modes of television, you’ll apply those critical lenses to their close study. Of particular importance is learning how to make connections between conceptual issues and ‘television’ as a cultural phenomenon and practice (of viewing, critiquing, sharing, and creating). Although this class focuses on television, it’s lessons can be applied to and contextualized in the larger mediascape; you should gain a deeper understanding of how media shapes us, as individuals, as groups and communities, and as cultures.

2\) As a complement to these critical tools, you will enhance your reflexive media awareness—your processual and dialogic understanding of your relationship to television—so that you can more fully articulate your experience, contextualize it, and empathize with others’ perspectives. You will develop your communicative/generative skills by articulating and refining your thoughts in oral, written, and presentational forms, and (if you are so inclined) by creating media works in various formats as a way of engaging with concepts we encounter in this class.

3\) Finally, this class uses a collaborative learning process; the course will be shaped by your interests and prior knowledge as a community, as well as by my disciplinary, personal and pedagogical expertise. Through the use of non-traditional educational strategies, you’ll engage in meta-examinations of the learning process, considering how the course functions as a ‘medium’ for learning.

## Learning Outcomes

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes (CLOs):

- Identify, explain, and evaluate conceptual and theoretical strategies involved in media studies as relates to television.
- Apply critical lenses to media texts, and debate the value and function of those critical strategies.

- Work collaboratively with a group of peers to compose a multi-faceted critique of a media text.

## Grading Criteria

### Criteria Details:

ASSESSMENT: ‘CRITIC POINTS’-BASED ‘GAMEFUL’ GRADING

Introduction:

Grading for this class will unfold a little differently than you may be used to. Using a ‘gameful’ approach, your performance in the class is based on the points that you earn, much like in some game systems (a video game, a table-top role-playing game, or what have you). Everyone begins with 0 points. At the start of the semester, there are 400,000+ points to be earned through various tasks. As you accomplish tasks in the class, you build up points towards your final grade. Except for the first two and last assignments, no assignment or task is ‘required;’ instead, you are able to plot your course through the class based on your interests and your schedule. You will be given a worksheet at the beginning of the semester that you can use to help you to plan out your goals and keep track of your points; make sure to refer to the assignments list and the calendar at the end of the syllabus to plan your own personal schedule.

‘Medal’ Based Scoring

Assignments will be assessed using a ‘medal’ system. For most daily and small assignments, that ‘medal’ will reflect the following categories. For certain larger projects, we may establish a more detailed rubric for grading, or reach an understanding through discussion and through my pedagogical expertise. In many cases, particularly for larger assignments, you may be invited (or ask) to revise an assignment in order to improve its medal.

Medal Points % Description

Diamond* 120% A sparkling exemplar for the class, full of dazzling insight

Platinum* 110% A perceptive, clever, and resilient exploration of assignment

Gold 100% Solid, weighty, polished, addressing all required aspects of assignment

Silver 90% A response with clarity and strength, but maybe a little tarnished

Bronze 80% Mostly complete, but hurried; some clarity, some weaknesses

Copper 70% Somewhat incomplete, and bends under the stress of examination

Nickel 60% Partial or relatively flimsy assignment

Tin\*\* 50% Dull or lifeless response, crumples in the breeziest of discussions

*To receive a ‘Platinum’ medal requires that you go above and beyond the requirements of the assignment, and the ‘Diamond’ requires even more in-depth work of great quality.

\*\*Below ‘Tin’ means you simply failed to turn in the assignment, or it’s too incomplete to grade

Late assignments

As a general rule, when there is a deadline, I’ll follow the following policy: For minor assignments, you will lose 1-2 ‘medal’ rankings if it is not on time, and I won’t be accepting minor work more than the next class period late unless we discuss it. For major assignments with a strict deadline (like the final), late work will automatically receive at least a 1 ‘medal’ ranking deduction, and one more ranking for each further day that it is late. Note, for final projects, I will base my ‘final-final’ reception date (even with late penalties) based on the date by which grades are due (TBA).

Revisions of Assignments

For some assignments, there is a draft / peer review / development process built into the syllabus’ calendar (see schedule below). However, if you feel that the grade wasn’t as high as you think you could have achieved on an assignment, we can make arrangements for a resubmission where you could receive a maximum of half the points you lost, as long as time permits. Note that this will NOT be possible for the final projects due to time constraints on grading.

Acts that violate this trust undermine the educational process.
