## Learning Outcomes

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes (CLOs):

- • Recognize and apply key social science concepts for analysis of food and agricultural issues, such as social class, gender, commodity chain, agri-food system, food justice, food system, vertical and horizontal integration, commodity chain, globalization, and nutritionalization.
- • Use social theories and concepts to interpret, explain, and critique the socio-cultural, political, economic, and philosophical factors that influence contemporary food production, distribution, and consumption.
- • Critically analyze and assess social science research on the agri-food system.
- • Generate original hypotheses about the agri-food system—including possible solutions to problems such as hunger, environmental degradation, and rural poverty.

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes Assessment Measures:

**Assignments:**

Grades will be based on student performance in the following assignments. Note that there is a semester-long research project, with grades for key milestones.

Attendance and participation (50 points)

- Class attendance is required, and when you are in class you are expected to actively participate in discussions and activities. Each day of active participation in class earns two points. I give everyone 10 free points toward the 50 point goal, recognizing that not everyone can participate actively all the time.

Reading response journal (50 points)

- You will maintain a weekly reading response journal and turn it in for review at designated intervals. For this assignment, you should not worry about achieving a “proper” or “elegant” writing style. Use your own voice. The goals are analysis, communication, and making connections among issues, theories, data, and experiences. The length is up to you, but you should aim to write at least 300 words each week that respond directly to the assigned readings and to the “Consider” questions posed in the syllabus.
- You can earn up to 5 points per week (for 10 weeks) for your reading responses. You will hand in your journal (all entries) on the following dates: **June 10, July 15, and August 19**. Each entry should have a title and a date. Each time you hand in the journal, include the previous journal entries, including my comments, so that there is an ongoing conversation in the document. I recommend that you use Google Docs for this assignment so that you can maintain one continuous document and allow me to comment on it.

Discussion leadership (25 points)

- Pairs of students will be responsible for guiding and structuring class discussion of the assigned readings for 45 minutes once during the semester. Your main goal in this assignment is to orchestrate a rich, fruitful discussion. Therefore, you will have to read the chosen selection and come up with a list of provocative questions to help guide, nurture, and stimulate the conversation of your classmates. **By 12 noon on the day prior to the class meeting**, the pair of discussion leaders should send me **6-10 proposed discussion questions and a list of terms/concepts (with definitions)** that are important for understanding the text. I will provide feedback and suggestions in time for you to make changes before the class meets. Some questions should be factual, asking fellow students to summarize main point(s) of the reading or to ponder the usage of key terms/concepts. The others should be open-ended, asking students to share their interpretations and opinions of the reading. Your grade for this assignment will be based half on the questions and definitions that you prepare and half on your facilitation of the discussion. More guidance for this assignment will be provided in class.

Food experience essay (25 points)

- You may complete this assignment **at any point during the summer semester**, up until August 16. The objective of this assignment is to participate in a food- or farming-related activity that is new to you and to write a short essay (maximum 1,000 words) in which you connect your experience to the key ideas of this course.
- You must propose a food experience to your professor in advance. Possible ideas include: preparing a meal from seasonal food purchased at a farmer’s market, trying a new kind of ethnic cuisine, interviewing elders about your family’s food traditions, visiting a farm, preparing a recipe that is challenging for you, volunteering at a food pantry, learning to preserve produce, visiting a food manufacturer or slaughterhouse, foraging for edible plants, living on a restricted food budget for a week, or something else that is new and interesting to you.
- The student garden at Nason Hall will need to be regularly watered and weeded. This is a great opportunity for a few students from this class, and could count as your new food experience.

Preliminary research bibliography (10 points)

- Decide on your term paper topic and use the library to find at least 25 relevant books, research articles, and magazine/newspaper articles. Download digital sources and save them in a reference manager like Zotero or Mendeley. Borrow physical copies of books and organize them in your workspace. If you are not able to access physical copies of books, use interlibrary loan to request scans of selected chapters. Turn in a bibliography listing these sources using Chicago author-date style.
- Due **June 10**

Annotated bibliography (10 points)

- After reviewing the sources you collected, refine your term paper topic by posing a more specific research question. Turn your preliminary research bibliography into an annotated bibliography that summarizes the value of each source for answering your research question. Eliminate sources that turned out not to be useful. Add sources that you discovered while taking notes on your initial list of sources (e.g. books cited by a source you read).
- Recommended: organize your annotated bibliography into themes and alphabetize within those themed sections.
- Due **July 1**

Thesis outline, presentation, and revision plan (10 points)

- In a short outline (maximum 2 pages), state the argument that you can make with the evidence you have considered so far. Outline the key ideas that support your thesis and cite the sources that provide supporting evidence of each of those points.
- Then, in a short (5-10 minutes) talk, explain your research question, your preliminary thesis, and the evidence that led you to this conclusion. Listen attentively to questions and feedback and write up a plan for responding to the critique.
- Turn in your outline along with your revision plan.
- **Presentations July 22, outline and revision plan due July 26**

Draft paper and revision plan (10 points)

- Turn in a complete draft of your paper for peer review. Word count should be between 4,500 and 5,000 words (20 double-spaced pages), excluding bibliography. Cite all sources using Chicago author-date style. This draft will be peer reviewed by a classmate.
- Draft must be ready for peer review in class on **August 5**

Final term paper (50 points)

- Revise and proofread your paper and turn it in for a final grade.
- Due **August 12**

Web presentation of research findings (10 points)

- As the final step of your research project, create web content for a public-facing research report. Summarize your term paper in 2 catchy paragraphs and choose at least one image (with appropriate permissions) to include on the class website. Select 3-5 sources as “recommended reading” to list at the bottom of the page.
- Due **August 16**

### Academic Policies:

- ### Academic Integrity

  Student-teacher relationships are built on trust. For example, students must trust that teachers have made appropriate decisions about the structure and content of the courses they teach, and teachers must trust that the assignments that students turn in are their own. Acts that violate this trust undermine the educational process. The Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities and The Graduate Student Supplement define various forms of Academic Dishonesty and you should make yourself familiar with these. In this class, all assignments that are turned in for a grade must represent the student’s own work. In cases where help was received, or teamwork was allowed, a notation on the assignment should indicate your collaboration.

  Violations of academic integrity may also be reported to the appropriate Dean (Dean of Students for undergraduate students or the Dean of Graduate Education for graduate students, respectively).

  If you have any question concerning this policy before submitting an assignment, please ask for clarification. In addition, you can visit the following site for more information on our Academic Integrity Policy: [Students Rights, Responsibilities, and Judicial Affairs](https://info.rpi.edu/dean-students/student-rights-responsibilities-and-judicial-affairs).
