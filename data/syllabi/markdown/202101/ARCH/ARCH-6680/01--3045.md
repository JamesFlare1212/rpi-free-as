### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes Assessment Measures:

***Student Learning Outcomes***

Students who successfully complete this course will be able to:

1\. Display an understanding of the parallel and divergent histories of architecture, landscape architecture, urbanism, and their related histories, theories and discourse in terms of political, economic, social, ecological, and technological factors, among others.

2\. Display an understanding of cultural movements which influence urban form, architecture, and landscapes including the various needs, values, behaviors, as well as social and spatial patterns that characterize cities in vastly different cultures and contexts. Within this framework students will develop an understanding of the role and responsibility of the architect to ensure equity of access to sites, buildings, and structures, in a variety of contexts.

3\. Identify, analyze, and explain the important theories, strategies, and polemics underwriting urban growth and its relation to geographical territory change over the past 40 years.

4\. Gain an understanding of the architectural theory AND the historical evolution of ideas. Students will be presented with summaries of canonical works and historically significant texts and conversations.

5\. Think about and analytically examine urbanism, architecture, and landscape through text and image and be able to communicate coherently the visual and verbal vocabularies of these ideas and visions in written and oral forms.

## Grading Criteria

### Criteria Details:

***Assignments + Grading***

Weekly readings will be posted on the course LMS site or delivered to students via email. By enrolling in the course, you are automatically given access to the course LMS. There will be approximately 50-100 pages of reading a week. Lectures & Section Meetings will be difficult to follow without knowledge from the readings, and it will be virtually impossible to catch up on late reading responses or missed Lectures.

Some of the texts are difficult, and will require a second reading. For best success: first skim and mark key points in your initial read through, follow through by rereading a second time more carefully and critically while taking detailed notes. Best practices includes reading with a pen and/or highlighter to mark your major observations and to take notes in the margins. It is also recommended that you keep one ‘tab’ on the main text, while at the same time keeping the footnotes nearby. Footnotes help you further understand where the author is coming from and where the ideas they are writing about are derived. At times authors will offer even more insightful information in their footnotes than in the main text itself.

Your reading responses will be viewed not only as solutions, but as methods of framing (non-obvious/oblique/et) questions. Responses should be thoughtful and articulate. Please do not summarize the texts. Responses must be your own thoughts and questions about the ideas the authors are addressing.

The course work will consist of four central components:

1\. Weekly Reading Responses (x12, 1-page responses) /2pts each

2\. Midterm Essay /24 points

3\. Final Project /30 points

### Additional Academic Integrity Course Policy and Penalty Information:

**Virtual Learning Guidelines**

Conducting a course online requires an adjustment to traditional ways of working. While nothing can replace in-person learning, the instructors ask that students remain open-minded to engaging with new pedagogies and workflows. In the time of this pandemic, schools of architecture and professional design practices alike are exploring innovative pedagogies and practices to retain school culture and even improve collaborative methods virtually. I would like our course to do the same together. At the same time, the instructors would like to acknowledge that challenges and frustrations are bound to occur - we will have space to vent - and hopefully address any serious matters with empathy and respect. Your education is paramount, but your health and well-being is most important.

***Academic Integrity***

The Rensselaer *Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities* defines various forms of Academic Dishonesty and procedures for responding to them. Academic dishonesty violates the trust between students and teachers. Students must trust that teachers have made appropriate decisions about the structure and content of the courses they teach. Teachers must trust that the assignments students turn in are their own performance. Violations of this trust undermines the educational process. Cheating, plagiarism (defined as the lack of attribution or citation of the work of others), willful damage or theft of another person’s work violate academic integrity. The first instance of the breach of this trust will merit a failing grade in this course and the violator(s) will be reported to the Dean of Students for disciplinary action.
