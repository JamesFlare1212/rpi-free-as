## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

*Author James Snyder provides a commonly accepted definition of research; it is “a systematic inquiry directed towards the creation of knowledge”*

Linda Groat in *Architectural Research Methods*

As an extension of the “Design Research Studio” taught in the fall semester, the “Interdisciplinary Research Studio” is structured around each student’s already defined individual research project. The goal this semester is to define and execute a systematic study of your subject matter in order to arrive at specific knowledge that can be replicated and transmitted to an interdisciplinary audience.

All student regardless of their specific topic of inquiry and methodology created will have to tackle the bigger issues that are outlined below.

The world’s population reached nearly 7.6 billion as of mid-2017 implying that the world has added approximately one billion inhabitants over the last twelve years. Today, the world’s population continues to grow, albeit more slowly than in the recent past. Ten years ago, the global population was growing by 1.24 per cent per year. Today, it is growing by 1.10 per cent per year, yielding an additional 83 million people annually. The world’s population is projected to increase by slightly more than one billion people over the next 13 years, reaching 8.6 billion in 2030, and to increase further to 9.8 billion in 2050 and 11.2 billion by 2100. (*2018 revision report, United Nations*). For the United States, according to the U.S. Census Bureau, United Nations, Pew Research and other sources, the U.S. will grow to 438 million people by 2050 – up 35% from our 2017 population of 326 million. (Global population grows to 9.7 billion by 2050.) And upwards of 75% of future Americans will live in cities and urbanized areas (up from 50% in 2008). In other words, by 2050 there will the same number of Americans living in cities as there are in the entire nation today!

When considering the built environment as a whole - Material Extraction, Manufacturing, Transportation, Construction, Active life, Maintenance and Disposal - the building industry has the largest carbon footprint of any other human activity on the planet. According to the U.S. Energy Information Administration (EIA), the building sector consumes nearly half (47.6%) of all energy produced in the United States. It was responsible for nearly half (44.6%) of U.S. CO2 emissions in 2010. By comparison, transportation accounted for 34.3% of CO2 emissions and industry just 21.1%. More importantly, at a global scale, according to a 2018 study by the United Nations, more people live in cities, with 55% of the world’s population residing in urban areas. In 1950, 30% of the world’s population was urban, and by 2050, 68% of the world’s population is projected to be urban. If we consider that continuing population growth and urbanization are projected to add 2.5 billion people to the world’s urban population by 2050 and 3.6 billion by 2100, **what are strategies that will allow the future growth of the urban built environment on the planet to become more** **resilient****?**

If we assume the same urban distribution by 2100 (68%) globally, this would mean that from 4.2 billion people that live in cities today, we will have 7.6 billion living in cities by 2100. This for us would mean - with a conservative estimate - that by 2100 (*this is in the next 80 years*) we would have to almost double the amount of cities on the planet. What are alternatives to the current practices of the building that will make the building industry more energy efficient and environmentally friendly? Where will all the material for such a huge construction project come from? How are we going to provide power for double the amount of building infrastructure and what does the overall growth mean about the quality of living in the future cities? How can we use information as infrastructure for the future city in order to cope with the complexities arising from such intense city densification?

These are some fundamental questions that we are trying to address through design and research in this studio.

**In the age of the Anthropocene, it is more relevant than ever before to consider any human activity in a larger context of production and consumption (processing) of MATERIAL, ENERGY and INFORMATION at scales far greater than those of the immediate object of inquiry.**

In this second installment of the studio we will still use the following as a definition: **Architecture is the “effective” distribution of MATERIAL, ENERGY AND INFORMATION in space for cultural affect.**

The studio will be divided in to three major phases: *Planning Research & Design*, which will intensively develop and formalize the student’s research plan and methodology; *Producing Research & Design*, which will focus on the execution of the design experiment and the acquisition and documentation of data; *Situating Research & Design*, which will reiterate the literature review and the specific contribution of the student’s research to their field of inquiry while, in parallel, developing final presentation assets

## Learning Outcomes

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes (CLOs):

- Students will learn how to develop a research methodology for a specific proposal incorporates a complex interrelationship of social, economic and technological issues.
- Students will understand how to integrate technical/scientific research to the building and thinking of architecture and design.
- Students will acquire skills of graphic and oral communication that interrelate knowledge from different disciplines within a coherent presentation.
- Students will develop a capacity to understand how their specific research approach is always linked to various other scales perhaps outside of their specific research scope.

## Grading Criteria

### Criteria Details:

GRADING CRITERIA AND PERCENTAGES OF FINAL GRADE:

10% Participation: Active participation in class discussions, pin-up/reviews, and quizzes will constitute the grade

40% Midterm: The midterm will be a cumulative review of the work performed to that point, including all research, analysis, and design. Students are expected to have a fully formed thesis for their project, correct to zoning bulk mass, structural concept, articulated bay, and schematic development on environmental systems and their building’s envelope. 50% Final project: The final project will review the full and final integrated development of the above requirements from the midterm, including full environmental analysis.

### Additional Academic Integrity Course Policy and Penalty Information:

STATEMENT OF ACADEMIC INTEGRITY

The Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities defines various forms of Academic Dishonesty and procedures for responding to them. Academic dishonesty violates the trust between students and teachers. Students must trust that teachers have made appropriate decisions about the structure and content of the courses they teach.

Teachers must trust that the assignments students turn in are their own performance. Violations of this trust undermines the educational process. Cheating, plagiarism (defined as the lack of attribution or citation of the work of others), willful damage or theft of another person’s work violate academic integrity. The first instance of the breach of this trust will merit a failing grade in this course and the violator(s) will be reported to the Dean of Students for disciplinary action.

The definitions and examples presented below are samples of types of academic dishonesty and are not to be construed as an exhaustive or exclusive list. The academic dishonesty policy applies to all students, undergraduate and graduate, and to scholarly pursuits and research. Additionally, attempts to commit academic dishonesty or to assist in the commission or attempt of such an act are also violations of this policy.

- Academic Fraud: The alteration of documentation relating to the grading process. For example, changing exam solutions to negotiate for a higher grade or tampering with an instructor’s grade book.
- Collaboration: Deliberate facilitation of academic dishonesty in any form. For example, allowing another student to observe an exam paper or allowing another student to “recycle” one’s old term paper or using one another’s work in a paper or lab report without citing it as another’s work.
- Copying: Obtaining information pertaining to a graded exercise by deliberately observing the paper of another student. For example, noting which alternative a neighboring student has circled on a multiple-choice exam.
- Cribbing: Use or attempted use of prohibited materials, information, or study aids in an academic exercise. For example, using an unauthorized formal sheet during an exam.
- Fabrication: Unauthorized falsification or invention of any information in an academic exercise. For example, use of “bought” or “ready-made” term papers, or falsifying lab records or reports.
- Plagiarism: Representing the work or words of another as one’s own through the omission of acknowledgment or reference. For example, using sentences verbatim from a published source in a term paper without appropriate referencing, or presenting as one’s own the detailed argument of a published source, or presenting as one’s own electronically or digitally enhanced graphic representations from any form of media.
- Sabotage: Destruction of another student’s work. For example, destroying a model, lab experiment, computer program, or term paper developed by another student.
- Substitution: Utilizing a proxy, or acting as a proxy, in any academic exercise. For example, taking an exam for another student or having a homework assignment done by someone else.
