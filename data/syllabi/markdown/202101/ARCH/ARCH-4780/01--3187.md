## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

The study of today’s small American towns and cities has been historically overshadowed by the development of large cities, despite their unique roles in the development of industry, agriculture, trade, transport and unique cultural identities. Often the narrative of the small city has been characterized as the story of local or regional centers that play peripheral, secondary or reactive roles to that of the larger metropolis. Over the last decade, and particularly during the COVID pandemic era, multiple shifts in migration patterns, models of living and working, and robust physical and virtual infrastructures are beginning to drive innovation in the small city lifestyle and the role and lifestyle within small cities play. Despite Troy’s glorious past as an economic urban powerhouse in the 19th century, the collapse of its iron, steel and collar manufacturing industry, saw a rapid decline in its urban fabric and economy. Nevertheless in the last decade, Troy has experienced an economic comeback due to renewed activity within the food, retail and real estate sectors. From 2013 to 2018, Troy like many other upstate New York cities began to see two and three-family homes saw their sales prices double, and selling in half the time. The rise of niche, small retail and a growing number of food enterprises including restaurants and microbreweries have contributed to the appeal of Troy’s urban revitalisation. However as the COVID-pandemic continues, the health of retail industries in small cities have experienced intense challenges. While shifts in the food sector have taken advantage of takeout or socially-distance space planning where possible, the full implications of new models of living, programming and the occupation of shared public space in small cities are yet to be seen.

"... much of modern life that seems “natural” is in fact the product of economic and ideological interests—it is culturally determined. That goes for our notions of dwelling as well as for our understanding of what constitutes the ideal family and how to house it." (Niklas Maak in the Living Complex)

As part of a wider SoA partnership with TAP Inc, the Troy Community Land Bank, Habitat for Humanity and engagement with the Future of Small cities, the Life Cycle Studio is an interdisciplinary, co-design studio that investigates the development of new retrofit housing typologies for the city of Troy. Following John Turner’s declaration that “housing is a verb”, the studio aims to develop a particular focus on (i) new patterns of occupation according to dynamic owner-tenant models, (ii) the integration of life-cycle thinking and environmental technologies in close response to patterns of occupation and housing rituals, (iii) social and economic innovation around housing models responding to a complex cyber physical context as well as (iv) innovative spatial programming and infrastructure that connects housing programming to the growing urban ecological campus along the street, which include existing urban farms, small businesses, Nature Lab, the Troy Bike Rescue, and the Sanctuary for Independent Media.

**STUDIO BACKGROUND**

The Life Cycle Studio is a joint Building Sciences and Architecture studio that offers students the opportunity to engage with and develop skills in an expanded research and design methodology for an existing, real-world deep retrofit building project. The project-based approach, retrofitting an actual building previously owned by the Troy Community Land Bank, enables active engagement and learning in a direct and immediate way. Given that roughly 75% of buildings that will exist in 2050 already exist today, the studio’s focus responds to this incumbent 21st century condition that has necessitated a shift away from the ‘blank slate’ towards the retrofit building and its wider urban landscape as the primary context of response. The retrofit focus also responds to New York State’s goal to transition to 70% renewable electricity by 2030, shift to 100% clean power by 2040 and to reduce carbon emissions by 80% by 2050. The studio will be run in partnership with TAP Inc., Troy Community Land Bank, Habitat for Humanity (Capital District) and engaged with the Future of Small Cities Institute.

## Course Goals

### Goals:

The Life Cycle studio 2021 is proposed as the first milestone studio working directly with a retrofit house owner, and is part of a long-term initiative by the SoA for evolving new academic-community models of engagement. The goals are to:

- To overcome the false dichotomy between ‘architecture’ and ‘building’ through a comprehensive approach that not only accepts but seeks technical and material constraints in search of ‘poetics of economy’ targeted to formulate a new language.
- To question the traditional role of architect and emphasis on creative “authorship” and building sciences as “consultant” for a practice embedded within the community and engaged in the social, political and economic processes that shape our built environment.
- Understand the architectural object [the house] not as an isolated formal paradigm but as an integrated element of larger built matrixes [the street, the neighborhood, the city]; and questioning the contradictions of urban upgrading that displaces and segregates [gentrification] the urban environment.
- Integrate the school of architecture to its immediate community, dedicated to the nurturing of not just employable designers but engaged citizens in a democratic society.

Develop new co-design methodologies around social, economic and environmental goals.

## Course Content

### Content Details:

**Phase 1: Encyclopaedia of 3325 6th Avenue**

To gain a working knowledge of the complexities of 3325 6th Avenue and its urban context, the first four weeks of the semester will be devoted to documenting and analyzing three scales of the studio’s site, 3325 6th Avenue. Students will produce three ‘synthetic’ drawings at the scale of the neighbourhood, the city and the dwelling. Each drawing will comprehensively document an understanding of context through a series of layered drawings that unpack the architectural, environmental, cultural, economic, social, historical and future imaginings of 3325 6th Avenue to its surrounding site. Students are asked to approach each drawing as a practice of layered analysis. Each drawing is composed of three distinct layers --the mapping layer, the collage or “cabinet of wonders” layer and the technical drawing layer.

**Phase 2: Schematic Design and Phase 3 Design Development**

Working in teams, architecture students will engage in the development of a number of proposals stemming from the Deep Retrofit Strategic Plan. Outputs will include a range of 3D files, digital 2D drawings and renders of proposed retrofit design.

**Phase 4 Visual Narratives Presentation**

The last two weeks of the semester will focus on the development of a virtual narrative presentation integrating work from Phases 1-3 for the final review.

## Learning Outcomes

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes (CLOs):

No Course Learning Outcomes are available for this course.

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes Assessment Measures:

1. Students will have a basic understanding of the urban conditions, socio-economic, environmental context and challenges facing small and mid-size post-industrial cities and their existing housing stock.
2. Students will be able to critically identify and interpret existing constraints and exploit them as conceptual grounding to develop novel design proposals that interwork structure, systems and program in a comprehensive piece of adaptive reuse (and ‘retrofit’) architecture.
3. Students would have critically revised the suitability of prevalent market housing models, proposing new typological solutions for the diverse needs of contemporary forms of living.
4. Students will have been introduced to existing community and financial models and speculated how they could be articulated to support housing options for new contemporary lifestyles.
5. Students will have explored new and alternative technologies, building systems and tactical approaches (retrofit, refurbishment, conversion, adaptive reuse, etc.) to inform the design within existing buildings.
