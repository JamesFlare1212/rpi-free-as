## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

This course will offer a rigorous, 4000-level (pre-graduate) introduction to the history of American technology from Colonial times to the present. Major topics that we will address in the course include:

- Technology and the American colonial experience
- Importing the British industrial revolution
- Building a national infrastructure
- The American System of Manufactures
- Agricultural mechanization
- Urban expansion
- Westward expansion
- Technology and the American imperial experience
- Technology and the ascent of science
- Prosperity and Consumption
- Technology and the Great Depression
- Wars and the “American Century”
- Technology in Postwar America
- Technology and 1950s America
- Technology and the Cold War
- From Viet Nam to Economic Crisis
- Globalization and the Engineering Workforce

In addition to functioning as a traditional reading seminar, the course will introduce students to the basic, underlying skills of historical inquiry. Although we generally will not be working with primary (archival) sources, students should be prepared to identify and analyze academic texts on specific historical topics that interest them, with the guidance of the instructor. In addition to the formal prerequisite, some familiarity with, or interest in history will be an important prerequisite for the course. Please note that grades and evaluation standards will be adapted to the diverse needs of students, ranging from those taking the course for general humanities credit, to those interested in graduate study in history, STS, and allied disciplines.  
Course Format

In order to achieve the objectives of this course, the class will generally follow the following format:

- **On Tuesdays** we will normally operate as a reading seminar, in working with one or more of the chapters from the required texts for this course.
- **On Fridays** students will report on their own “independent inquiry” into history, including the additional readings they chose for the week. (Think of this as “show and tell” based on a specific article that you found for the week.) On occasion, Dr. Akera may supply additional information based on his own research. You may work on these additional inquiries in small groups.

Please see the detailed schedule for exceptions due to special exercises at the start of the semester, as well as institute holidays. During the early part of the semester, we will also spend some time as needed learning how to identify appropriate academic literature using databases and other online and off-line resources.

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes Assessment Measures:

# Principal Assignments

The principal assignments for the course will consist of the following:

- Required readings
- Interpretive essays (8-12 essays, depending on standing (*see below))
- Final paper (proposal, list of sources, outline, first draft, final draft)
- Electronic portfolio
- Self evaluation

***Important Note:** If you are an STS Major or Minor, or a History Minor, or are considering graduate study in History or a related discipline, please see me to discuss the different set of requirements that apply to you. I will be asking you to let me know of you degree plans at the start of the semester.

Required Readings & Independent Historical Inquiry

For those who are not majoring or minoring in History or STS, the required readings for this course consists of the assigned readings plus at least one academic article (or substantial primary document) each week that you identify through guided, independent historical inquiry. The additional articles that you find may fall into one of the following categories:

- A historical article on the week’s topic
- An article outside of the history of technology, such as in U.S. History or the philosophy or sociology of technology that provides a useful context for interpretation and analysis
- A historiographic article (we will discuss what this is in class)
- An article dealing with a “concept” that is relevant to the week’s discussion (e.g. “technological determinism”, “technology and modernity,” or “the science-technology relationship”)

You may also select books, and do one or more book reports for “extra credit.” While you may—and indeed are encouraged to—consult with online sources such as Wikipedia in order to gain some general background, these are not “academic articles” and will not in themselves satisfy the independent historical inquiry requirement. Again, I will guide you through the process of identifying appropriate sources beyond those you find in the assigned textbooks.

Interpretive Essays

Your interpretive essays should be turned in on Tuesdays (emailed as MS Word of pdf; please do not just send a link). To both demonstrate and strengthen your skills of historical interpretation, you will be asked to submit regular essays based on the required readings, including the readings you turn up through independent or guided historical inquiry. Non-majors and minors will be required to submit at least eight (8) essays during the semester, and at least one essay every other week beginning on the third week of class or earlier. Only one essay may be turned in during any given week. These essays must offer your own interpretation of the historical (and other) texts that you read. In general, each essay should cover a specific week’s topic, although where opportunities arise, they may be cast as comparative essays that deal with topics from two or more weeks in the class. These essays should be between 400 and 750 words in length. You should not turn in any essay that has not been thoroughly rewritten or revised, and proofread at least once.

This is a “regular” writing assignment. Regular reflection is necessary for you to build up your capacity to interpret historical texts, and therefore it is important that you not “pile up” this assignment for the end of the semester. *To reiterate, you must turn in one essay at least every other week beginning no later than the third week of class.* (You will be advised to drop the course if you do not meet this requirement.)

Each interpretive essay will be evaluated, using our Uniform Evaluation Scale (see below), based on the following rubric:

- Reading (20%) – Does the essay indicate that you have done the expected readings, and that you have read the texts thoroughly? Do you understand the author’s arguments, including its major findings, and some of the subtleties of her or his analysis?
- Interpretation and analysis (40%) – Do you offer your own interpretation of the texts, as opposed to simply summarizing what you read? Is your essay build around a question or some specific line of inquiry? Do you compare, contrast, and/or integrate, if appropriate, multiple historical texts, and analyze the different perspectives of the different authors? Do you incorporate your own experiences, or relate the historical evident to present day concerns if the opportunity arises to do so in your essay?
- Informed judgment about the issue addressed in the essay (20%) – Do you come to a reasoned judgment about the author’s arguments, or new perspectives and insights about some aspect of history that you gained as a result of your reading and analysis? Is this more than opinion? Informed judgment is about providing sound evidence and arguments for your point of view.
- Quality of writing (20%) – Is the essay well written? Is it clear that this is not your first draft, but that you’ve been spending some time rewriting and reworking this material in an effort to produce a strong essay? While I will not place much emphasis on grammar (I don’t teach this in this course), is it clear that you have at least proofread the text so that you don’t annoy the reader? (Hint: You never want to annoy a person who’s grading your work! Okay, just do it. Set aside the time. If need be, I’ll accept your essay one day late and by email, if this is what keeps you from proofreading your text.)

Final Paper

The final paper should be a “cumulative” work that helps you draw together all of the material you read on a given topic or theme during the semester. The final paper may deal primarily with a single topic, or deal with a theme that works across multiple weeks and topics. If you choose the latter, it really should not be necessary for you to do any additional reading beyond the required readings (including the articles you found), although some care may be necessary in choosing your additional readings. You are of course welcome to choose additional readings and materials specifically for the final paper.

One obvious option for the final paper will be to take one of your interpretive essays and build it up through additional reading and reflection. On the other hand, you may wish to write on a topic that appears later on in the semester. Either way, you will be asked to submit a brief proposal, list of possible sources, outline, and a first and final draft so you have an opportunity to improve your historical interpretation and analysis based on my feedback.

The final paper should be at least 1500 words in length, and be based on at least 4-6 sources (this can include up to two individual chapters from Pursell). For those who are majoring in STS or are considering graduate studies in History and related fields, the final paper should be between 3000 and 4000 words in length, with 12 or more sources. Please turn in all material typed and double spaced. We may also arrange an impromptu discussion of everyone’s final papers.

Electronic Portfolio & Self Evaluation

All students will be asked to submit an electronic portfolio containing all of your work from this semester. My evaluation of your overall portfolio contributes to the final “class participation” grade (it’s more than just class participation, and represents how you’ve developed as a student capable of sophisticated inquiries in History), which is worth 1/3 of your final grade, so **make sure to save all of your assignments (and back it up on a thumb drive, etc…) so you can compile your portfolio at the end of the semester.** Please upload your portfolio to Google Drive or similar, and send me a link. Please DO NOT create a website with links, as I will be downloading the entire portfolio.

Your portfolio should consist of downloadable folders entitled:

- “Essays” containing all of your interpretive essays.
- “Articles” (optional) containing a selection of the articles you found yourself that you thought were most interesting.
- “Other Sources” (optional) containing interesting digital images, primary documents, and other material that you wish to share with me and with students in future semesters.
- “Final Paper” with the outline, first draft, and final draft of your paper.
- A required self-evaluation of your efforts and accomplishments in relation to the three major objectives for this course. Further details will be provided.

Al items, except your self-evaluation, may be shared with current and future students as part of a past work archive. (You may, however, designate specific items that may contain personal and/or private information as “DO NOT SHARE.” Please note this at the top of your essay, or paper.)

Both the electronic portfolio and self evaluation will be used in the final grading process.

## Grading Criteria

### Criteria Details:

# Grading and Evaluation Criteria

Uniform Evaluation Scale

I will be using the following uniform evaluation scale for all of your assignments:

- Basic (D) Limited and only rudimentary demonstration of the desired trait or ability. Adequate effort and engagement with the assignment (including the required readings, if applicable).
- Developing (C) Clear evidence of an emerging/developing competency in the required trait or ability.
- Solid (B) A solid demonstration of the required trait or ability; Solidly meets the instructor’s basic expectations with regards to the learning outcomes for the course.
- Strong (A) “Strong,” or even “Nuanced” or “Outstanding” performance that demonstrates original thinking, unique strengths as a student, and/or excellence in ways that exceed the basic expectations of the course and instructor.

Evaluation Weights & Criteria

The following weights are given to the principal assignments:

- Interpretive essays 33-1/3%
- Final paper 33-1/3%
- Class discussions, participation, and electronic portfolio 33-1/3%

In addition, you must complete all major assignments, including each of the components of the final project, in order to receive a passing grade in this course.

**Interpretive Essays**

I do not expect anyone, other than STS majors, to attain a grade above “Developing” in your early interpretive essays. Your grade for the interpretive essays will be based on what grade you are able to consistently demonstrate by the end of the semester. However, failure to engage seriously with the assignment may result in a failing grade for the assignment, which may affect your grade. (It won’t count towards the minimum number of essays required.)

At least eight (8) interpretive essays (with a passing grade) are required

- -1 letter grade (from your interpretive essay grade) for each missing essay

**Final Paper**

Grades will be assigned for each project component.

Your final paper grade will be a weighted average of these grades, with the following weights

- Project proposal (5%)
- List of possible sources (10%)
- Outline (10%)
- First full draft (25%)
- Final draft (50%)

You may collaborate with others on the final paper. If you do so, you will be asked to include a brief memo describing who did what work for each component of the final paper. Individual grades will be assigned based on my best judgment of the work contributed by each individual. The expected length indicated is per person.

### Other Course Policies:

# The Fine Print

Class Participation: Class participation is an integral part of this course and is reflected in the grading structure. On the other hand, I consider active listening and silent engagement to be as important as speaking up in class. Indeed, those who dominate classroom conversation may negatively impact other students’ ability to remain engaged and learn, and hence be considered a detriment to our goal of creating a strong community for historical inquiry. Please be aware of this, and be willing to work on the subtle art of seminar conversation. It is important that you demonstrate your engagement with this class. However, I do specifically recognize that some students have difficulty speaking up in class (for example, ESL students). If you feel this may be the case, please speak with me early on in the semester to make special arrangements (for example, placing greater emphasis on your written assignments).

Late Submissions: No late papers or assignments will be accepted except through advance arrangement with the instructor.

Gender Inclusive Language: Students in this course are expected to use gender-neutral or gender-inclusive language in their writing. Every time you use a masculine-oriented word to refer to people in general, the implicit effect, even if unintended, is to whisper: *women don’t count.* Essays and other work that do not use gender inclusive language will not receive a passing grade. If you are unfamiliar with the practice of gender inclusive language, please refer to this Wikipedia entry: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gender-neutral_language

COMM+D: Writing is an important component of any professional career, whether or not you work in the humanities. Once you’re on the job, you will be writing upwards of 1-2 dozen memos and emails each day, in addition to any formal study or report that you produce. Your performance will always be evaluated based on how well you convey your ideas. Periodically, I may ask you to seek the services of the Center for Global Communication+Design (COMM+D). It is located on the first floor of Folsom Library. You may obtain further information at https://info.rpi.edu/comm-d. I may also require you to have someone in the Center go over your written assignment before you can resubmit it for consideration. If this is the case, you **must obtain a stamp from the Center,** and turn in all versions of your work, and my original feedback, in addition to the final revised version. Keep in mind that improving the mechanics of writing on any assignment will not be enough to receive a higher grade if the content remains inadequate.

Communications Intensive Requirement: Although the course does not yet have this standing, I will be submitting the course to be recognized as a “communications intensive” course.

ESL/LD Students: The requirements for this course will be adjusted to serve the needs and capabilities of ESL and LD students. Students who have difficulties reading or writing should feel free to notify me about your particular situation. In general, the guideline I will use for making adjustments will require you to spend eight hours on out-of-class activities each week. All students are also expected to spend a minimum of two hours per week on written assignments.

Use of Student Generated Materials: All written materials submitted for this course will be considered as contributing to the general educational mission of Rensselaer. They may be posted online or elsewhere as part of a student work archive, or used for purposes of educational research and assessment. However, I also respect student rights to privacy and intellectual property. Students are therefore granted an a priori right to exemption from the public disclosure of their work. Requests for exemption should be submitted in writing (either a general note or a note on specific assignments will suffice). Student generated material are also not to be used for purposes beyond those specified herein without the express permission of the student(s) involved, and any scholarly work published from this material must also do so without individual identifiers except when requested by the student herself or himself. A notice to this effect will accompany any public or private posting of student works.

Academic Honesty: Student-teacher relationships are built on trust. Students must trust that teachers have made appropriate decisions about the structure and content of the course, and teachers must trust that the assignments students turn in are their own. Any and all acts that violate this trust undermine the educational enterprise. They contradict our very reason for being at Rensselaer. The *Rensselaer Handbook* defines various forms of academic dishonesty and the procedures for responding to them.

Students should note, in particular, that academic penalties for plagiarism are harsh. Any use of another person’s work, including the use of other people’s ideas and interpretations, must be acknowledged through proper attribution. All direct use of another person’s words must be placed in “quotations.” You must indicate when you paraphrase another person’s work. You should use an accepted citation format such as the MLA Citation Style (See Purdue University OWL, MLA Citation Guide). In college, we do expect you to know what constitutes plagiarism. If you are at all uncertain about the academic norms regarding plagiarism, play it safe, and just speak with me about it first.

For this course, penalties for academic dishonesty, especially in cases involving plagiarism, can vary from failure of assignment to failure of the course plus a citation in the student’s academic record. The specific penalty will depend on my assessment both of the intent and severity of the case. As noted in the student handbook, any such assessment will be based on the instructors’ best judgment about what occurred.

Collaboration: Collaboration is not only allowed, but encouraged in this course. Interpretive essays should be written individually, but may be based on discussions that occur in and out of class. Final papers may be jointly written. The only requirement is that you give proper attribution to the source of your ideas (including class discussion), and in the case of jointly written papers, provide clear indication of who wrote what sections, and who did what work. (A separate memo may work the best.)

### Additional Academic Integrity Course Policy and Penalty Information:

Students should note, in particular, that academic penalties for plagiarism are harsh. Any use of another person’s work, including the use of other people’s ideas and interpretations, must be acknowledged through proper attribution. All direct use of another person’s words must be placed in “quotations.” You must indicate when you paraphrase another person’s work. You should use an accepted citation format such as the MLA Citation Style (See Purdue University OWL, MLA Citation Guide). In college, we do expect you to know what constitutes plagiarism. If you are at all uncertain about the academic norms regarding plagiarism, play it safe, and just speak with me about it first.

For this course, penalties for academic dishonesty, especially in cases involving plagiarism, can vary from failure of assignment to failure of the course plus a citation in the student’s academic record. The specific penalty will depend on my assessment both of the intent and severity of the case. As noted in the student handbook, any such assessment will be based on the instructors’ best judgment about what occurred.

# Guide to Additional Resources

Finally, the “independent inquiry” portion of this course will be much easier to do if you have access to the following resources. Selected pages have been posted on LMS under “Additional Resources”. These books may also be available in Folsom Library:

- Merritt Roe Smith and Gregory Clancey (eds.), *Major Problems in the History of American Technology,* Major Problems in American History (series; Boston: Houghton Mifflin, 1998).

A selection of more advanced (graduate level) articles on key topics in the history of US technology.

- Carroll Pursell (ed.), *American Technology* (Malden, Mass.: Blackwell Publishers, 2001).

This earlier compilation by Pursell offers historical articles and primary documents on many of the topics addressed in Pursell’s two textbooks, but obviously with a different voice than Pursell’s. They can provide you with an opportunity to see if you agree with Pursell’s interpretation of the texts which he clearly relied upon to write his latest books. It also includes various lists for further reading.

- Carroll Pursell (ed.), *A Companion to American Technology* (Malden, Mass.: Blackwell Publishers, 2005).

Pursell’s *Companion* is built around specific “themes,” and contains articles written by other historians of technology. It will be especially useful if you want to write a final paper organized around a theme that covers multiple weeks, rather than a specific week’s topic. The themes covered include “Sites of Production”, “Sites of Contest (Conflict)”, “Technological Systems”, and “Technological Culture.”

- Carroll Pursell (ed.), *Technology in America: A History of Individuals and Ideas,* 2nd edition (Cambridge, Mass.: MIT Press, 1990).

This text, also based on articles written by other historians of technology, focuses on specific individuals and their ideas. They therefore give you a different, detailed entry into a particular topic, which may also be useful in writing your interpretive essays.

- Ruth Schwartz Cowan, *A Social History of American Technology* (New York: Oxford University Press, 1997).

This book often offers an alternative interpretation with a stronger social history component than *Machine in America.* It may be useful for writing critical essays: Where a chapter parallels Pursell, you can easily write a comparative essay on that unit.
