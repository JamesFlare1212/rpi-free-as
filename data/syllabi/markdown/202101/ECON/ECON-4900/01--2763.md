## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

For Economics majors, usually taken in the student’s final year, the Seminar in Economics is a capstone research experience. Students may write a research paper on an economics topic, or may participate in an Economics faculty member’s research project. Writing, revision, and discussion are required.

**Theme of Our Course**

We will emphasize a particular theme rather than trying to be inclusive and look into any and every topic in economics. The theme we will use is patent data research. Focusing our research to use patent data has several advantages. Working on related topics makes it easier to help each other by sharing ideas and approaches. Having data to support projects helps ensure that good-quality projects are possible. Data spanning many fields of economics also facilitates a range of research topics.

As examples of patent data research, here are some projects you might pursue. You could:

- Follow the build-up of knowledge within a technological field, tracing flows of knowledge and related ideas through patent citations.
- Analyze competition among firms in an area of technology, by identifying competing firms making a product.
- Explore how well patent data work as forecasting metrics, to predict future activity in different areas of technology.
- Analyze the flow of scientific knowledge across scientific disciplines, to see how one discipline influences another.
- Propose mathematical network analysis methods to trace not only the most-prominent path of patents citing each other within an area of technology, but alternative technological paths that play out over time.
- Analyze how the research composition of universities evolves over time.
- Measure the progress of an environmental or energy technology over the years, to test the drivers of technological progress such as how worldwide government subsidies or R&D funding spur technological progress.
- Use patents as a lens to study technological progress in a health care technology.
- Examine movements of leading inventors between employers, asking whether you can identify economic drivers of their job changes.
- Compare alternative metrics of the importance of patents, and how choice of metric affects research outcomes.
- Examine the tendency of companies to file product- but not process-related patents, to check which types of business file more or fewer process patents, and why.
- Trace the quantity and composition of patent output by nation, to probe which nations have grown their technological output fastest and why, and the reasons for shifts in areas of technology being developed in each nation.
- Examine why some patent authorities disclose more or less information in the Espacenet patent database, and how the information gaps affect research.
- Check which companies file more versus less international patent portfolios, where they file, and why.
- Analyze quantitatively the trends in patent research in economics, to show how and why they are changing over time.

Any of these and many other possibilities can make for a good Seminar in Economics project.

**Choice of Topic**

Students in Seminar in Economics will each focus on one, narrowly-defined topic. Given our time constraints—a semester is short!—we don’t have much time to think about topics. We just have to pick something and get going. Therefore, you will have just one week to pick a topic.

I encourage you to organize topics so groups of people work on similar topics. That way you can share ideas such as data sources, important literature, and research methods. You are still required to write up your own assignments and reports, do your own data analyses, and give your particular project its own character that fits your personal interests, approach, and conclusions.

Two approaches are allowed. They are: pick a topic related to our theme, or help an Economics professor as a research assistant.

- Most people should find a topic related to our theme, one that fits your interest, has information available, and seems doable during the semester. For example, any of the list of research projects above would make a reasonable topic, although you will narrow down the research question more explicitly. More about data resources is below.
- A research assistant experience lets you help an Economics professor who has indicated that she or he would like assistance with a particular project. This differs from our main Economics Seminar activity in that the sponsor has chosen the research agenda, and you do what needs to be done for their research needs. This provides a chance to participate in their research and see how their type of research works, while helping them, even though you will probably only deal with a small piece of the overall research agenda. You might meet with the sponsoring professor once to find out what needs to be done, and then only as necessary during the semester to ensure the job is getting done right. Please understand that the sponsoring professors are busy and are not acting as teachers in this role, so you will probably only interact with the sponsoring professor a few times. You will still participate in the Economics Seminar course, to discuss your research, get ideas about how research works, and provide written reports about your research goals and progress in the research assistant role. Research assistance opportunities will be posted on LMS.

Whichever you do, it is important to confirm my (Prof. Simons’) approval. I will check that your project seems do-able and appropriate for an Economics degree, and I will confirm any research assistant projects.

Most research activity and writing will be largely independent. However, we will try to help each other as the semester goes along—that’s much of what we will do during our weekly meetings. We can best come up with methods and resources and ideas for topics related to the main theme.

How should you choose a topic? Consider these criteria:

- Future career plans: Build knowledge for an upcoming career and to aid job interviews.
- Interest: Be motivated to keep moving ahead throughout the semester.
- Do-ability: You have one semester, and time requirements are often underestimated (by 3x).
- Contribution: Ensure there is some sort of research contribution relative to prior work.
- Data availability: Check right away whether high quality, in-depth data are available.
- Methodology: Econometric data analyses, with literature review and qualitative insight, are the norm and doable. Experimental or detailed descriptive studies may also be appropriate. Mathematical or other theory work is rarely feasible with reasonable quality. Literature review alone is usually not acceptable (systematic bibliometric data studies can be okay). Replications and extensions of prior work are generally fine.
- Research assistant projects: The Economics professor must agree your participation, for Seminar in Econ credit.
- Research assistant project content: Some higher-level thinking or analysis is required (not just data collection or transcription or downloading articles).

Some suggested research assistant projects with Economics professors are listed on LMS.

Most projects involve data analysis through econometrics or related methods. As a general rule, plan to use large complex databases rather than small simple ones; this will make your project results more substantive. Panel data is the norm for the sorts of econometric analyses we’ll do, and rather than analyzing ten companies or inventors over ten years, you will tend to do better if you can analyze a hundred or a thousand companies or inventors over twenty tears. Or you might analyze the citation network not among fifty patents, but among thousands or tens of thousands of patents. Sometimes you have to keep the numbers reasonably small because of difficulties compiling the data or because the topic of study only has so many data points, however, remember that more data will generally make it more feasible to find results, and that if you are relying on a small dataset to analyze a complex topic, you probably won’t be able to figure anything out. Do not plan simply to do a time series analysis of ten or twenty data points, because given the topics we are working on, it will be too hard to figure out what is causing the patterns in your data and what to make of your results. (You could do a detailed analysis of economic history evidence that mainly draws on qualitative facts from many sources, and happen to include a graph of ten or twenty data points, but then the substance of your paper would be based primarily on the qualitative evidence, not the 10-20 data points or a regression with them.)

Your specific topic or project choice *must* be approved by the instructor of this course. Approval is required because some topics are not feasible in practice, require special permissions, or lack sufficient economics content. Human subjects research is subject to federal rules that require special consideration.

**Checking Prior Work, for Topic Choice and Background Knowledge**

In choosing a topic, you should have a reasonable sense of what prior work has done on the topic. If prior research has proven that your proposed idea misses the point, is overly simplistic, or addresses the obvious, then maybe you need to adjust your plans. In fact, one helpful way to pick a research topic is to find a recently published paper in a good economics journal and use it as a starting point—you can then ask yourself whether you could replicate (to check validity) or extend the original study, or whether you notice anything you might disagree with to investigate further, or whether the paper suggests a need for further work to answer remaining questions.

To identify good economics journals, journal rankings can help. One ranking that reflects how economists actually think about journal quality (rather than just citation counts which can be very field-specific) is Kristie M. Engemann and Howard J. Wall, “A Journal Ranking for the Ambitious Economist,” *Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis Review*, May/June 2009, pp. 127-140: https://files.stlouisfed.org/files/htdocs/publications/review/09/05/Engemann.pdf. Any of the journals in their list, especially near the top of their list, should be quality sources.

To locate prior work, you should get very familiar with our library’s resources to search for research literature and to obtain research work. The Rensselaer library web page is: https://library.rpi.edu. Near the top on the right side is a link saying “+ DATABASES”. Click on that link, to open up further links that say Databases, e-Journals (Subjects), and e-Journals (Title). Click on Databases. Then click on E and go to EconLit, or click on S and go to Scopus. In Scopus, you may want to focus on economics, business, or social science papers, so that you identify more relevant published research literature. Also useful is JSTOR (click on J among the databases), where you can search many of the leading economics journals; it may help to restrict the search by choosing an Item Type of Articles only, and perhaps a Discipline of Economics only. If you cannot obtain an article from links in the databases, then use the library’s e-Journals (Title) link (click on “+ DATABASES” on the library webpage to show the e-Journals (Title) link).

If an article cannot be obtained at Rensselaer, either electronically or in print copies, you can use Interlibrary Loan. The link to “Interlibrary Loan (ILLiad)” is on the library’s main web page. This is sometimes important, since some of the most major journals charge so much for access to recent issues that the library finds it more cost-effective to have us use interlibrary loan. Loans of articles are often very quick, within 2 days or less, and come in convenient electronic form—watch your email for a note that lets you download the article from your Interlibrary Loan account page. For books, first try “ConnectNY” on the main library web page.

**Written Report**

Regardless whether you carry out research on your own topic, or work with a professor on an existing research project, you must write and rewrite a report. You will have ongoing opportunities for discussion of your work. You will review two other reports, and the reviews may provide ideas to redraft and improve written reports.

Your report should be concise and pithy, documenting your research. Typical reports might be 8-25 pages long, but there is no specific length requirement. Shorter is better unless you have substance that requires more length. Typical sections might be: (1) introduction (introduce the topic, say what you are doing, briefly state your conclusion, and briefly defend the conclusion against likely critiques); (2) explanation of your approach in the context of prior literature (this may develop one or more specific testable hypotheses); (3) data and methods; (4) empirical findings; (5) conclusion. For an ongoing research project with a professor, you may not be able to flesh out all of the above parts fully – for example no conclusion may yet be available – but definitely clarify the point of the research, methods you are using, the contribution you are making, and insights regarding potential conclusions or findings.

Write for an audience of economists, people with at least a Bachelor’s and likely a Master’s or Ph.D. in Economics. The best stylistic model is a published research paper in a good Economics journal, such as *Economics Letters*, the *American Economic Review*, or the *Journal of Political Economy*.

References must be included where necessary. Citations must consistently follow a single widely-accepted method: the format of any leading economics journal. Always cite appropriately ideas and information that are not your own, and show with quotation marks those words that are not your own.

References must include mainly journal articles (or books) from research publications of the sort you find by searching EconLit or Scopus or JSTOR articles in journals (not just found by searching the Internet).

If others contributed to your project, attach a 1-2 page description of the contributions that they and you made to the project and report.

January 27 – Introduction and organizational meeting

February 3 – Confirm your topic by today

Before class, submit a one-paragraph summary of the overarching topic or research activities, their relevance to the assignment at hand, and specific questions you hope to address. (Narrow down your topic enough to be doable well before the end of the semester, to be interesting and non-obvious, and to be positioned reasonably in the context of previous research. Some change after this date is okay, and may be necessary if the topic really isn’t chosen well enough yet.) If the list is not ready for today, that’s okay, just please try to set it up soon, and definitely by the time of the Detailed Summary below.) Submit in LMS, in Word or PDF format.

February 17 – Detailed summary of planned research due

Before class, submit a detailed summary. The summary should introduce the topic or project, state specific ideas your paper or project will address, analyze what the existing research literature says about the topic, and describe what specific argument you hope to make. If you are conducting an empirical analysis, you should state clearly the data sources and methodological approach, with particular econometric equations to the extent possible. For existing research literature, you must include references to published articles (and books) found by searching EconLit or Scopus or JSTOR articles in journals (cite and reference articles and books in the format used by *American Economic Review* or another leading economics journal). Include a planned timeline indicating steps involved and when you plan to finish each step. Submit in LMS, in Word or PDF format. This is not a commitment for what you have to do; it is a plan. Your plan will change as the project evolves, and that is okay, but I would like you to detail your plan as much as possible early on in the process, so that you can think about how to keep moving forward on the project and what will or won’t work. I will distribute the detailed summaries, so classmates can discuss each other’s planned projects.

March 3 – Literature review due

This is a draft of relevant literature discussion and references for your project report. Your literature review should document key literature, related directly to your project. This is not a lengthy description of prior related literature. Rather, it should identify the prior work that is most relevant to your specific research, include text that puts your work in the context of prior work, and document any relevant methods that you may need to use to carry out your work. The text can be simple and to the point, as for example in *Economics* Letters articles, based on knowledge of the literature and specifically how it is relevant for your work. Include citations and references in the format used by *American Economic Review* or another leading economics journal. Use at least one of EconLit or Scopus (and probably JSTOR, library catalogs, and Amazon) to identify relevant sources. For RA-type projects that do not require a literature review, I require you to do at least a brief literature review anyway. If you do not really need to do literature review for the project, then do whatever seems most useful to fit with the project, and include text, in-text citations, and references on for example the topic and/or methods used. Submit in LMS, in Word or PDF format.

March 10 – Data overview due

This assignment ensures that you have obtained the data you need for your analysis, so plan ahead to determine the data you need and get it with enough time to write this data overview. In the overview, state the data source(s) and, if you are extracting data from a bigger database, make clear what parts of the data you are using, how those parts were determined, and the reason for that method. Include a summary of the different data tables, the format and linkages of tables, the number of observations, and a description of variables and summary statistics for any variables that you expect you might use. If you want to do more (correlations, graphs, regressions, etc.), feel free. This should provide draft text that describes the data for your project report, and ensure you have the relevant information to start carrying out your data analyses. *If your project does not involve obtaining and using data*, then you may instead turn in a report on some other aspect of your project such as the detailed methods to be used. Focus on something that is an important next step in moving your project forward. Please indicate at the start of the assignment that your project does not involve obtaining and using data, and what you are doing instead for this assignment. Submit in LMS, in Word or PDF format.

April 6 – Full paper due

The full written report is due by this evening, in LMS via a discussion board topic. To submit this draft written report, go to our course’s LMS page, and use the LMS discussion board called “Draft Written Reports and Comments.” Create a new thread with your name (and brief topic if it fits) as the subject, and in your message, attach a Word or PDF file with the draft written report. (PDF is recommended for cross-platform compatibility.)

April 13 – Comments due, from each student, on 2 papers you review

To provide comments on at least two other students’ draft written reports, pick students who have relatively few people signed up so far (if possible) to provide review comments. That way everyone will get comments. To reserve that you will provide comments for someone, reply to their thread with “In progress comments from (your name)”. Then, when you are done writing comments for someone, reply to their thread with “Comments from (your name)” indicated as the subject. Your comments should discuss what you think of each paper and how it can be improved. Comments can be in a Word or PDF file or in the text of your post. Issues to address are:

- Is the paper interesting and useful?
- Does it clearly state a research question or goal?
- Does it answer the question/goal or at least show contributions made so far to help answer it?
- Does it successfully put the work in the context of relevant prior research?
- Does it clearly describe data sources and methods used?
- Does it prove its point(s) convincingly?
- Do econometric and/or analytic methods used (where relevant) seem valid? What improvements can be made?
- Are references cited in a consistent and appropriate manner?
- How can the organization, editing, and writing quality be improved? (Don’t say much on grammar and spelling though.)
- What major critiques of the paper’s main point can you suggest? If possible, suggest ways the author could address these critiques too.

The comments will not be used for grading the draft or final written reports. Please don’t hold back, but say whatever you can to help the author improve their research and writing. It will help them a lot more to be honest about your reactions and about potential fixes, rather than just trying to be nice. I will grade the comments based on the quality of the suggestions to improve the author’s research and writing. Extra credit (up to 3 points on the final course grade) will be available to people who provide a third set of comments, if the comments are particularly substantive and high-quality.

May 3 – Final version of paper due

After submitting the paper on Apr. 6, continue to improve it, in any ways that seem productive including the suggestions provided by the reviews. Your continuing improvements will involve both more research work and editing and rewriting. Submit in LMS, in Word or PDF format.

## Policies

### Attendance Policy:

This is largely not a lecture course, but a doing course. In a classroom, the doing is doing-together: discussion, usually in small groups—we rearrange positions in the room and trade ideas on each other’s work and help each other out. These activities will not work when we have to wear masks, sit six feet apart, and refrain from relocation. In contrast, meeting with each other online is entirely feasible. Therefore, this course will operate online only this Spring.
