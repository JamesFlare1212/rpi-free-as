## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

Welcome! This course explores how to make sense of ethical issues posed by the proliferation of artificial intelligence technologies. In order to do so, we will first discuss the philosophical issues with ethical theories, and how we can be justified in making ethical judgments in particular cases. We will then survey a number of issues, some of which are as follows. How should we approach thinking about ethical obligation generally? How should we think about designing an ethical algorithm? As autonomous cars are most likely the robots to be integrated into our lives at scale, what challenges do they pose for human-robot interaction design, psychology, law, and socio-political systems? Which ethical approach or theory should an automated process be programmed to follow when there’s no legal guidance? How ought we make sense of obligation and responsibility in the context of the decisions of an autonomous AI (designer, user, AI itself)? Trust is an essential component of social human life. But how will integrating AI into our lives require us to understand the novel philosophical and legal issues related to interpersonal trust between people and AI? How can we know if AI has a moral status, and deserves the respect a person deserves? Is it permissible for companies to “teach” their products through interactions with the general public? Are people not nearly concerned enough about the advent of general AI? Are we too worries? How can we think about such uncertain risks generally? What is a reasonable approach to uncertain risks?

## Course Goals

### Goals:

1. Students should develop an explicit rather than tacit understanding of the ethical challenges posed by AI.
2. Students should be able to develop some views about the ethical issues of AI. While some of these problems are very abstract, others present (almost daily) dilemmas and so students ought to leave the course better prepared to think effectively about these problems.
3. Develop the ability to communicate, in both oral and written form, clear and effective argumentation.
4. Distinguish and evaluate different philosophical arguments.
5. Be able to plan and work with other people on abstract and difficult projects.

## Policies

### Attendance Policy:

Attendance is mandatory for students who can reasonably attend our live WebEx sessions. Things that might affect this are substantial time-zone differences (I don’t expect anyone to be waking up at 4am all summer to make our WebEx sessions), internet connectivity and tech issues, and conflicting class scheduling commitments. Baring those kinds of concerns, you should attend our live WebEx meetings. Each unexcused absence beyond the second will result in the subtraction of three percentage points for the final course grade for each day that you are absent from class. If for example, you have a final grade of 90% but have three unexcused absences, then your final grade will be lowered to 87% because of that one unexcused absence beyond the second. Of course, not all absences are unexcused. An absence is excused if (a) you let me know you will be out of class beforehand and we plan accordingly or, if that is not possible, as soon as you can afterward and (b) you are absent for a reason that is either documented or can be documented. I reserve the right to request documentation. For example: Court date, death in the family, etc. “Woke up late,” or “just wasn’t feeling it today,” or no documentation would be an unexcused absence. I am willing to be extremely flexible and reasonable about what counts as unexcused provided you email me ahead of time.

### Additional Academic Integrity Course Policy and Penalty Information:

1\. Collaboration is encouraged for all assignments for this course. As always, your writing must reflect your own thoughts unless you are quoting others.  
2\. Student-teacher relationships are built on trust. For example, students must trust that teachers have made appropriate decisions about the structure and content of the courses they teach, and teachers must trust that the assignments that students turn in are their own. Acts that violate this trust undermine the educational process. The Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities defines various forms of Academic Dishonesty and you should make yourself familiar with these. In this class, all assignments that are turned in for a grade must represent the student’s own work. In cases where help was received, or teamwork was allowed, a notation on the assignment should indicate your collaboration. Submission of any assignment that is in violation of this policy will result in a penalty of failure for the course and a letter sent to the Dean for further action. If you have any question concerning this policy before submitting an assignment, please ask for clarification. It is my duty to protect the students who play by the rules. I will penalize all cases of discovered cheating to the fullest extent I possibly can. Remember, it is easier to catch cheating than most students think.  
3\. Among the requirements for both the student and the instructor in this course is that we do our best to respect the dignity of all persons and ourselves. Words or deeds directed against a person or group where it is obvious that the sole intent is to insult or otherwise do them harm will not be tolerated. In a similar vein, we should do our best to sensitize ourselves to the unfair and derogatory racial, religious, and sexual connotations of various English expressions and eliminate those expressions from casual use. (A guide to gender-fair language can be obtained from the Writing Center and from the Library). Having said this, however, it must also be said that it is crucial to Rensselaer’s mission that there be placed no undue constraint on the free expression of ideas and opinions. Thus, the mere fact that someone considers an expression or act false or offensive is not by itself sufficient to warrant sanction. Our ability to have important conversations presupposes that we have a space where people feel respected and encouraged to exchange ideas, and I will enforce standards of good faith argumentation and discussion to that end. Basically, value, respect, and support each other in our classroom speech. If you tend to use language like, “that’s a pretty dumb idea actually,” expect to be sanctioned by me. Better phrases like, “I see why that’s a tempting idea, but I think it doesn’t work because of this reason” are just superior when it comes to preserving our space for productive conversation. Respecting others doesn’t mean agreeing with them or deflecting with phrases like “I guess you’re entitled to your opinion,” but rather involves an obligation to politely challenge them on ideas that you see as problematic. All of this applies to me as well! In the words of Bill and Ted, let’s be excellent to each other.
