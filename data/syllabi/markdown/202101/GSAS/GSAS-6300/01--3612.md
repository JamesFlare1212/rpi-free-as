## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

This multidisciplinary course provides theoretical and methodological orientation to critical games research, and seeks to address both the broad and pragmatic implications of the question, “What does it mean to do research via games?” Through a hybrid lecture-workshop class format, students will gain experience with methodological research techniques in various disciplines game design draws from--visual arts, humanities and media studies, social science, and computer science--as well as independent research design and proposal writing.

As a core part of the “Critical Game Design” graduate curriculum, students will be introduced to standard research practices in conjunction with critical deconstructions of those research practices. Students will grapple with questions of power, construction of knowledge, positionality, and epistemic justice in research while developing their identity as researchers.

As a “Jam” course, assignments will be largely shaped by individual student interest, but will also feature collaborative planning and skills-sharing. Students will develop games-related research projects that employ diverse methodologies. Ideally, the proposals developed in the class will directly contribute to the student’s independent thesis project.

While the course readings, discussions, and design exercises will be the same, the design of this class also recognizes the different needs and timelines of Master’s students and Ph.D. students. Master’s students will be working closely with their advisors and each other to develop the methodology section of their Master’s projects, while Ph.D. students will be working more speculatively to survey the field of game design research methods, and develop first drafts of a research proposal.

## Course Goals

### Goals:

By the conclusion of this class, students will demonstrate the ability to:

- Distinguish between methodology and methods
- Articulate the methodological formations and practices of several disciplines that contribute to game design and game studies
- Identify a research site (as broadly defined by diverse fields), and derive a research question from that site
- Apply a research methodology to a research question
- Develop research methods, derived from their selected methodology, appropriate to their research question
- Contextualize their research within larger structures of academic institutions, knowledge structures, and power structures
- Articulate their own positions as researchers within disciplines and systems of power

Project Descriptions:

Brownbag Exercise

Due Date: Varies per student, Week 5 through Week 9

Beginning in Week 5, three students each week will present a one-hour brownbag session of their current work. Brown bags are not polished research presentations; rather, they are work-in-progress talks where students present their current research interest, potential research questions, ideal methods, and literature backgrounds. Most importantly, student presenters bring questions and other issues they are currently facing with their project to the class,

Each student in the class will participate in the brownbag activity once during the semester, The students presenting brownbags that week will act as a co-instructor with the faculty instructor of the course for the week of their reading assignment. During their brownbag cycle, the student will:

- Prepare three (3) readings that provide conceptual, empirical, or methodological background for their project. The student presenter will select one of these readings as a required reading for that week’s class, while the other two readings will serve as supplementary material. As such, each brownbag week will have 3 required readings (one from each presenter) and 6 supplemental, non-required readings. The three readings will be posted on the class Discord page the Wednesday before their presentation.
- Present for 15 minutes on their research topic. This presentation will be informal, but should have some visual aids to help guide the class along. The presenter can assume that the other students in the class have read the required reading. Each presentation should highlight:
  - o The background of the project
  - o The potential research question
  - o Some potential methods for exploring that question
- Work with the class instructor to run a 30 minute discussion of their topic/the reading/their project. The goal of this discussion is to generate feedback on their project and to further develop some new research directions and methods for their project. Each student will be given 30 minutes, with 15 minutes of flex/break time between each brownbag session.

Student attendees will be responsible for thoughtfully participating and taking notes during the discussion. Each week, students who did not present during the week’s brownbag, or are not presenting during the following week’s brownbag, will compile a 250 word written feedback document for each of that week’s presenters, alongside with a peer-reviewed source of literature that the student has found that they believe could contribute to the presenter’s project (conceptually, methodologically, empirically). The feedback document should include in it at least a couple of sentences on the major arguments of the literature piece and why the finder thinks it might be helpful. As there are three presenters in a week, each audience member will write 750 words, and find three peer-reviewed sources, each week that they are not presenting or preparing to present. To keep the writing assignment amounts even, Brownbag Week 1 presenters do not have to prepare feedback for the final Brownbag presenter group, so that each student group is only responsible for three cycles of brownbag feedback. These Feedback documents should be posted to the class Discord page by the Sunday night following the brownbag presentation.

Peer Review Exercise

Due Date: Week 12 of class

Students will submit a research document draft (Project Methodologies for Master’s Students, and Research Proposal for Ph.D, Students) in Week 11 of class. Each student will receive three sources of feedback on their proposal: two from other students in the class, and one from the faculty instructor (MS students will swap with MS students, PhD with PhD). Each student in class will be responsible for peer-reviewing two student papers. For each peer review, the student will be responsible for assessing:

- The presence or absence of the major components of the project proposal outlined
- The quality of writing and of ideas in each of those sections
- Intellectual unity and consistency throughout the proposal document
- Appropriateness of the methodology for the research question
- Appropriateness of the methods derived from the methodology
- The writer’s understanding of positionality in research
- Overall scope of the project

Like the brownbag feedback, peer-reviewers should also suggest further readings and other resources that they believe would contribute to the reviewed project. Students should use the Microsoft Word inline editor and comment feature for feedback, and post the files on the Discord server. Reviews should be about 500 words in length each.s

Final Project: The Research Document

Every student will be responsible for submitting a research proposal by the end of class. This research proposal, ideally, will serve as the backbone of the student’s dissertation project proposal and external funding proposals, or will contribute to the student’s analysis of their Master’s Project findings, successes, and failures.

The Research Proposal will be due in two steps: a draft, to be read and reviewed by two classmates and the instructor, and the final proposal, submitted to the instructor. The draft will be due in Week 11, with reviews due back to the student by Week 12. The final proposal will be due the first Friday of finals week, May 7th.

Master’s Students: Master’s Project Methodologies and Findings

Length:

- o Draft: 7 pages, single spaced, 1” margins, 12 point Times font (in accordance with HASS Fellowship guidelines)
- o Final: 10 pages, single spaced

Draft Due Date: Week 11 of class

Final Due Date: May 7th

For the final project, CGD MS Students will work with their advisor to build a methodological framework for their Master’s Project.

1. **Background.** Summarize your field of study and the focus of your dissertation research. Include a description of your background, prior experience, and preparation for this inquiry. Situate your work relative to current and historical research in the field, providing citations for relevant publications and sources.

2. **Research Question.** What is the research question your project allows you to answer?

3. **Methodology**. Detail the methodological procedures you will follow to address your questions. How is this approach appropriate for your topic and consistent with best practices in fields that are contributing to your Critical Game Design practice?

4. **Assessment**. Describe the strengths and limitations of your work. How will you assess your results? Note that assessment standards and practices will vary widely depending upon the nature of your project, the disciplines you are pulling from, and the goals of your advisor.

5. **Project Description, Design Processes, and Results:** Describe the ongoing nature of the project, the steps you took to develop it, and what the material outcomes of your project were (i.e., what did you design or develop?). Include setbacks and solutions, as well as images documenting the design process and final work. Detail your testing results—how did testing go, what kinds of data were you able to generate?

6. **Conclusion and Future Work:** What were your project’s findings? Findings are more than just the “raw” data that you have collected (again—the form that your “data” takes is going to be very different for each of you, depending on your project and your advisory), they are interpretations and projections of that data—what story did you project tell? Finally, describe how your project opens up avenues for future work, either in terms of further questions that you want to ask about your research topic, or about what new questions researchers can ask because of the findings your project generated.

Ph.D Students: Research Proposal

Length:

- o Draft: 7 pages, single spaced, 1” margins, 12 point Times font (in accordance with HASS Fellowship guidelines)
- o Final: 10 pages, single spaced

Draft Due Date: Week 11 of class

Final Due Date: May 7th

Every student will be responsible for submitting a research proposal by the end of class. This research proposal, ideally, will serve as the backbone of the student’s dissertation project proposal and external funding proposals.

The Research Proposal will be due in two steps: a draft, to be read and reviewed by two classmates and the instructor, and the final proposal, submitted to the instructor. The draft will be due in Week 9, with reviews due back to the student by Week 10. The final proposal will be due in Week 16 of class.

The structure of the Research Proposal draft is derived from the HASS Fellowship model, ideally to make application for internal funding easier for the student:

1. **Background.** Summarize your field of study and the focus of your dissertation research. Include a description of your background, prior experience, and preparation for this inquiry. Situate your work relative to current and historical research in the field, providing citations for relevant publications and sources.

2. **Research Questions.** List your research questions; the issues, hypothesis, or problems that you will address

3. **Methodology**. Detail the methodological procedures you will follow to address your questions. How is this approach appropriate for your topic and consistent with best practices in your field?

4. **Timeline.** Provide a timeline for the completion of your research and dissertation, listing deadlines by which you hope to accomplish major goals.

5. **Impact.** Describe the larger cultural significance of your research. How will your work impact critical issues across disciplinary boundaries?

6. **Assessment**. Describe the strengths and limitations of your work. How will you assess your results?

7. **Future Goals.** What are your future research and career goals?

The structure of the final Research Proposal retains the structure of the draft, but expands upon the Background, Methodology, and Impact sections. Students will be expected to incorporate critical and deconstructive insights from course readings and discussions into those sections. In particular, students will more clearly articulate the epistemic and political implications, limitations, strengths, and dangers of their research project, as well as their own personal and structural position in relation to their research. Students must also discuss their plans for mitigating, where possible, some of the larger structural and epistemic limitations and dangers of their project.
