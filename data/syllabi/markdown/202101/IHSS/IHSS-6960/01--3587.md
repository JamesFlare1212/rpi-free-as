## Course Description

This course is graduate version of my undergraduate course, Consumer Cultures. In the past three decades, new scholarship has emerged around consumption that places it alongside industrial production as a legitimate area of scholarly inquiry. That said, consumption surpasses production in its variation and complexity, and the literature remains more fragmented rather than unitary. As such, our course will run as a kind of research seminar, where we will work together as a class to examine and speak across the diverse literatures that occupy this academic arena.

We will begin by laying an analytic foundation for the course by revisiting several of the reference texts from the undergraduate course (in about four weeks’ time). During the remainder of the course, we will focus primarily on historical and ethnographic texts that provide empirical depth and analytic purchase on wide variety of phenomena including those of conspicuous consumption and the construction of social identities; the production and management of happiness and psychological discomfort; and social and subcultural bonds forged through shared practices associated with the acquisition, use, and exchange of objects. As an IHSS seminar, we will also be looking at the intersection between the history of technology and the expansion of consumer culture; the role of consumption, audience, and patronage in the art worlds and its critical engagement with consumer culture; and the role of communication & media in the construction of both varied and dominant cultures of consumption.

The course is also be relevant for those with an interest in sustainability, insofar as it provides insights into the resilience of consumption in capitalist economies, with attention to the alternative values and ethics necessary to accomplish sustainable transitions. The course will be structured as a readings seminar, but will offer opportunities for original archival and ethnographic research, as well as artistic final projects. The class is designed primarily for PhD students, but MS students, including those in electronic arts, are welcome to join us so long as you are prepared to do the work associated with a PhD level graduate seminar.

## Major Assignments

CITI Training

Note: If you intend to conduct **oral interviews** or **ethnographic observation** for your portfolio or final project, you must complete CITI RCR training by February 5th and email me your completion certificate. You must then obtain IRB approval of the proposed research by February 19th, with me or some other faculty member listed as your advisor (with a copy of the application and approval emailed to me; even if you do not intent to publish material based on the data, or the data exists in the public domain (e.g. posted online) but might be subject to expectations of privacy (e.g. Twitter), you must seek an IRB exemption for this human subjects research).

Collective Readings / Viewings List

Building on the initial list that I compile, we’ll be building up a graduate level readings list on consumer cultures and related topics. A section of the list will also be dedicated to listing artistic works in various media, and other material of interest. Contributions may be made to any section within the list, but special focus will be given during class to the upcoming **three weeks** so people may choose alternate reading based on what you find. (You may also add headings for topics that are not on our schedule.)

During each class, we’ll spend some time introducing each other to what we found. I would love for this collective endeavor to “take off,” but work expectations will be calibrated to the value of the exercise, and discussed and reviewed at appropriate intervals.

Readings

As graduate reading seminar please expect to do a significant amount of reading! For those enrolled in the PhD program in STS and C&M, you should plan on reading a book a week (or the equivalent in scholarly articles), plus several additional books or article clusters for book reports.

Those enrolled in the PhD program in the Arts, and those who are in a co-terminal or standalone master’s program should use this seminar to build up your capacity for reading, but more judicious skimming of the assigned texts, along with reading appropriate to your field of study will be acceptable. You should make sure to read enough of the assigned readings to participate fully in our seminar conversations, if only to ask questions that will help advance others’ understanding.

Alternate readings: Everyone is expected to read (aka carefully skim) or reread the assigned reading with sufficient attention to detail to be able to participate in a discussion of the text. However, if you have already read the text, you should review your knowledge of the text (skimming through it again as appropriate), and then choose an alternate book or set of articles to report on. This will count as ½ of a book report. Others may also choose alternate readings to focus on in addition to carefully skimming the assigned reading as indicated above. (Arts students, in particular, are encouraged to do so to focus on literature about art, art history, and art worlds and subcultures that parallels the assigned text.)

Thought Pieces

You should produce a reflective piece of writing that pulls together what you read during a given week, and relate it, where appropriate, to earlier readings and topics. The main purpose of the reflective writing is to develop your own thoughts about a subject, and to prepare your for discussion during seminar. As such, thought pieces must be completed prior to class. Please be prepared to comment on what you found most interesting about each week’s reading.

Please feel free to email me your thought pieces prior to class, so I can review what you wrote prior to the start of class. (I would appreciate this, but it’s not mandatory.) These need not be polished pieces of writing. However, those of you who are participating in the STS Portfolio Exam are encouraged to write in a manner suitable for the exam.

There is no specific length requirement; quality of ideas matter more than length, and may be exploratory rather than fully developed. (300-400 words might be appropriate for those in the STS & C&M PhD programs; while you may also choose to write to the same extent, 200-300 words will be considered sufficient for those in the Arts PhD program or in a master’s program. Arts PhD students should continue to relate the readings to their own artistic practice, and may occasionally (no more than 1/3 of the time) include such material in lieu of a thought piece.)

“Book” Report

Those enrolled in the STS & C&M PhD programs should read at least two additional books and report on them at appropriate points during the semester. These additional readings can complement the assigned text for a week, or they may be related to your final paper or project and relate only tangentially to the particular week’s topic.

Those in the Arts PhD program and co-terminal and standalone master’s students should find other work, suitable to their interests and preparation, to compile and read in preparation for their final project. You may report on these works in class. This may be an article, artwork with commentary, or critical discussions of a particular artist or collective, etc…

Those enrolled in a PhD program should expect to spend around 5-8 hours searching for, reading, and preparing for each report. Slides are welcome, but not required, except when presenting visual media. Reports are oral, and there is not required written component for the reports (they may, however, be written up as your thought piece for the week).

Portfolio or Catalog

Compile an annotated portfolio or catalog containing one of the following related to a specific phenomenon of interest that relates to cultures of consumption:

- 20-30 historical or archival documents
- 4-6 semi-structured oral interviews of a relevant group of subjects
- 10-15 hours of ethnographic observation, conducted across at least five (5) different locations and/or sessions. (May be in person or online.)
- A portfolio or catalog of artistic works, catalogs, and other material of interest. The body of work should be comparable in content and scope to the other options, and appropriately organized if the content is diverse. (For a list of major digital art collections, try this link.)

In general, I would recommend choosing one of these options rather than combining them, but will accept hybrid portfolios if it makes sense for you to pursue such an approach. We will discuss the detailed requirements of this assignment in class. Each annotation should be at least 3 sentences in length, and may be as long as a full paragraph if this is useful to you.

Final Paper or Project

For PhD students, the final project for the course may be one of the following:

- **A critical or interpretive review essay** (2000-3000 words) that draws together relevant literature on a specific topic or phenomenon related to cultures of consumption. The essay should develop or explore a specific argument or set of arguments, and must be more than a summary of the relevant works. Engagement with assigned readings that are broader in nature, and relevant to the topic or phenomenon is expected. Please take a look at review essays in the relevant fields for appropriate models.
- **An original analysis of historical sources or ethnographic data, with appropriate analytic introduction and framing** (2000-3000 words). Instead of a review essay, write a paper that provides your preliminary analysis of the archival documents, ethnographic observations, or cultural, artistic, and material artifacts that you collected this semester. The essay must be grounded in the assigned texts, or else any additional literatures you find and read.
- **An artistic work, with a written, scholarly introduction.** For those in the Arts PhD program (including EARTS), you may choose to produce a critical or interpretive artistic object, or sketches, drawings, and other material in preparation for a finished artistic work as your final project. The work must be introduced using a scholarly tradition and style appropriate to your field of study (at least 900 words). It must, however, include robust citations to assigned texts and other literatures as appropriate to your chosen subject. The final presentation will also be a significant (33-1/3%) component of the final project grade for those working on artistic projects.

Master’s students may scale back the final paper to 1500-2000 words for the first two options. For the final projects, I do expect a polished piece of writing, different from your weekly thought pieces. I will not be asking for a draft of your final paper, so please self-organize as a class to share ideas, outlines, and your first and final draft with one another. There will be a final presentation of your project to the class, with date TBD.

### Additional Academic Integrity Course Policy and Penalty Information:

**Statement on Academic Integrity:** Especially at the graduate level, students are expected to be entirely familiar with the general norms of academic conduct. Specific RPI policies regarding academic honesty may be found in the *Rensselaer Student Handbook,* with some additional information contained in the Graduate Supplement. As required by our institution, I have to stipulate a range of penalties for plagiarism and other instances of academic dishonesty. Depending on my reading of your intention, understanding of academic practice, and the severity of the case, students may receive anything from a request to revise and resubmit an assignment, to permanent failure of the assignment. In cases of willful and substantial acts of academic dishonesty, including plagiarism, students may also receive anywhere from a one letter grade penalty for the course to failure of the course and a recommendation regarding further action. There is a formal appeals process for grades, as described in the student handbook.
