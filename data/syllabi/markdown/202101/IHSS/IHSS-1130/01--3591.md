## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

This course explores the philosophical and ethical implications of technological developments that promise to shape -- and perhaps to jeopardize -- human life and society in the 21st century and beyond. Artificial Intelligence. Robots as social companions. Robots in military and medical settings. Genetic modifications and technological implants for superhuman abilities. Students will improve their insight as well as critical reasoning skills as we examine, analyze, and evaluate such controversial topics through the lens of philosophical reasoning.Throughout the course we will ask what sort of beings do we want to be and what sort of society do we want to have. This is a communication intensive course.

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes Assessment Measures:

**Course Learning Outcomes**  
1\. Students will be able to explain and communicate the essential characteristics of Philosophy and the uses and value of Philosophy.  
2\. Students will be able to explain the concept of existential risk, and will be able to identify several proposals for mitigating portions of the total risk to humanity.  
3\. Students will gain ability to explain different kinds and degrees of human enhancement, to identify relevant ethical and social issues, and to discuss pros and cons of different types and degrees of enhancement.  
4\. Students will be able to identify current philosophical issues, themes, and ethical concerns in the field of artificial intelligence.  
5\. Students will be able to identify and discuss ethical issues concerning the current and future use of robots in such areas as warfare, health care, and social companionship.  
**Course Assessment Measures**  
PAPER 1: Assesses Learning Outcomes 1, 2, 3  
MIDTERM EXAM: Assesses Learning Outcomes 1, 2, 3  
PAPER 2: Assesses Learning Outcomes 3, 4

## Course Calendar

### Calendar Details:

A day-by-day calendar of topics and readings was provided to you in a separate document, but the following is a condensed version of the course calendar:  
Week 1: Overview of course. Introduction to arguments. Subfields of Philosophy.  
Week 2: Start book *The Precipice* by Toby Ord. Humanity's future potential if we survive. Concept of "Existential Risk." Natural existential risks, such as asteroids and super-volcanoes.  
Week 3: Anthropogenic existential risks, such as nuclear weapons, climate change, and severe environmental damage. Future risks, such as pandemics and A.I. with goals not aligned with ours. Dystopian scenarios.  
Week 4: Combining and comparing risks. International coordination to minimize risks. Research on existential risk.  
Week 5: Start book *Better than Human* by Allen Buchanan. Adamant views for and against biomedical enhancement. Why unguided evolution is not in fact the embodiment of wisdom. Are human nature and the human good the same or different?  
Week 6: Concerns that the rich will get biologically richer. Focusing on unjust inequalities. Why governments will care about enhancements. Is enhancement corrupting? PAPER 1 DUE.  
Week 7: Appreciating what we already have. Would enhancements amount to treating ourselves as objects? Should we seek moral enhancements? REVIEW FOR MIDTERM.  
Week 8: MIDTERM EXAM. Start book *Life 3.0* by Max Tegmark. Brief history of complexity. Beneficial A.I. movement. Misconceptions and the road ahead.  
Week 9: What is intelligence? What is learning? Natural language and A.I. A.I. use in various sectors of society.  
Week 10: A.I. and the future of employment. Achieving human-level A.I. How an A.I. could take over the world. What might the next 10,000 years hold?  
Week 11: Prospects for egalitarian utopia. A.I. as a protector god? Start book *Moral Machines* by Wallach & Allen. Good and bad artificial agents. Morality and military robots. PAPER 2 DUE.  
Week 12: Delegating responsibility for decisions to machines. Could a robot ever be a real moral agent? Design of autonomous moral agents. Top-down and bottom-up approaches. Rules for robots.  
Week 13: Bottom-up developmental approaches. Artificial life. Merging top-down and bottom-up approaches. Logicist A.I. Emotional intelligence and moral decision making.  
Week 14: Affective computing. Challenges of multi-agent environments. Moral decision making involving rules. REVIEW FOR FINAL EXAM.

- ### Academic Integrity

  Student-teacher relationships are built on trust. For example, students must trust that teachers have made appropriate decisions about the structure and content of the courses they teach, and teachers must trust that the assignments that students turn in are their own. Acts that violate this trust undermine the educational process. The Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities and The Graduate Student Supplement define various forms of Academic Dishonesty and you should make yourself familiar with these. In this class, all assignments that are turned in for a grade must represent the student’s own work. In cases where help was received, or teamwork was allowed, a notation on the assignment should indicate your collaboration.

  Violations of academic integrity may also be reported to the appropriate Dean (Dean of Students for undergraduate students or the Dean of Graduate Education for graduate students, respectively).

  If you have any question concerning this policy before submitting an assignment, please ask for clarification.
