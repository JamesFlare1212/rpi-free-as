Responses to Media/Resources

You will respond from a mix of assigned media (readings, films, podcasts, etc.) and (increasingly, as the semester progresses) media and resources of your own choosing. Your responses will take a variety of forms, including written and oral responses, with specific prompts provided for many of the resources. You will be encouraged, throughout, to find concepts, principles, and ideas and run with them - i.e. let us know how these media changed your thinking and/or practice.

Participation

You are expected to discuss the readings, watching, listenings, exercises, activities, etc., and are encouraged to bring ideas with you from other classes, things you are reading, experiences beyond school, etc. You will be asked to help contribute to a culture that creates both a safe(r) space (built on a foundation of respect) and encourages students to stretch a bit beyond their comfort zones (answering a question if you’re not entirely sure what to say, for example). You will also give regular updates on your design projects. Your participation in this class should take into account the guidelines for developing a thriving class culture, collaboratively developed starting on the first day of class, and iterated upon in our #designing-culture Discord channel. If you have any questions about any of these guidelines, please be in touch!

Project Proposal Iterations

Your initial project proposal will describe the following aspects of your anticipated design process: values, context, goals, analysis & assessment, design, implementation, evaluation, and reflection and integration. You will also include a proposed timeline. A second iteration of this proposal will provide a general update and respond to a few additional guiding questions designed to provide an opportunity to reflect on your design process thus far.

Design journal and reflections on practice

This grade is really about what you do, the design work you’re journaling about. Once we get rolling with your projects, at minimum, you should have a weekly journal entry reflecting on your progress, the design process, and any challenges that arise. You’ll be asked to turn in reflections based on specific prompts or drawing on the insights that you develop in the process of journaling (you will not be asked to turn in your journal itself, this is for you).

Prototypes

You will go out and construct something (a product, a garden, an organization, a database, new connections, etc.). A key part of the iterative design process we’ll work with this semester involves crafting prototypes and getting feedback on these from relevant stakeholders and incorporating this feedback into future prototypes. Not all projects will have physical prototypes; some will prototype experiences, processes, or systems. Please be in touch (with us, with your peers, and with an “advisory board” that you’ll assemble to inform your project) about this and all components of this class if any questions come up!

Presentations

You will give periodic presentations on your progress with the various elements of the design process, applied both to yourself and to your project work. These presentations will typically include graphical or other media content, should be highly engaging, and will provide important opportunities for peer-review and feedback. You also may be asked to prepare presentations, exercises, workshops, or other modes of engagement with the readings and other resources we work with.

Final Design Report

Besides your prototypes, you will produce a report that ties all your work together. This should be the equivalent (as far as effort, breadth, depth) of a 10 page (double-spaced) paper, with minimum 10 solid references, but can actually take other forms (perhaps a podcast series, a radio series on WOOC or WRPI, a film, or other creative ways of expressing your research and design). All projects will include at least some written component that reflects and analyzes the various forms of design work employed.

## Policies

### Academic Policies:

- ### Academic Integrity

  Student-teacher relationships are built on trust. For example, students must trust that teachers have made appropriate decisions about the structure and content of the courses they teach, and teachers must trust that the assignments that students turn in are their own. Acts that violate this trust undermine the educational process. The Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities and The Graduate Student Supplement define various forms of Academic Dishonesty and you should make yourself familiar with these. In this class, all assignments that are turned in for a grade must represent the student’s own work. In cases where help was received, or teamwork was allowed, a notation on the assignment should indicate your collaboration.

  Violations of academic integrity may also be reported to the appropriate Dean (Dean of Students for undergraduate students or the Dean of Graduate Education for graduate students, respectively).

  If you have any question concerning this policy before submitting an assignment, please ask for clarification. In addition, you can visit the following site for more information on our Academic Integrity Policy: [Students Rights, Responsibilities, and Judicial Affairs](https://info.rpi.edu/dean-students/student-rights-responsibilities-and-judicial-affairs).

### Additional Academic Integrity Course Policy and Penalty Information:

For this course, the following penalties will apply:

- Significant acts of plagiarism (e.g., text copied verbatim from an unidentified source): Failure of the course and a written judgment in student’s official record.
- Minor acts of plagiarism (e.g., referencing the findings of others without appropriate citations): Failure of the assignment.
- Other acts of academic dishonesty: Penalties range from a warning to reduction of final grade by one letter grade to failure of the course, depending on the severity of the violation as determined by the instructor.

As is evident above, penalties for plagiarism are significant. All direct use of another person’s words must be placed inside quotation marks. You must also indicate where you paraphrase another’s work and where you borrow another’s specific ideas or interpretations. If you have questions regarding proper citation practices, see the instructor for clarification before the assignment is submitted.

While collaboration is strongly encouraged throughout the course, others cannot do work for you. All assignment activities must be carried out by the individual or team members submitting the assignment for a grade. Other people may show you how to do something (say, when using computer software), but you must follow up by doing the work yourself.

The Rensselaer Handbook provides specific procedures by which a student may appeal a grade. You should speak to the professor before initiating an appeal. If this does not lead to satisfactory resolution, you have the option of appealing your grade by writing to the head of the STS Department no later than 10 days after your grade has been posted.
