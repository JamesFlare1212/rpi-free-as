## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

This course teaches basic historical, anthropological, and sociological concepts for making sense of how psychoactive drugs have been used in historical and contemporary societies. We analyze how licit and illicit drugs serve as “technologies” in specific settings and subcultures; how these technologies are produced, distributed, and consumed; what meanings are attached to them and how these change over time; how drug policy governs them; what these technologies and their use tell us about society and culture in different times and places; and impacts of biomedical knowledge on drug users, including persons with addictions.

We focus on representation of drug use and drug users in popular culture, science, medicine, and social science, tracing how public and private values are translated into drug law and policy. We study how social and cultural change affects specific communities of drug users. You will learn how scientific knowledge generated about drug addiction affects policymaking, and how evidence-based policy works within this arena. Policy is defined as state management of populations defined in response to social problems.

As an upper-level interdisciplinary humanities and social sciences course, this course stimulates critical thinking by making ethical and political relations visible in public policy texts, court cases, the policymaking process, and social outcomes. Although we center U.S. drug policy because it has greatly influenced regulation of the global drug trade, we discuss the “new drug history” emerging all over the world. Meanings attributed to drug use vary historically and culturally; comparative examples abound and each student is asked to present a comparative case in addition to a US-based presentation as a way to build awareness of drugs into understandings of global citizenship. As a Communication Intensive course, students will receive and give feedback that helps them develop reading, writing, discussion, and presentation skills.

**Monday, January 25**

We define “drugs” in multiple contexts in which they have been produced, distributed, and used throughout history. Gather your course materials and complete on-line reading for next class. **For today’s class, please read:** Jonah Engel Bromwich, **“This election, America stands united on one topic,”** https://www.nytimes.com/2020/11/05/style/marijuana-legalization-usa.html and come to class prepared to discuss it.

**Thursday,** **January 28**

Drug policy debates often revolve around media “panics” that mark boundary conflicts between social groups—some constructed as “normal,” and others labeled “deviant.” Lines are drawn between “legal” and “illegal” substances, behaviors, and activities. Howard S. Becker likens ‘drugs’ to pharmacological ‘weeds’ in which boundaries between ‘weeds’ and ‘not weeds’ are ‘socially constructed.’ This process is studied by Science and Technology Studies (STS), an interdisciplinary field combining history, sociology, and anthropology of science and technology. Sociologist Craig Reinarman considers what drug ‘scares’ (or ‘moral panics’) have to do with social order and disorder, and how various interest groups **frame** drugs in society. **Frames** are basic to this course: please attend to current examples of **how drugs and drug scares are framed.** We will do an in-class exercise using them. . . . an activity that will be as exciting and interesting as **your** examples.

**Reading required for today’s class:**

1. Howard Becker, “Drugs: What Are They?” LMS
2. “The Social Construction of Drug Scares,” *Craig Reinarman,* LMS

## Learning Outcomes

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes (CLOs):

- \- Ability to think critically about and discuss—in oral and written form—contemporary social problems related to drugs and drug policy, recognizing the matrix of factors (economic, technical, biophysical, cultural, etc.) that frame problems and constrain solutions.
- \- Ability to respond to complex, content-related questions concerning historical events in the regulation and control of legal and illegal drugs
- \- Ability to analyze and present findings on particular historical, cultural, and social contexts
- \- Specific knowledge concerning events that led to the current regulatory regime governing global drug production, distribution, and consumption.

Drug Subcultures Research Paper (25% + 10% abstract and bibliography) 10-12 pages

Please turn in a 300-word abstract about what drug subculture you intend to cover and annotations complete with full bibliographic citation of 5-7 sources (academic articles, books, nonacademic sources, etc.) that you plan to use for your drug subcultures paper. This is due on March 18, 2021. The full paper is due on April 15, 2021.

Be resourceful—use Google Scholar, websites like erowid, the Drug Policy Alliance, and journals such as *International Journal of Drug Policy, Social History of Alcohol and Drugs, Contemporary Drug Problems,* etcetera. Everything has antecedents and with a topic such as drugs, interdisciplinary search strategies will be necessary. There are vast literatures on drugs: anthropology, history, sociology, political science, law and policy, pharmacology, medicine, and social work.

Avoid choosing common, ubiquitous substances for your drug subcultures paper (i.e. alcohol, coffee, tobacco). Writing should total 10-12 pages + final bibliography, typed, double-spaced, 12-point Times New Roman font, formatted with 1-inch margins, with all proper citations (use Chicago style unless you have another preference) listed in an annotated bibliography. Although you may link your drug subcultures project to your presentation, implosion paper, or other assignments, you may not “recycle” text among these assignments.

For example, you might examine how cannabis/marijuana is used among terminally ill patients or sufferers of Tourette’s syndrome for your drug subcultures project, look at a specific artifact for your implosion project, study “medical marijuana” initiatives in particular states for your US drug policy project, and then look at cannabis use in India, Uruguay, or Denmark for your global drug policy paper. The important part of this assignment is that it should involve significant shaping from *you*, the author. You are not assigned a topic—you need to generate one about which you are curious, concerned, or compelled to comprehend.
