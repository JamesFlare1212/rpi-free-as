## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

The theory of computation currently stands as the most promising candidate for understanding the incredible abilities of the human mind. In parallel with the development of cognitive science, recent advances in artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML) have brought about new computational theories of learning. This course will explore the growing convergence between computational theories of human and machine learning. In particular, the course will focus on major theoretical frameworks including deep learning, Bayesian inference, information theory, and reinforcement learning, utilizing recent journal articles from both machine learning and cognitive science literatures. These topics will be critically evaluated from the perspective of how principled computational theories can be instantiated in cognitive systems. Both successes, and limitations, of current computational theories will be considered. This course is a combined undergraduate / graduate seminar, and **all students are expected to participate actively in class discussions.**

## Learning Outcomes

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes (CLOs):

- Students who complete this course will: 1) Acquire a deeper understanding of the human mind as a computational system, and how it relates to state-of-the-art approaches in machine learning
- 2\) Gain proficiency in reading and critically evaluating peer-reviewed scientific literature
- 3\) Demonstrate the ability to engage in critical discussion related to scientific theories
- 4\) Develop skills for presenting complex topics in an accessible manner

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes Assessment Measures:

| **Assessment** | **Due date(s)** | **Learning outcomes** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Written summary / discussion points | Due 24 hours before each class | 1,2 |
| Class presentations | Assigned individually | 1–4 |
| Discussion participation | Weekly | 1–3 |

- **Written summary / discussion points:** Students will be required to read all assigned materials before each class. To ensure that students are keeping up, students will also complete a short writing assignment *for each assigned reading*. The writing assignments will utilize the following template:
- — Brief summary of the reading (~1 paragraph)
- — A bullet list of at least three discussion points/questions. These should demonstrate the ability to critically evaluate the reading. For example, students might identify strengths/weaknesses of a proposed theory, or suggest how the assigned reading relates to other topics that have been discussed in this class or elsewhere. These discussion points will serve as a scaffold for in-class discussions
- — (Optional) a bullet list of clarification questions or confusions about the reading

The writing assignments are due at least 24 hours in advance of the class meeting.

**Note:** Students do not need to submit writing assignments on weeks that they are presenting. They are still expected to participate in discussion of all assigned readings.

- **Class presentations:** Each reading will have one student assigned to prepare a presentation (lasting ~30–45 minutes, not counting discussion time) that summarizes the reading. The presentation should serve as a mini-tutorial on the topic of the reading, illustrate the main points or results described, and potentially describe cited or related work. Each presentation will be followed by a critical discussion of the paper by the class. The presenting student will also serve as a discussion leader/facilitator for the discussion and will given access to the submitted weekly writing assignments. Depending on the number of students enrolled, each student can expect to prepare 1–4 presentations over the duration of the course. Students who are presenting on the same week on related topics are encouraged to coordinate their presentations. Additional details will be provided in the handout, “How to Present a Paper”.
- **Discussion participation:** This course follows a “seminar” format. This means that students learn not by passively listening to lectures delivered by the instructor, but rather by actively discussing and engaging with the course material. This is a more challenging format than lecture-based courses. Consequently, participation in class discussions will serve as the largest single component of student assessment.
