## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

**Course Description**

Cities are first and foremost social projects which, in turn, produce social products. In this course we are interested in understanding the negotiated construction of architecture, landscapes, and infrastructure, as they emerge from social and political acts, having far-reaching impacts on urban and ecological life.

We are also concerned with becoming conscious of the many ongoing and contested theories of the urban. We acknowledge that there cannot be any single ‘unifying’ urban theory which will encompass the depth of understanding of the subject matter in all contexts and times. Rather, in this course students will be provided with a diverse set of texts and authors from which to position themselves within ongoing debates of what constitutes urban theory and how one may approach studying urban conditions as in practice and in scholarly research.

**Citation Etiquette**

In this course we will exclusively use citations written according to the:

Chicago Manual of Style https://www.chicagomanualofstyle.org/home.html

How to write proper bibliographic citations:

- Always put in quotation any phrase copied from another source. This wording can be from a PDF, book, journal, magazine,etcetera.
- Wikipedia cannot be cited as a reference in academic writing. Wikipedia may be used for fact-checking, in which case it is useful. I have also suggested (on a previous page in this Syllabus) and encourage you to use the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy for fact checking and overall understanding of the authors and theories discussed in this course: Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy https://plato.stanford.edu/
- When using a unique idea that is outlined in another source, even if you are rephrasing this idea in your own words, you should still make footnotes and a citation of the original author. Everyday knowledge, or common knowledge, need not be cited, but if another student or researcher were to pick up your writing, they should be able to ‘get to the source’ of your analysis by reading through your citations and footnotes -- thereby arriving at the sources of your writing on the subject.

## Course Goals

### Goals:

**Goals + Objectives**

Consciously or unconsciously, when humans have established settlements of any scale, those interventions have always had an intimate connection to and impact upon the geography and environments in which they occur. One goal of the course is to develop an understanding of the works of architecture and urbanism as historically-produced embodiments of collective hopes, desires, and anxieties, as much as the unique visions of individual authors. Another goal is to consider how the history of these ideas continue to bear on the globalized context of contemporary urban discourse. Themes we will address include the relationship between ideal visions & material realities; between architectural form & social change; the role of technologies, modes of production, and cultural movements in the production of urban space. This course will ask students to question the roles and responsibilities of Architects, Urbanists, and Landscape Architects in response to constructing in a variety of environmental and social conditions in highly diverse urban contexts.

## Course Content

### Content Details:

**Content**

This course will cover:

1\. Vocabulary for understanding urban and landscape theories and practical knowledge for application in the field.

2\. Historical trajectories of urbanism, including comparative studies of similarity and difference, and case studies of specific urban conditions in history; as well as the influence of the design professions within these urban morphologies.

3 .Contemporary urban design strategies for architects to consider when dealing with 21st century urban scenarios, especially those involving landscapes, urbanism, social justice, and the environment.

## Learning Outcomes

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes (CLOs):

- Outcome 1 URBAN & LANDSCAPE DISCOURSE: students will be able to identify and analyze elements of urban and landscape design, and to critique their discourses, and critically compare and contrast their methods and strategies in design. (PC1, PC4)
- Outcome 2 HISTORY AND THEORY OF URBAN FORM: students will be able to identify and analyze the effects of socio-economic, cultural, political, ecological, and technological factors in shaping urban form. (PC4)
- Outcome 3 SOCIAL EQUITY & INCLUSION : Students will be able to identify and discuss, in their own words, an understanding of the role and responsibility of architects in ensuring equity and access to sites, buildings, and structures within urban and rural contexts. (PC4, PC8)

(PC1, PC4, PC8)

### Additional Academic Integrity Course Policy and Penalty Information:

**Academic Integrity**

The Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities defines various forms of Academic Dishonesty and procedures for responding to them. Academic dishonesty violates the trust between students and teachers. Students must trust that teachers have made appropriate decisions about the structure and content of the courses they teach. Teachers must trust that the assignments students turn in are their own performance. Violations of this trust undermines the educational process. Cheating, plagiarism (defined as the lack of attribution or citation of the work of others), willful damage or theft of another person’s work violate academic integrity. The first instance of the breach of this trust will merit a failing grade in this course and the violator(s) will be reported to the Dean of Students for disciplinary action.
