## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

In ecology, resilience is the capacity of an ecosystem to respond to a perturbation or disturbance by resisting damage and recovering quickly; while organizational resilience is defined as "the ability of a system to withstand changes in its environment and still function. It is a capability that involves organizations either being able to endure the environmental changes without having to permanently adapt, or the organization is forced to adapt a new way of working that better suits the new environmental conditions. From Wikipedia The world’s population is projected to increase by approximately one billion people over the next 10 years, reaching 8.6 billion in 2030, and to increase further to 9.8 billion by 2050 and 11.2 billion by 2100. (2018 revision report, United Nations). For the United States, according to the U.S. Census Bureau, United Nations, Pew Research (pewresearch.org) and other sources, the U.S. will grow to 438 million people by 2050 – up 35% from the 2017 population of 326 million. And upwards of 75% of future Americans will live in cities and urbanized areas (up from 50% in 2008). In other words, by 2050 there will the same number of Americans living in cities as there are in the entire nation today! When considering the built environment as a whole - Material Extraction, Manufacturing, Transportation, Construction, Active life, Maintenance and Disposal - the building industry has the largest carbon footprint of any other human activity on the planet. According to the U.S. Energy Information Administration (EIA), the building sector consumes nearly half (47.6%) of all energy produced in the United States. It was responsible for nearly half (44.6%) of U.S. CO2 emissions in 2010. By comparison, transportation accounted for 34.3% of CO2 emissions and industry just 21.1%. More importantly, at a global scale, according to a 2018 study by the United Nations, more people live in cities, with 55% of the world’s population residing in urban areas. In 1950, 30% of the world’s population was urban, and by 2050, 68% of the world’s population is projected to be urban. If we consider that continuing population growth and urbanization are projected to add 2.5 billion people to the world’s urban population by 2050 and 3.6 billion by 2100, what are strategies that will allow the future growth of the urban built environment on the planet to become more resilient? If we assume the same urban distribution by 2100 (68%) globally, this would mean that from 4.2 billion people that live in cities today, we will have 7.6 billion living in cities by 2100. This for us would mean - with a conservative estimate - that by 2100 (this is in the next 80 years) we would have to almost double the amount of cities on the planet. - What are alternatives to the current building practices that will make the building industry cope with the challenges of the 21st century? o Where will all the material for such a huge construction project come from? o How are we going to provide power for double the amount of building infrastructure? o What does the overall population growth mean about the quality of living in the future cities? o How can we use information as infrastructure for the future city in order to cope with the complexities arising from such intense city densification? These are some fundamental questions that we are trying to address through design and research in this studio. In the age of the Anthropocene, it is more relevant than ever before to consider any human activity in a larger context of production and consumption (processing) of MATERIAL, ENERGY and INFORMATION at scales far greater than those of the immediate object of inquiry. In this studio we will use the following as a definition: the built environment occurs as the “effective” distribution of MATERIAL, ENERGY AND INFORMATION in space for socio-natural affect. (This definition paraphrases a prior definition by Mark Goulthorpe “Architecture as distribution of material is space for cultural affect” to include Sanford Kwinter’s often communicated understanding of space as Material, Energy and Information) This Studio is the first of a two-part studio that will extend throughout the year.

## Course Goals

### Goals:

During this semester the goal of the studio is twofold: 1. You will identify and quantify a problem or opportunity that permeates the built environment and understand it as a holistic system of inputs and outputs, products and by products at all scales and all levels. Furthermore, you will propose a more preferable alternative as an intervention to the current condition (system) and identify the steps required to get to it. 2. You will begin a PILOT design project that synthesizes the analytical work done to a comprehensive research project. Instead of responding to a brief, you will develop a design research project whose program,method, systems, mass and/or performance as an intervention to condition you have identified in your systems diagram. Your goal is to interrogate through the lenses of Material, Energy and Information, alternatives to the current practices for the built environment.

## Learning Outcomes

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes (CLOs):

- Students will learn how to develop a design proposal for a specific problem of opportunity for the built environment at a scale of their choosing that incorporates a complex interrelationship of social economic and technological issues.
- Students will learn how to integrate technical/scientific research to the building and thinking of the built environment.
- Will acquire skills of graphic and oral communication that interrelate knowledge from different disciplines within a coherent presentation.
- Through diagramming, oran presntations, and drawings students learn how: 1. One scale of the built Environment informs/impacts another. 2. Criteria established by considerations of environmental and infrastructural performance inform and affect morphology, as well as the design of material energy and information systems in the buit environment. 3. will learn how to integrate disciplinary knowledge from other disciplines that early into the research - design process.

### Additional Academic Integrity Course Policy and Penalty Information:

The Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities defines various forms of Academic Dishonesty and procedures for responding to them. Academic dishonesty violates the trust between students and teachers. Students must trust that teachers have made appropriate decisions about the structure and content of the courses they teach. Teachers must trust that the assignments students turn in are their own performance. Violations of this trust undermines the educational process. Cheating, plagiarism (defined as the lack of attribution or citation of the work of others), willful damage or theft of another person’s work violate academic integrity. The first instance of the breach of this trust will merit a failing grade in this course and the violator(s) will be reported to the Dean of Students for disciplinary action. The definitions and examples presented below are samples of types of academic dishonesty and are not to be construed as an exhaustive or exclusive list. The academic dishonesty policy applies to all students, undergraduate and graduate, and to scholarly pursuits and research. Additionally, attempts to commit academic dishonesty or to assist in the commission or attempt of such an act are also violations of this policy. • Academic Fraud: The alteration of documentation relating to the grading process. For example, changing exam solutions to negotiate for a higher grade or tampering with an instructor’s grade book. • Collaboration: Deliberate facilitation of academic dishonesty in any form. For example, allowing another student to observe an exam paper or allowing another student to “recycle” one’s old term paper or using one another’s work in a paper or lab report without citing it as another’s work. • Copying: Obtaining information pertaining to a graded exercise by deliberately observing the paper of another student. For example, noting which alternative a neighboring student has circled on a multiplechoice exam. Cribbing: Use or attempted use of prohibited materials, information, or study aids in an academic exercise. For example, using an unauthorized formal sheet during an exam. • Fabrication: Unauthorized falsification or invention of any information in an academic exercise. For example, use of “bought” or “ready-made” term papers, or falsifying lab records or reports. • Plagiarism: Representing the work or words of another as one’s own through the omission of acknowledgment or reference. For example, using sentences verbatim from a published source in a term paper without appropriate referencing, or presenting as one’s own the detailed argument of a published source, or presenting as one’s own electronically or digitally enhanced graphic representations from any form of media. • Sabotage: Destruction of another student’s work. For example, destroying a model, lab experiment, computer program, or term paper developed by another student. • Substitution: Utilizing a proxy, or acting as a proxy, in any academic exercise. For example, taking an exam for another student or having a homework assignment done by someone else.
