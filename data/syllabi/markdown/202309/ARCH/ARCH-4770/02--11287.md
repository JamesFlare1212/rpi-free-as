## Course Goals

### Goals:

**THE RPI-RISSE DESIGN STUDIO**

This Vertical Studio will explore the historically undervalued field of adaptive reuse in relation to architecture’s capacity to engage social concerns associated with various forms of displacement. The course’s inquiry into displacement is conceptually framed by the intersection of two contemporary societal changes – an increase in global migration and a decline in organized religion in the US. The architectural implications of these dynamics – the need for basic shelter/services for people displaced from their home(land), on the one hand, and houses of worship that have lost their original use, on the other - will be explored through the reimagination of a former church structure and grounds as a transitional sanctuary for those involved in the process of resettlement.

Architecture, here, is seen as a social construction and part of material culture. Aligned with contemporary “broken world thinking”, the studio will explore design as a form of repair, reconfiguration, and renewal. It will cultivate a kind of architectural imagination that productively synthesizes two seemingly different design processes: working with existing things + working with change. If the former is often associated in architecture with the inert and fixed, then the latter is affiliated with the indeterminate and emergent. Taken together, this pairing presents a conceptual paradox – buildings as having aspects of both physical permanence and active immateriality. As such, this studio will engage a fabric of concrete material and spatial relationships, while situating this activity within the more abstract framework of time. Adaptive reuse is seen here through the wider lens of adaptation writ large. Thus, we will seek alternatives to the binary conceptual framework of “old” and “new” that form the basis of the traditional playbook of adaptive reuse practices. We will use design techniques that take as their starting point the leftovers, aftermaths, and side-effects of displacement. Such design activity will consider how small, but strategic interventions can have catalytic effects that extend beyond the realm of use (and re-use), unfold over time, and engender new forms of flexibility, resiliency, connectivity, and collectivity. This work will endeavor to extend the lives of buildings and help improve the quality of life for those who encounter them.  
The design studio’s project will focus on the adaptive reuse of a multi-building complex in Albany, NY with two interrelated scopes of work: a comprehensive framework/strategic plan for the whole site complex and series of discrete, phased design interventions. It will consider what impactful/transformative change looks like when designing with modest means for a vulnerable population. This work will explore issues of displacement as they are associated with a historically significant building and a new program that supports an emerging community seeking basic human needs and rights as its members find their place in a new world.

## Learning Outcomes

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes (CLOs):

- OUTCOME 1: Research 1a. abilty to use / develop specialized knowledge - Students will conduct and implement discipline-specific research on the field of adaptive reuse (principles and practices), working with a historic structure (preservation standards/guidelines), and circular building construction methods (deconstruction and salvage technologies).
- OUTCOME 1: Research 1b. abilty to use / develop broad and integrative knowledge - Students will conduct and implement interdisciplinary research on the global migration crisis and the local refugee/immigrant experience in the Capitol Region, with the end-goal of advancing architectural design solutions that address complex societal concerns and challenges.
- OUTCOME 2 Intellectual Design Skills 2a. ability to design - Students will learn to differentiate between, and demonstrate the ability to design for, different types of architectural change (use, configuration, material, iconography, etc.), different durations/frequencies of change (temporary/permenant, continuous/incremental, etc.), and various forms of displacement (displaced peoples, uses, building components, etc.)
- OUTCOME 2 Intellectual Design Skills 2b. ability to design using information resources - Students will learn to identify and analyze existing site conditions through the use of professional-level base documentation and demonstrate the ability to use this technical information in the design of strategic interventions (alterations/additions) into an existing built fabric of a multi-building complex with precision, clarity, and impact.
- OUTCOME 2 Intellectual Design Skills 2c. ability to design engaging diverse perspectives - Students will learn to identify the diverse perspectives of project stakeholders and demonstrate the ability to integrate alternative worldviews and lived experiences into a design project: the conversion of a former church structure (originally purpose-built for a single religious congregation) into a multi-purpose, non-denominational facility to be used by a multi-cultural, global community of people of different nationalities, ethnicities, and religious affiliations.
- OUTCOME 2 Intellectual Design Skills 2d. ability to design with ethical reasoning - Students will learn to make informed value judgements regarding the appropriate allocation of limited resources (material, spatial, ecological, financial, etc.) for a partner organization using modest means to support a community of people in need. Students will demonstrate the ability to articulate the ethical dilemmas inherent in the choices of where, when, and for whom these resources will be distributed and how they are integrated into a project design.
- OUTCOME 2 Intellectual Design Skills 2e. ability to design with quantitative fluency - Students will learn to evaluate a "real word" program, translate a qualitative client "wish list" into a quantitative program brief, and propose modifications/amendments to the brief. Students will demonstrate the ability to interpret and synthesize different types of information (written/numerical/visual), establish and calculate numerical areas/space allocations, analyze multiple "test-fit" scenarios, and determine the project's scope of work, including the types, amounts, and extents of the design changes to be made.
- OUTCOME 2 Intellectual Design Skills 2f. ability to design with communicative fluency - Students will learn to differentiate between different constituencies/audiences (architectural, client, and general public) and demonstrate the ability to articulate a design problem, problem-solving methods, and solution in these different contexts.
- OUTCOME 3 Collaboration 3a ability to collaborate with team - Students will demonstrate the ability to work effectively in 2-3 person teams using collaborative methods and interpersonal skills. Students will demonstrate the ability to develop a strategy for group work (knowledge/info sharing, ideation, workflow, project management, etc.) that recognizes different group member’s strengths, documents the strategy so that others may understand it, implements the strategy, communicates and evaluates the results, and modifies the strategy as needed.
- OUTCOME 3 Collaboration 3b ability to collaborate with a community partner - Students will demonstrate the ability to work effectively with an external partner organization and collaborate with project stakeholders (leadership, staff, clients) through direct and indirect engagement. Students will learn to solicit input + feedback, exchange information + ideas, identify opportunities + challenges, and participate in a sustained dialog that informs the design work.
- OUTCOME 4 Civic and Global Engagement 4a. ability to engage the civic realm - Students will demonstrate the ability to engage issues of public interest/concern, specifically the influx of refugee/immigrants and disappearance of religious congregations in the Capitol Region. Students will develop skills of thoughtful listening, engaged dialogue, and critical thinking to develop designs aimed at making positive change for the common good.
- OUTCOME 4 Civic and Global Engagement 4b. ability to engage the global realm - Students will demonstrate the ability to engage complex/contested public issues associated with global migration and resettlement, understand and appreciate the different backgrounds, worldviews, and lived experiences of a global community, and develop designs that aim to have expansive, transformational impact.
- OUTCOME 5 Design Execution 5a. Midterm Review - Students will demonstrate the ability to implement their learning in Outcomes #1-4 in a clear, complete, and compelling way in a presentation that includes diagrams, mappings, orthographic drawings, axonometrics, and renderings.
- OUTCOME 5 Design Execution 5b. Final Review - Students will demonstrate the ability to implement their learning in Outcomes #1-4 in a clear, complete, and compelling way in a presentation that includes diagrams, mappings, orthographic drawings, axonometrics, and renderings.
- Additional grade requirements Attendance AEFIS student assessment (required) incomplete assessments will result in no grade for the course

- ### Academic Integrity

  Student-teacher relationships are built on trust. For example, students must trust that teachers have made appropriate decisions about the structure and content of the courses they teach, and teachers must trust that the assignments that students turn in are their own. Acts that violate this trust undermine the educational process. The Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities and The Graduate Student Supplement define various forms of Academic Dishonesty and you should make yourself familiar with these. In this class, all assignments that are turned in for a grade must represent the student’s own work. In cases where help was received, or teamwork was allowed, a notation on the assignment should indicate your collaboration.

  Violations of academic integrity may also be reported to the appropriate Dean (Dean of Students for undergraduate students or the Dean of Graduate Education for graduate students, respectively).

  If you have any question concerning this policy before submitting an assignment, please ask for clarification. In addition, you can visit the following site for more information on our Academic Integrity Policy: [Students Rights, Responsibilities, and Judicial Affairs](https://info.rpi.edu/dean-students/student-rights-responsibilities-and-judicial-affairs).

### Additional Academic Integrity Course Policy and Penalty Information:

**STATEMENT OF ACADEMINC INTEGRITY**

The Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities defines various forms of Academic Dishonesty and procedures for responding to them. Academic dishonesty violates the trust between students and teachers. Students must trust that teachers have made appropriate decisions about the structure and content of the courses they teach. Teachers must trust that the assignments students turn in are their own performance. Violations of this trust undermines the educational process. Cheating, plagiarism (defined as the lack of attribution or citation of the work of others), willful damage or theft of another person’s work violate academic integrity. The first instance of the breach of this trust will merit a failing grade in this course and the violator(s) will be reported to the Dean of Students for disciplinary action.
