## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

Course Description

This survey course is intended as an introduction to Cognitive Neuroscience. The topics covered will focus on exploring the neural underpinnings for cognitive processes, such as sensation, language, attention, motor control, executive functions, social communication, emotions, consciousness, and learning/memory. Basic aspects of nervous system function and neuroanatomy, brain development/evolution, structural and functional imaging techniques, and other research methods used in Cognitive Neuroscience will be discussed.

## Learning Outcomes

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes (CLOs):

- Students who successfully complete this course will be able to compare and contrast the techniques (direct and indirect measures of brain activity) that are used in Cognitive Neuroscience (cognitive tasks, clinical-pathological correlations, to imaging).
- Students who successfully complete this course will be able to identify brain regions that are involved in the basic aspects of cognition (sensation/perception, attention, memory, emotion, executive processing, social cognition).
- Students who successfully complete this course will be able to communicate in scientific language about important questions in Cognitive Neuroscience.
- Students who successfully complete this course will be able to describe findings and applications of research in Cognitive Neuroscience through articles published in professional journals as well as other media.

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes Assessment Measures:

**Attendance and participation (10%):** Class participation and attendance are mandatory and grades will be determined by student’s attendance and engagement in class and written responses to course materials.

**Assignments**

**Journal article critiques (10%):** Students are required to write critiques for 5 of the articles groups will be choosing together. Each critique is due in the class that the article will be discussed (see schedule). These should be brief (1 page), explicitly include student’s thoughts about the work, and submitted on LMS. The work should be the student’s own and this is not to be done as a group project.

**Group presentation (“Journal Club”) (10%):** Students will be required to participate in one group presentation this semester, which involves choosing the article to read, leading the discussion of the journal articles we are reading; the articles will be approved by the professor. Groups will be formed early in the semester based upon similar interest in topics and/or schedule availability (TBD).

**ZAPS lab assignments (15%):** Students will complete 12 online ZAPS labs related to the content in class throughout the semester. The work should be the student’s own and this is not to be done as a group project.

**\*\* Any assignment that is worked on outside of class needs to be submitted via the LMS link by 11:59 pm on its due date to be considered on time. See lateness policy below for more details.\*\***

**Quizzes & Exams**

**Quizzes (10%):** Quizzes, based upon the assigned reading, will be completed each week (by most Tuesdays). These quizzes will be multiple-choice, true/false, short answer question quizzes associated with assigned readings for the class meeting and the lecture materials that were covered the week prior. No make-up quizzes will be permitted. The instructor will drop the lowest quiz grade for each student.

**Exams 1, 2, and 3 (45%; 15% each):** There will be two exams during the semester consisting of multiple-choice, short answer, and true or false questions. Questions will be related to materials (e.g. lectures, readings from text and journal articles, exercises) covered in the class up until when the exam is given.

**Professor Walf’s I.D.E.A. Statement** In this classroom, we will acknowledge and aim to respect **i**nclusivity, **d**iversity, **e**quity, and **a**wareness. In our classroom, all students are welcome and will be supported regardless of race/ethnicity, gender identities, gender expressions, sexual orientation, socio-economic status, age, disabilities, religion, regional background, neurodiversity, citizenship status, nationality and the many other diverse identities that we each bring here. Indeed, the diversity that the students bring to this classroom should be viewed as a strength, resource, and benefit.

We will be focusing on the material from a (neuro)science perspective. We must be aware that science is not always objective and unbiased and historically has not included representation of diverse individuals in the experiments or the researchers themselves. For example, individual differences between human subjects in experiments were often minimized or considered confounds because of the added complexity they may have contributed to the analysis of the data. However, in this classroom, we will strive to integrate a diverse set of experiences for a more comprehensive understanding of the material in this course, despite the potential for added complexity. An important part of a college education is learning about the complexity of various issues. We will also expand our understanding by hearing from diverse voices in the field through our readings and discussions of the material. Moreover, students in this class are encouraged to participate during class meetings and all efforts will be made for students to have an equitable opportunity to contribute and succeed in a supportive learning environment. Grading will also be equitable. Final course grades are non-negotiable and will be based upon the same metrics described in the syllabus for each and every student.

Our classroom will represent a diversity of individual beliefs, backgrounds, and experiences. We all must listen and show respect for every other member of this class even if we do not necessarily agree. Please honor the uniqueness of our classroom community and appreciate the opportunity we must learn from one another. If you have any questions or concerns, please discuss with me.
