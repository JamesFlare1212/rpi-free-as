## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

We probably all agree that reading is important. But why? Seriously, what does reading even mean?

Though reading can encompass any encounter with “texts” of any genre or medium, this course acts as an introduction to specifically literary texts. Reading is often understood as a solitary, contemplative, or escapist act—something we do in private, quietly, even in secret. But we will see that reading is very much the opposite: it is a social act. Read with openness and a willingness to examine both ourselves and our society, the texts in this class offer us unexpected ways of reflecting on our own values and grasping the ways we are implicated in the lives of others—how we intersect with and mutually constitute each other. Reading helps us narrate, and therefore make thinkable, the relationships and systems that structure our everyday lives.

This class presupposes that vital to an education in any discipline is the creative act of reading. In addition to technical training, a genuine education includes developing the attentiveness, curiosity, and critical thinking that allow us to examine ourselves and the broader culture and society in which we participate. This process is not always easy, as it involves developing ways of reading that are largely undervalued in our contemporary society; can seem distant from the activities of everyday life; and sometimes require uncomfortable, and even at times painful, self-reflection.

This course is meant to help students learn to read well in any genre, by reflecting critically on their past and present experiences with literature, on their personal literary habits, and on a selection of key literature that stages encounters and conversations with writing, engagements with art and culture, and the creation of readerly experience. This course will be of interest to students wanting to become conscious readers of all sorts of literary and cultural texts and to better understand how art and culture contribute to the creation of social life, in ways seen and unseen.

This course has been approved to grant credit toward Rensselaer’s Communication Intensive (CI) Requirement for undergraduate students.

## Learning Outcomes

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes (CLOs):

- Reading and Interpretation: Read select literary texts from a range of periods—Romantic, modernist, contemporary—that represent a range of perspectives while building core skills in reading, interpretation, and argumentation appropriate to the humanities.
- Written/Oral Communication: Articulate thoughts and ideas clearly and effectively in written and oral forms to academic and general audiences; demonstrate ability to listen effectively, speak publicly, and express ideas to others; recognize potential communication barriers and adjust as needed; and write, revise, edit, and proofread.
- Critical Thinking/Problem Solving: Exercise sound reasoning to analyze texts, interpret new ideas, formulate effective questions, and overcome problems. Obtain, interpret, and use knowledge, facts, and qualitative data and demonstrate originality and inventiveness. Identify strengths and weaknesses of solutions, conclusions, or approaches to problems and texts.
- Intellectual Curiosity, Creativity, and Maturity: Demonstrate a desire to know and learn more (about self, others, and society) and approach ideas and conversation with imagination, creativity, and intellectual good faith.
- Capacity for Improvement: Understand intellectual inquiry as an ongoing process, approach assignments and projects as occasions for continuous improvement and learning, practice self-reflection and meta-cognition, engage in intellectual conversation with others, provide and receive constructive feedback from others.

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes Assessment Measures:

| Literacy Narrative 1 | A brief personal narrative that documents formative experiences with reading, studying, and/or writing about literary texts while analyzing critically one’s literacies. |
| --- | --- |
| Close Reading Essay 1 | A brief analytic essay, focusing on the fundamentals of close reading, of using textual evidence to advance arguments about literary and cultural texts. |
| Close Reading Essay 2 | A mid-length analytic essay, focusing on the fundamentals of close reading. |
| Literacy Narrative 2 | A longform persuasive literacy narrative, in which students incorporate analyses of texts they have read this semester and develop their own “theory” of literacy. |
| Course Engagement | Throughout the semester, regular attendance, focus, participation, collaboration, and consultation will contribute to students’ overall engagement with the course materials, their professor, and their peers. |

In response to each assignment submission, Dr. Murray will provide feedback in the form of comments, questions, and provocations. These are offered to help students take responsibility for their own ideas and critical interventions and to develop them in academic and other contexts. Comments are offered to help students:

- Develop core skills in literary and cultural analysis
- Hone writing and composition skills
- Become attentive and critical readers of literature, culture, and media of all sorts
- Develop, articulate, and justify your ideas, insights, and arguments
- Pursue the ideas they find most interesting and meaningful

## Policies

### Attendance Policy:

This course focuses on in-class discussion and collaborative learning, anchored in textual analysis. Aside from absences for illness, religious holidays, and documented absences for university-related activities, your physical and active intellectual presence at each class meeting is essential. Your success, and that of your peers, depends on your preparedness for class and your readiness to discuss our course materials (see Appendix 5 for more information on what constitutes such active presence).

Each of you will have an allotment of three (3) unexcused absences that do not fall under any of the categories above. Any further absences will incur a penalty of half a letter on your final grade.

If you miss any class meetings because of the Add/Drop period, you still must complete all reading and writing assignments from that time.

### Additional Academic Integrity Course Policy and Penalty Information:

Student-teacher relationships are built on trust. For example, students must trust that teachers have made appropriate decisions about the structure and content of the courses they teach, and teachers must trust that the assignments that students turn in are their own. Acts that violate this trust undermine the educational process. The Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities and The Graduate Student Supplement define various forms of Academic Dishonesty and you should make yourself familiar with these. In this class, all assignments that are turned in for a grade must represent the student’s own work. I take plagiarism and other forms of academic dishonesty seriously.
