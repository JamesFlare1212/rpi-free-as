## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

This course will introduce you to the field of motion picture studies from aesthetic, critical, reflexive, historical, and conceptual/theoretical perspectives. The aim is to examine, enhance, and transform your personal relationship to motion pictures/film in a dialogue-based setting. Using student-centered learning techniques, the course will reflect your interests as a learning community, with the aim of developing a thoughtful, critical, and empathetic approach informed by an awareness of the global mediascape. We will focus on fictional work and stories. We’ll watch lots of movies and think deeply about how they do what they do to us as individuals and as groups, and what they mean to our cultures.

## Course Goals

### Goals:

1\) You will develop a faculty for critical media engagement with motion pictures, learning how to apply aesthetic, historical, and critical frameworks as tools towards deepening your understanding of them. You will learn some of the fundamental terms and concepts used in film and media studies. Of particular importance is learning how to make connections between conceptual issues and cinema as practice (here, practices refers to viewing, critiquing, sharing, and creating).

2\) As a complement to these critical tools, you will enhance your reflexive media awareness—your processual and dialogic understanding of your relationship to motion pictures—so that you can more fully articulate your experience of motion pictures, contextualize it, and empathize with others’ perspectives. You will develop your communicative/generative skills by articulating and refining your thoughts in oral, written, and presentational forms, and (if you are so inclined) by creating media works in various formats as a way of engaging with concepts we encounter in this class.

3\) Although this class focuses on film/motion pictures, its lessons can be applied to, and contextualized by, an awareness of the larger mediascape; you should gain a deeper understanding of how media shapes us, as individuals, as groups and communities, and as cultures.

## Learning Outcomes

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes (CLOs):

- -Identify, explain, and evaluate conceptual and theoretical strategies involved in media studies as relates to film and motion pictures.
- -Apply critical lenses to motion picture media texts, and debate the value and function of those critical strategies.
- -Work collaboratively with a group of peers to compose a multi-faceted critique of a body of films.
- -Collaboratively produce a timeline of the development of motion pictures from historical, economic, and cultural perspectives.
- -Produce a creative or critical text in response to a body of films or film-related cultural artifacts.

## Grading Criteria

### Criteria Details:

ASSESSMENT: ‘CINEMA POINTS’-BASED ‘GAMEFUL’ GRADING  
Introduction: Grading for this class will unfold a little differently than what you’re likely familiar with. Using what might be called a ‘gameful’ approach, your performance in the class is based on the points that you earn, much like in a game system (a video game, a table-top role-playing game, or what have you). Like in some games, or in many popular films/TV, there’s a ‘clock’ defined by milestones in the semester; many assignments have numerous target dates by which you can turn in work, while some have only one.  
Everyone begins with 0 points. At the start of the semester, there are 600,000+ points available. As you accomplish tasks in the class, you build up points towards your final grade. There are central tasks for everyone to complete (like the opening activities, daily readings and assignments, and the required ‘journals’ connected to participation in the class). But most assignments in the class, including the final project options, are on a self-selected basis; it is up to you to figure out which of the available assignments you will take on in order to build up enough points to get the ‘rank’ (and grade) you want by the end of class. There are different types of assignments: Some are collaborative, some individual, some are presentations, some written, and so on. There’s quite a variety (see below).  
[Consider: Aren’t all of your classes already games? Here, we’re just changing the game rules in hopes that it will change your experience, and change what ‘achievement’ looks like.]

‘Medal’ Based Scoring  
Assignments will be assessed using a simplified ‘medal’ system. Keep in mind that this is not like an A through F, or a percentage grade. For most assignments, the ‘medal’ reflects the categories described below, attributing points based on how well you’ve addressed the assignment’s parameters. For certain larger projects, we may establish a more detailed rubric for grading, or reach an understanding through discussion--but in general, expectations should be clear through the guidelines for the assignment. In some cases, particularly for larger assignments, you are invited to revise an assignment in order to improve its medal; we can always discuss the reasoning for the medals that have been attributed.  
With journals/self-assessments, you’ll be assigning yourself medals based on criteria (see below).  
Medal Points % Description  
Diamond* 120% A sparkling exemplar for the class, full of dazzling insight  
Platinum* 110% A perceptive, clever, and resilient exploration of assignment  
Gold 100% Weighty, polished, addressing all required aspects of assignment  
Silver 90% A response with clarity and strength, but maybe a bit of tarnish  
Bronze 80% Relatively solid response; some clarity, some weaknesses  
Copper 70% Mostly complete, but bends under the stress of examination  
Nickel 60% Partial or relatively flimsy assignment that needs strengthening  
Tin\*\* 50% Dull or lifeless response, crumples in the breeziest of discussions  
*To receive a ‘Platinum’ medal requires that you go above and beyond the requirements of the assignment, and the ‘Diamond’ requires even more in-depth work of great quality.  
\*\*Below ‘Tin’ means you simply failed to turn in the assignment, or it’s too incomplete to grade
