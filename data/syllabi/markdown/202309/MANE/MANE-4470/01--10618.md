## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

Course Summary  
This is an introductory course on the principles of radiation protection – also known as health physics – that is required for all nuclear engineering majors. This course begins by imparting a fundamental understanding of radiation sources, radiation transport, and the interaction of ionizing radiation with matter including living matter. This foundation informs the study of the biological effects of radiation, internal and external dosimetry, and occupational and environmental radiation protection standards and regulations. Throughout the course this knowledge will be applied to:  
– analyze and minimize (as needed) radiological risks, and  
– analyze and mitigate (as needed) radiological accidents.  
Analyses will rely on computational tools and extensive use of publicly available data sources. The ability to access, understand, and correctly apply data from these sources is crucial to this course and to nuclear engineering practice. Accounting for uncertainty – in the analytical methods and in the extensive data used as input to these methods – is also a crucial part of this course.  
In particular, there are ten fundamental topics we will study:  
1\. Sources of Radiation  
2\. Radiation Physics  
3\. Radiation Kinetics  
4\. Radiation Transport  
5\. Radiation Interaction with Matter  
6\. Radiation Interaction with Living Matter (Radiobiology)  
7\. Radiation Dosimetry, Safety Standards, and Regulations  
8\. Radiation Detection  
9\. Internal Radiation Safety  
10\. External Radiation Safety  
Additional classes will be devoted to case studies, student presentations, and exams.

## Grading Criteria

### Criteria Details:

Grading and Deliverables  
Final exam (final exam week): 35% total  
Two exams, 20% each 40% total  
Homework, exercises, and discussions: 25% total  
The exams may have both open book and closed book components. Additional exam details will be covered prior to each exam. The exams are in part designed to determine if you have learned from mistakes you and/or your peers made in the homework and exercises.  
Homework will be assigned weekly and is to be uploaded as a PDF to the LMS prior to the start of the next class (usually one week from the date assigned). Homework will be discussed in the class in which it is due so late homework will be far less useful to you – and this reduced utility will be reflected in your homework grade.  
If you do submit a homework assignment late or for some other reason you do not get full credit on a submitted assignment, you may regain some credit by adding value to your submission (or re-submission): e.g., going beyond the question posed to provide a more comprehensive answer, or adding additional information relevant to the problem. For example, if the assignment asks you to add various pairs of numbers you could write about how adding even-even pairs or odd-odd pairs result in an even sum, while even-odd pairs result in an odd sum. Not especially deep, but still added value that goes beyond the question asked. Repeating the problem for a different input and comparing and contrasting the results is another good way to add value. For example, if the problem asks about the decay of various uranium isotopes you might compare those results with the decay of various thorium and/or plutonium isotopes as these are also fission fuels.  
Please note that the homework you submit is considered public within our class, just like an in-class discussion. Excerpts from your homework may be posted to the LMS and/or presented by you or me in class. Thus, please write legibly. You are expected to make mistakes in your homework. We often learn more from mistakes and the point of reviewing the homework is to do just that. When mistakes are presented they are not to demean or ridicule, but to inform and learn from. This is why my evaluation of your homework is based on good-faith effort and not on correctness.  
In-class exercises and discussions must be completed in the class or classes they are conducted in unless an extension is granted or an alternative is allowed (e.g., if a class is missed). Alternatives may involve more work than the original exercises and discussions so it is recommended that you avoid needing an alternative when possible.  
Certain assignments will have minimum thresholds which must be met to pass the course. For example, you may be required to revise and resubmit certain assignments until you achieve a minimum threshold grade. These assignments and their thresholds will be clearly indicated when assigned. In particular, three Exemplary Investigative Projects will require that you get an A on each to pass the course. The purpose of feedback on these projects is to earn that A through your revisions. Thus, it is imperative that you submit these assignments in a timely manner so that you have time for what could be multiple feedback and revision cycles.  
Final letter grades correspond to numeric course grades as follows:  
A ≥ 94 A- ≥ 90  
B+ ≥ 87 B ≥ 84 B- ≥ 80  
C+ ≥ 77 C ≥ 74 C- ≥ 70 F < 70  
This system establishes a common base grade scheme for the entire class. However, I reserve the right to “curve up” at the end of the course by applying one or more alternative grading schemes to determine each student’s final course grade:  
1\) As long as each scheme is applied equally to every student in the class; and  
2\) Each student receives the highest grade determined by all schemes so that no student’s grade will be lower than the base grade scheme, only the same or higher.  
Note that the minimum threshold to pass the course is 70%. There is a chance that your life, or someone else’s life, could depend on you embracing some level of content from this course. A course grade of 70% or above is a proxy for asking you to achieve that level of proficiency.  
Grade Disputes  
Grade disputes for exams must be made in writing to me within 14 days of the exam being returned in class or made available for your viewing. For each problem being disputed please identify the question and why you believe the grade should be different. For disputes of homework, exercise, and discussion grades please arrange to meet with me. You are encouraged to dispute a grade if you believe it is warranted, but you must do so in a timely manner so you can learn from the experience.

### Additional Academic Integrity Course Policy and Penalty Information:

Academic Integrity  
Student-teacher relationships are built on trust. For example, students must trust that teachers have made appropriate decisions about the structure and content of the courses they teach and how student work is evaluated, and teachers must trust that the assignments that students turn in are their own. Acts that violate this trust undermine the educational process. The Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities and The Graduate Student Supplement define various forms of Academic Dishonesty and you should make yourself familiar with these.  
In this class, all homework assignments (that are turned in for a grade) must be significantly representative of your own work unless it is specifically stated that multiple authors are permitted. As an individual you will submit your own work with your own substantial and significant individual content and style.  
In cases where teamwork is allowed, a notation on the assignment should clearly identify all collaborators and, if requested or if you choose to, their individual contributions.  
That said, I strongly encourage you to collaborate in studying for this course and working on the exercises and homework assignments to maximize your learning and to broaden your experiences in how one might apply what you learn to solving challenging radiological engineering problems. Nonetheless, at the end of the collaborative process you must have your own copy of your deliverable in which you identify your collaboration (e.g., in comments and/or references clearly indicated in the assignment). Prior to submission you should review and revise your deliverable to match your unique style and approach to organizing and communicating your information.  
Specific to this course, academic dishonesty can take the following forms.  
1\) Taking an exam with any assistance from another person, regardless of who they are, unless the instructor specifically states that it is a group activity (e.g., a group oral exam).  
2\) Using any materials (such as a crib sheet or a calculator) that you did not bring for you and you alone to use on an exam, unless given explicit permission by the proctor (e.g., typically for extraordinary circumstances such as the failure of your calculator during an exam, etc., though such assistance should not be expected or relied on).  
3\) Using any materials (such as a text, extra crib sheets, a laptop, a cell phone, any electronic device – wireless or otherwise, etc.) that are not explicitly permitted during an exam.  
4\) Interaction with a wireless device (e.g., a cell phone or tablet) during an exam (except when explicitly allowed by the proctor) will be considered an illicit data exchange and will result in a zero for the entire exam.  
5\) Exiting the exam room without permission, or not returning in a prompt and timely manner when given permission. “Prompt and timely” is at the discretion of the instructional team.  
6\) Submission of a deliverable that, except for short phrases or properly referenced material, consists of any content not created by and original to you.  
7\) Indicating someone as “present” (e.g., on an attendance sheet or submitting an in-class exercise) who is not present in class at the time the indication is made.  
8\) Collaboration is encouraged. However, assisting someone else with work where they intend to turn in the work as if it was done on their own will itself be considered a serious violation of academic honesty. Give credit for any assistance received, and have sufficient content of your own making according to the expectations of the deliverable.  
The penalty for submission of an exam that is in violation of this policy is failure of the course and referral to the Dean of Students with a recommendation for expulsion.  
Submission of a deliverable that is in violation of this policy will result in a grade of zero on the assignment for the first offense. If there is a subsequent infraction the student fails the course and is referred to the Dean of Students.  
If you have any questions concerning this policy before submitting a deliverable please ask for clarification.  
If you are ever concerned about passing the course please talk to me as soon as possible. I will work with you as I would anyone and everyone in class to help you help yourself. A student’s dishonesty in this course might be motivated by greed or an inflated sense of entitlement, but it should never be motivated by fear.
