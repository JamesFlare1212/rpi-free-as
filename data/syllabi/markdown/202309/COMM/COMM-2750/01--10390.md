## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

Even though our culture is awash in entertainment and information options, from gaming to social media to a near-infinite variety of live entertainment and physical adventure options, the amount of TV and video Americans watch has gone up in the 25 years.

Is this a good thing? A bad thing? A little of both? What is driving it?

At the very least, television (and all the video-based media that are its generational descendants) offers a common, rich and accessible data set open to the practice of academic study. For that reason alone, it is worth a university student’s consideration. It is likely however that the academic study of television – as an invention, industry, historical product, and economic product – will inspire ideas about the culture in which you live in that you have never previously considered; ideas with the potential to influence your life path. But even if you ultimately decide television is nothing more than a way to forget about your troubles and just kill time, having available the tools of critical, historical and aesthetic analysis can greatly expand the range of imaginative fun we can while viewing television and ruminating on what we’ve watched.

## Course Goals

### Goals:

- Learn how to view television and other video media through multiple “lenses” – critical, historical and aesthetic; extract and contextualize information via analysis; and practice the art of assembling and sharing contextualized information publicly.
- Practice analyzing “texts” (TV shows, commercials and other ephemeral media and critical articles) as a scholar: including the art of “reading” a text fairly*,* situating a text within prior knowledge, determining if the material is worth deep exploration, *defacing* the material, developing questions, attempting to write a thesis and develop an article or presentation, discovering weaknesses, doing further research, and finally creating new knowledge (or a challenge to conventional wisdom) and sharing it.
- **Do the above with a lightness, humility and sense of humor and joy. After all, this is television not aerodynamics or statistical mechanics. Have some fun. Every idea that emerges through even a modest “analyzing” process is worthy of sharing.**
- Gain insight into the historical interplay between the mediums/technologies of communication and entertainment and the cultures with which they are in dialog.
- Expand our analysis of televised media to include not only televised programs that fall into the basic genres of cinema (drama, comedy, documentary, etc.) but also news, talk, reality, public service, shopping and other non-cinema media, as well as commercials, infomercials, pre-rolls and pop-ups and other ephemeral media.
- Explore the tension between art-making and commerce
- Explore the struggle for authorship and credit
- Learn a bit about a few of the “big thinkers” in television and media studies: including Marshall McLuhan, Raymond Williams, Neil Postman, Amanda Lotz and Monika Bielskyte.

- ### Academic Integrity

  Student-teacher relationships are built on trust. For example, students must trust that teachers have made appropriate decisions about the structure and content of the courses they teach, and teachers must trust that the assignments that students turn in are their own. Acts that violate this trust undermine the educational process. The Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities and The Graduate Student Supplement define various forms of Academic Dishonesty and you should make yourself familiar with these. In this class, all assignments that are turned in for a grade must represent the student’s own work. In cases where help was received, or teamwork was allowed, a notation on the assignment should indicate your collaboration.

  Violations of academic integrity may also be reported to the appropriate Dean (Dean of Students for undergraduate students or the Dean of Graduate Education for graduate students, respectively).

  If you have any question concerning this policy before submitting an assignment, please ask for clarification. In addition, you can visit the following site for more information on our Academic Integrity Policy: [Students Rights, Responsibilities, and Judicial Affairs](https://info.rpi.edu/dean-students/student-rights-responsibilities-and-judicial-affairs).
