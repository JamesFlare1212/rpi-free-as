## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

This course focuses on the co-evolution of life with Earth’s systems and targets students in Earth science, biology, chemistry, and physics. Topics include the emergence and early evolution of life on Earth, the environmental limits of life, microbial and metabolic evolution; the role of life in moderating global elemental cycles; earliest records of life; evolution of photosynthesis and the Great Oxidation Event; fossilization and evolution of complex life; mass radiations, mass extinctions and global change.

## Learning Outcomes

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes (CLOs):

- Explain the interactions between the geosphere and biosphere that affect the emergence of life; global biogeochemical cycles; modern and past biodiversity; evolution of complex life as recorded in the geologic record.
- Identify the environmental and geologic requirements for the emergence of life on Earth and explain the modern hypotheses for the origin and early evolution of life on Earth
- Demonstrate knowledge of major events in the history of the Earth and biosphere, including major extinctions and radiations and global change events.

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes Assessment Measures:

**Tasks:** In order to understand geobiology, you will need to learn skills. These will be your tasks. Sometimes these will be virtual fieldtrips, problem sets or completing an analysis.

**Reading Responses:** The goal of reading responses is to prepare you for class discussion. Responses can be a summary in *your own words*, connection**s** of material to other content (either in class, in the media, or in other classes; at least 2), question**s** you have (at least 3), and/or item**s** you learned/found interesting (at least 3). These responses do not need to be long (1 paragraph will suffice), but for full credit responses must be of substance. Responses are due at 10:00 am before class, submitted on BlackBoard. You are welcome to work ahead, but because readings may change due to schedule revisions, Blackboard responses will be open approximately 2 weeks prior to due date. It is possible that due to schedule changes, you may read and respond to a topic we do not discuss in class. Nevertheless, the reading is still connected to the material and will enhance your understanding of course content. **Late responses will not be accepted for reading responses.** The lowest reading response will be dropped.

**Take Home Exams:** There will be three take home exams distributed over the semester. These will be problem and/or inquiry based, and require you to utilize information you learned as well as conduct outside research. The first 2 exams are worth 15% each, and exam 3 will be worth 30%. You will have a week to complete the exams. There will be no final exam in this course.

**In class engagement and participation:** Active student participation in class is an essential part of the learning process. Students are expected to participate in class discussions and hands-on learning activities, as well as peer instruction and evaluation. Attendance will not be taken. However, if you do miss class, you are responsible for obtaining any material, announcements and assignments covered in class. During the semester we will have numerous in-class writing assignments, and other team activities, which will account for 20% of the final grade. The lowest grade among these (including an absence) will be dropped. No other absences will be excused.

### Additional Academic Integrity Course Policy and Penalty Information:

This course will utilize a ***zero-tolerance policy*** for any form of cheating or other academic misconduct. All submitted assignments will be subjected to copying and plagiarism checkers. University policy deals with the problem of academic dishonesty, cheating, and plagiarism. The Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities define various forms of Academic Dishonesty and you should make yourself familiar with these. No violation will be tolerated in this class. The sanctions provided in this policy will be used to deal with any violations.

Academic dishonesty includes, but is not limited to, copying material from another student or use of written notes during an exam, representing others’ work as your own for any assignment, changing answers on an exam paper after the paper has been graded, collaborating with other students or using outside sources during exams, quizzes, and other graded assignments. Any student caught cheating will be assigned an F for the course. Furthermore, violations of academic integrity may also be reported to the appropriate Dean (Dean of Students or the Dean of Graduate Education for undergraduate and graduate students, respectively).

Student-teacher relationships are built on trust. For example, students must trust that teachers have made appropriate decisions about the structure and content of the courses they teach, and teachers must trust that the assignments that students turn in are their own. Acts that violate this trust undermine the educational process. In this class, all assignments that are turned in for a grade must represent the student’s own work. In cases where help was received, or teamwork was allowed, a notation on the assignment should indicate your collaboration.

If you have any question concerning this policy before submitting an assignment, please ***ask*** for clarification.

***A special note on plagiarism.*** Please see the definition of plagiarism given below, as outlined in The Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights. To be clear, any time material that is not the students’ own is used, it should be in quotes and contain a citation. If a student paraphrases work from elsewhere, it also requires a citation. Furthermore, even when citations are used, others’ work cannot be used as a student response on an assignment. For example, in this class, if the main conclusions are given in an article, they cannot be used verbatim as a response in the post-discussion evaluation, even if a citation is present.

*“**Plagiarism** Representing the work or words of another as one’s own through the omission of acknowledgment or reference. For example, using sentences verbatim from a published source in a term paper without appropriate referencing, or presenting as one’s own the detailed argument of a published source, or presenting as one’s own electronically or digitally enhanced graphic representations from any form of media.”* - ACADEMIC INTEGRITY (2014-2016 Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights & Responsibilities, August 2014)
