## Learning Outcomes

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes (CLOs):

- • Efficacy using engineering design methods and formal design processes, as well as their critical deconstruction in ways that are useful to your professional practice as a designer. This course focuses on introducing you to a more structured design process grounded in engineering practice, including specific design methods found in that field. These methods are used to support problem formulation, concept generation, evaluation, audit, iteration, and implementation, in addition to project management. However, unlike what RPI’s engineering students encounter in IED (Introduction to Engineering Design), we teach you to reflect on and criticize these methods in order to develop your approach to design, one that must nevertheless remain viable within the design profession. This first learning objective also includes the studio’s focus on complex social systems. Your capacity to analyze these systems, identify and define systemic problems, and develop meaningful solutions will be part of our evaluation.
- • Demonstrated skills in prototyping, making and fabrication, evaluation, and design aesthetics, as consistent with a second year design student. We expect all students to acquire, at a basic level, practical skills in drawing, CAD, 3D printing, and woodworking. In addition, you are expected to develop or advance your making skills in at least one other area (e.g. metalworking, embedded controls, textiles, design software). You are expected to use these skills to design and build meaningful prototypes that contribute directly to your design efforts. Your ability to describe and evaluate a prototype in writing and in verbal form will also be evaluated as part of this learning outcome. Finally, we also expect you to begin developing and refining you aesthetic sensibility in a way that is consistent with your own career goals, and will be demonstrate this through a curated portfolio.
- • Demonstration of effective group work and other professional skill sets. Most designers work in a social setting, and must work well with clients, end-users, business people, and others. Again, we will be conducting our class as a simulated professional design studio, and expect professional conduct throughout. Selected exercises that you do—preparation of portfolio, written reports, and oral presentation—as well as your contribution to group work and the class will be assessed, more or less individually, in constituting this final component of your grade.

## Grading Criteria

### Criteria Details:

The following serves as a uniform evaluation criterion for all assignments:

*Outstanding (A+):* Work that significantly exceeds instructor expectations. Work that demonstrates an understanding of and/or the mastery of a practice that is clearly beyond the expectations of this course.  
*Strong (A):* Work that exceeds instructor expectations for student work and performance. Professional conduct in studio.  
*Solid (B):* Work that solidly meets instructor expectations regarding specified learning objectives. Also indicates solid attendance and good participation in class, as well as contributions to teamwork. Adequate time devoted to assignments outside of class.  
*Developing (C):* Some clear progress towards stated learning objectives, but current ability or knowledge falls short of learning outcomes. May include deficiencies related to attendance, professionalism, class participation, successful group work, and effort and/or contributions to the team.  
*Basic (D):*Demonstrates a basic understanding of the subject, or a rudimentary ability to follow a defined process, but has not achieved a level of mastery where it is advisable to continue to the next studio in DIS. May also include significant deficiencies related to attendance, professionalism, class participation, successful group work, and effort and/or contributions to the team.

- ### Academic Integrity

  Student-teacher relationships are built on trust. For example, students must trust that teachers have made appropriate decisions about the structure and content of the courses they teach, and teachers must trust that the assignments that students turn in are their own. ### Additional Academic Integrity Course Policy and Penalty Information:

Academic Honesty: Student-teacher relationships are built on trust. Students must trust that teachers have made appropriate decisions about the structure and content of the course, and teachers must trust that the assignments students turn in are their own. Any act that violates this trust undermines the educational enterprise. They contradict our very reason for being at Rensselaer. The *Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities* defines various forms of academic dishonesty and the procedures for responding to them. You are expected to have reviewed and understand the Handbook. The following additional policies apply to this course:

For this course, penalties for academic dishonesty, especially in cases involving false representation of work, may well result in your failing the course, with a citation placed in your academic record. While the process for assessing the situation is as outlined in the *Rensselaer Handbook,* the initial judgment about a violation, and the penalty if any, will be determined at the discretion of the instructors and what they believe to have occurred. If you have any doubts about a situation, especially regarding plagiarism, please check with us first.
