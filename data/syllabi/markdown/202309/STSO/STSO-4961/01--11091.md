## Learning Outcomes

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes (CLOs):

- 1\. Identify the basics of microbiology and immunology of infectious agents that cause specific diseases.
- 2\. Discuss the political, economic, social, cultural, and biological factors that contribute to regional epidemics across the globe.
- 3\. Evaluate the social construction of “risk groups,” including which groups suffer the highest burden of infection and why these groups are particularly vulnerable.
- 4\. Identify key contributions that anthropologists, sociologists, and epidemiologists have made to an interdisciplinary understanding of risk and vulnerability.
- 5\. Gain an understanding of the historical context surrounding epidemic disease events and their intersection with political ideology, public health policy, and American cultural production, and make applications to contemporary public health debates.
- 6\. Employ multiple modes of analysis (medico- historical, sociological, cultural, and literary) to explore how epidemic disease outbreaks have mobilized anxieties about race, ethnicity, sexuality, and national belonging.
- 7\. Think and write critically about primary source material to analyze and assess the political, cultural, representational, interpersonal, and affective dimensions of disease.

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes Assessment Measures:

Office Hours Meeting (5%)

You are expected to come to my office hours at least one time during the first part of the semester to chat with me about your experience and/or your personal process and engagement in the class. If my office hours do not work with your schedule, we can work together to find a time that works for us both. This one-on-one meeting is a requirement of the course, because I believe that the best learning happens when we

are all personally invested in the material and the class as a learning community. The better I can understand your personal process and experience in the class, the better I can help facilitate your learning and success. To earn credit, you must schedule this required meeting before December.

Attendance and active participation (5%)

In a discussion-based course, student attendance and active participation is required and crucial to creating a successful learning community. This means coming to class ready to discuss the assigned material with observations, questions, and comments, as well as participating fully in any small and large group activities. Our class time is specially designed to prepare you for successful completion of the other graded components of the course. Thus, your full engagement and participation during our class time will directly and positively influence your grade more broadly. I expect each of you to come to class having finished all the readings and assignments for the day. I acknowledge some of you may be uncomfortable in large group discussions, and so I

recognize engaged listening and note taking as valid forms of participation. Still, consider preparing one specific point about the readings to address in class each day, or challenge yourself to speak up in class once a week in some way.

During class discussion, you should be intentionally aware of your participation, if you find you are speaking repeatedly, step back and allow others the chance to speak up; conversely, if you notice that you are not speaking during class discussions, find ways to contribute to the conversation e.g. draw upon your notes for questions or previously formed thoughts.

Class Facilitation (10%)

From the 3rd week onwards, you will take turns being lead facilitators for weekly discussions. You must demonstrate thorough knowledge of the week’s readings and facilitate the discussion via a handout, questions, and/or a creative exercise. You are also encouraged to incorporate relevant artistic work, video clips, art, blog posts, and current events. The facilitation should take between 10-20 minutes and you are free to use this time as you see fit. During the facilitation exercise, you should consider yourself

in the shoes of a course instructor and lead the discussion in a way that you would if you were a teacher. For this exercise, you should avoid simply summarizing the readings; instead, you should share what you what find interesting, challenging, or

confusing. In preparing your ‘teaching plan’ you should incorporate other students’ QCQs. Don’t forget to

QCQs (15%)

You will be responsible for posting a critical reading response of at least 250 words on Canvas a total of 10 times during the semester (10 posts x 1pts). The QCQs are designed to prepare you better for class discussions. Think of these as your notes and reflections on the readings you will use for the assignments. These are informal writing opportunities to demonstrate your critical engagement with your peers on the classmaterial and concepts. Your responses must be submitted as a “QCQ” or Quote / Comment / Question in which you: (1) identify a significant passage from the text; (2) write a paragraph explaining its significance and how it relates to the author’s main claims; (3) pose a question for discussion that arose for you from the text. Responses for the Tuesday and Friday classes are due on Monday 5pm and Thursday 5pm, respectively. While I encourage you to respond and engage in discussions through the QCQs as often as possible, you are responsible for formally responding to a QCQ

posted by a classmate 5 times during the semester (5 posts x 1pts). Your responses should be at least 150 words and include a thoughtful engagement with both your classmate’s QCQ and the course material.

(adopted from Emily Waples): Due: Friday, 10/27

From Edgar Allan Poe’s Red Death to the “walker” virus of The Walking Dead, fictional epidemics have preoccupied the American imagination, exposing and commenting on broader sociocultural and political anxieties about communication, interrelation, and social belonging. For this assignment, working either individually or in groups of 2-3, you will design a cultural artifact that responds to fictional epidemic of your own creation. Critical Movie Analysis (10%)

We won’t be having classes during Week 11 and you won’t be responsible for doing any readings. Instead, you are expected to watch a movie focusing on a public health outbreak and write a short commentary on it. You should choose the movie from this list and your commentary should be around 1,000-1,500 words. You should use at least four academic sources from the syllabus while writing your analysis. Your analysis can include one or combination of the following along with your own reflections and critical interpretations:

1\. Semioticanalysis.Ifyou’reusingthisapproach,youareexpectedtointerpret the film’s symbolism. You should look for any signs that may have a hidden meaning. Often, they reveal some character’s features. To make the task more manageable, you can try to find the objects or concepts that appear on the screen multiple times. What is the context they appear in? It might lead you to the hidden meaning of the symbols.

2\. Narrativestructureanalysis.Thistypeisquitesimilartoatypicalliterature guide. It includes looking into the film’s themes, plot, and motives. The analysis aims to identify three main elements: setup, confrontation, and resolution. You should find out whether the film follows this structure and what effect it creates. It will make the narrative structure analysis essay if you write about the theme and characters’ motivations as well.

3\. Contextualanalysis.Here,youwouldneedtoexpandyourperspective.Instead of focusing on inner elements, the contextual analysis looks at the time and place of the film’s creation. Therefore, you should work on studying the cultural context a lot. It can also be a good idea to mention the main socio-political issues of the time. You can even relate the film’s success to the director or producer and their career.

4\. Mise-en-sceneanalysis.Thistypeofanalysisworkswiththemostdistinctive feature of the movies, audiovisual elements. However, don’t forget that your task is not only to identify them but also to explain their importance. There are so many interconnected pieces of this puzzle: the light to create the mood, the props to show off characters’ personalities, messages hidden in the song lyrics.

Health Policy Brief (35%)

Pitch Due: November 3rd  
Final Project Due: December 12th

The central purpose of a health policy brief is to contextualize a public health problem and offer potential solutions to eradicate or improve its population and individual level health outcomes. This final project assignment will allow you to apply the information and concepts you learn in the first half of the course on an actual public health outbreak. You are expected to approach a public health crisis from the perspectives of social epidemiology, public health, and medical sociology/anthropology. For this, you need to identify an ongoing epidemic or a similar public health phenomenon that affects a particular community either in the US or anywhere else in the globe. Some of the examples are the epidemics of opioid, suicide, depression, hepatitis, tuberculosis, HIV, avian influenza etc. Once you identify the location you will be looking at and the pathogen you will explore, you need to make a comprehensive list of all the social, political, economic, and historical factors that gave way to the particular epidemic you are investigating. While class readings will guide your analysis, you will also need to do your own research to access the most accurate and up-to-date information. You can use CDC or NHI databases as well as academic and journalistic sources.

Before the assignment is due, I will hold a workshop on how to write a health policy brief.

The written portion of your assignment should be somewhere between 1,500-2,000 words and you should incorporate four sources from the syllabus and at least two external sources. In addition to its written component (25%), your final project has a presentation component (10%). For this part, you need to prepare a PowerPoint presentation highlighting the central points of your policy brief. Think of this assignment as though you are sharing your findings with public and global health institutions and stakeholders to motivate them to take appropriate actions.
