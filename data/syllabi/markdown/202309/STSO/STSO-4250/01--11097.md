## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

This course involves a philosophical analysis of some of the basic moral issues raised by recent and anticipated developments in the areas of biology and medicine. The general question “What are moral problems, and how does one resolve them?” is examined in the context of concrete cases involving issues such as racial healthcare disparities, abortion, euthanasia, organ transplants, experimentation on human patients, patient rights and emerging ethical issues in the biomedical field. Students will be introduced to some of the prominent ethical theories in philosophy (Utilitarianism, Kantian theory, Virtue ethics, etc.) and will use these theories to examine contemporary issues in medicine. This is a communication-intensive course.

## Learning Outcomes

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes (CLOs):

- Students will recall the content and make inferences on key important points.
- Students will be able to demonstrate critical thinking and create solutions to problems based on learned content.
- Students will demonstrate ability to compare and contrast differing perspectives in the cases of particular ethical issues.
- Students will be able to evaluate multiple perspectives concerning bioethical issues and recognize that different value systems may lead to different ethical decisions.
- Students will be able to assess complex bioethical issues and the processes used to build resolution.
- Students will have the ability to express philosophical ideas and defend them effectively in argument, both in writing and orally.
- Students will be able to understand the process of writing and revision for an academic audience.

1. **Tests (3)** **(45% --- 15% each):** These open book tests (you may use your notes and textbook) will consist of multiple choice and short answer questions on reading and lecture material. **Test 1 – 9/28**, **Test 2 – 10/30 and Test 3 – 12/7.** There is no final exam for this course.
2. **Reflection Response (****5%):** The student will find an article in the popular media regarding a contemporary ethical issue and write an analysis of the position presented in the article. More information on this assignment will be distributed during the second week of class. Due 10/26.
3. **Journals (10%):** During each lecture students will be given a prompt to reflect and write on. The student must complete 15 of these throughout the course. These reflections will be recorded online through the LMS “Journal” tab under “Assignments” each day. Due: 11/27**.**
4. **Check-Ins (2) (5% --- 2.5% each):** Check-in surveys will be available to you at the 4 week mark of the course and the 8 week mark of the course. You will complete these online. These short surveys are meant to get your feedback on your perception of how the course is going, what can be improved, and some self-evaluation regarding your performance in the course.
5. **Final Paper (30%)**: The student will submit a paper of approximately 2500 words that examines an ethical issue of particular interest to them. The student will explain the ethical issue and evaluate the issue using Utilitarian and Kantian theory. An extensive explanation of each theory and thorough application of the theories is required. Basically, the student will explain the issue, explain the two theories, apply the two theories to the issue and then explain which theory best analyzed the issued including a discussion of the strengths and weaknesses of each theory. More information on the assignment will be provided in class. There will be five steps to be completed for this paper:

   2. **Consultation at The Center for Global Communication and Design (COMM-D) (****http://www.commd.rpi.edu** **) (3%) – DUE 10/16**
   3. **Submission of Draft for Instructor’s Review (4%) – DUE: 11/6**
   4. **Final Paper (20%) – DUE: 12/4**
6. **Class Participation (5%):** I cannot emphasize enough how important class participation is. The online classroom environment will be a safe and stimulating place for the critical examination of the topics we cover. Through these discussions we will delve deeper into the subject matter of the readings. Participation includes coming to class prepared, which includes doing the assigned reading(s), and actively and thoughtfully participating in class discussions. Participation may also consist of contributions to the weekly discussion board questions. We will discuss this requirement on our first day of class. Please do not mistake the participation grade as a “freebie.” Many people mistakenly believe that they can throw anything out there, no matter how illogically construed, and it counts as “doing philosophy.” This is not the case. Your discussion contributions must convey a thoughtful analysis of the material being covered.

4. Students’ rights and responsibilities are explained in *The Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities* (https://rpi.app.box.com/s/3f1cl3w1lt1mq8gqla68v6uk3vh1c19f) and govern the conduct of both faculty and students. Rensselaer’s policies on academic integrity are explained on pp. 16-19 and include strict prohibitions against academic dishonesty. Please note in particular that the plagiarism is “the omission of acknowledgement or reference” of sources, whether or not intentional (so “I did not mean to plagiarize” is not an adequate excuse). The definitions and examples presented below are a sampling of types of academic dishonesty and are not to be construed as an exhaustive or exclusive list. The academic integrity policy applies to all students.

1. **Academic Fraud** is defined as the alteration of documentation relating to the grading process; for example, changing exam solutions to negotiate for a higher grade or tampering with an instructor's grade book.
2. **Collaboration** is defined as deliberately facilitating an act of academic dishonesty in any way or form; for example, allowing another student to observe an exam paper or allowing another student to "recycle" one's old term paper or using one another's work in a paper or lab report without citing it as another's work.
3. **Copying** is defined as obtaining information pertaining to a graded exercise by deliberately observing the paper of another student; for example, noting which alternative a neighboring student has circled on a multiple-choice exam.
4. **Cribbing** is defined as the use or attempted use of prohibited materials, information, or study aids in an academic exercise. Examples include using an unauthorized formal sheet during an exam.
5. **Fabrication** is defined as the unauthorized falsification or invention of any information in an academic exercise. Examples include the use of "bought" or "ready-made" term papers, or falsifying lab records or reports.
6. **Plagiarism** is defined as representing the work or words of another as one's own through the omission of acknowledgment or reference. Examples include using sentences verbatim from a published source in a term paper without appropriate referencing or presenting as one's own the detailed argument of a published source, or presenting as one's own electronically or digitally enhanced graphic representations from any form of media.
7. **Sabotage** is defined as the destruction of another student's work. Examples include destroying a model, lab experiment, computer program, or term paper developed by another student.
8. **Substitution** is defined as utilizing a proxy, or acting as a proxy, in any academic exercise. Examples include taking an exam for another student or having a homework assignment done by someone else.

5. Incidents of academic dishonesty on any assignment will be graded 0 points for the assignment and may be reported to the Dean of Students and the Dean of the student’s college or school, with a request that the incident be entered into the student’s permanent record at Rensselaer.

Student-teacher relationships are built on trust. For example, students must trust that teachers have made appropriate decisions about the structure and content of the courses they teach, and teachers must trust that the assignments which students turn in are their own. Acts that violate this trust undermine the educational process.

**Appeals Process**: A student can submit a written appeal of an academic penalty to the Department Chair within five (5) Institute business days of being notified of the faculty member's decision. If the course professor is the Department Chair or Dean of the school or there are other circumstances that could create the perception of bias, steps must be taken to use other appropriate individuals for the appeal process. More information on this process can be found in the handbook.

### Other Course Policies:

***Inclusivity Statement***

This course is intended to reach students from diverse backgrounds and perspectives - all are welcome here. The diversity students bring to class, across gender, sexuality, age, disability, socio-economic status, ethnicity, race, religion, and learning needs is valued as an important asset and strength in the learning process.

We will have many opportunities in this class to explore challenging issues and increase our understandings of differing perspectives. Our conversations and structured dialogues may not always be easy, and we will need patience and compassion, and a foundation grounded in respect for others. Therefore, an additional aim of this course is to increase our capacity for these kinds of conversations, as this is a valuable skill in both scholarship and in life. I am committed to providing an atmosphere for learning that respects diversity. While working together to build this community I ask all members to:

- Share their unique experiences, values, and beliefs.
- Be open to the views of others.

- Honor the uniqueness of their colleagues.
- Appreciate the opportunity that we have to learn from each other in this community.
- Value each other’s opinions and communicate in a respectful manner.
- Keep confidential discussions that the community has of a personal (or professional) nature.
- Stories stay, lessons leave
- Use “I” statements
- Take Space, Make Space
- Accept that things may remain unresolved
- Embrace discomfort, but take a moment if you need it
- If you feel yourself getting angry or defensive, ask yourself why.
- You will make mistakes and apologize if you do (it is not about your intent it is about your impact)
- Take ownership of your words and actions. This is a good way to act with more intention and consideration of others in the classroom.
- Use this opportunity together to discuss ways in which we can create an inclusive environment in this course and across the Rensselaer community.
