## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

This course prepares students for hands-on research in the humanities and interpretive social sciences. Students will gain experience in thinking about and conducting ethical research in the interdisciplinary fields of Science and Technology Studies and Sustainability Studies. Throughout the semester, students practice a variety of humanities and social science research methods through a series of exercises that contribute to individual and group projects designed to give students experience with different kinds of research skills such as observing and interpreting everyday social life, archival analysis, discourse analysis, object or artifact analysis through the practice of implosion; conducting and coding semi-structured interviews, and analyze photographs and other visual data used in social and historical research.

## Learning Outcomes

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes (CLOs):

- 1\. Recognize relationships between data and social theory in published research.
- 2\. Recognize ethical problems of research and understand how to take steps to do research in right relation with subjects.
- 3\. Make ethnographic observations and make sense of them.
- 4\. Interview people and identify themes that come out of the interview.
- 5\. Collect textual and visual data through photographs and documents, and analyze it for discursive patterns.
- 6\. Formally present findings in written and oral form.

## Policies

### Attendance Policy:

***Attendance and Participation Policy*****:** To do well on the assignments listed above--and to maintain the goodwill of your classmates--full attendance at class meetings will be necessary. Please communicate promptly and openly with your professor and TA about any circumstances in your life that make it difficult to attend lectures and team meetings, so that arrangements can be made to support your participation in the class. We are trying to build an STS and Sustainability cohort here—one that you will connect with again during your senior year in Research Design and Senior Project.

### Other Course Policies:

- **Supportive participation (20%):** All students should contribute to class culture in meaningful ways. This includes sharing and responding to ideas in class, supporting one another’s research, asking questions--anything that helps contribute to an open, engaged classroom environment. Participation grades will be based on self-assessment, research journal at midterm, professorial assessment, and your final I-Search essay.
- **Research projects (60%; 4x 15%):** Students in this course will work in individually and in small groups (~5 students) on four projects. Projects will have you work through different elements of four different methodologies designed to generate data for purposes of social research: visual analysis; textual analysis (document); implosion of artifact/object; ethnographic or oral history interview. Some of this work will be submitted individually; some of it will be submitted in partnerships or teams. Due dates for these assignments are available on the course schedule. The professor reserves the right to change due dates depending on student progress.
- **I-Search essay (20%):** This is a written report about your research \*process\* that tells the story of your search this semester. You should keep a journal of your searches, including those that fail and those that succeed, and include them in this paper. Your culminating assignment is to demonstrate critical understanding of methods used in social and historical research. This paper counts as your final exam. It is DUE in full on our last day of class.

### Additional Academic Integrity Course Policy and Penalty Information:

***Honesty:*** If you are using somebody else’s words, put them in quotes and cite exactly where they came from. If you are using someone else’s ideas, cite them. If you are using ChatGPT, make sure to indicate that with quotation marks and citation. Seriously, the instructor is way more interested in \*your\* thoughts than in the products of AI. The *Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities* defines various forms of academic dishonesty and you should make yourself familiar with those. Violations of academic integrity include, but are not limited to: academic fraud, collaboration, copying, cribbing, fabrication, plagiarism, sabotage, and substitution. All assignments that are submitted for a grade must represent your own work and your contribution must be identified even in the case of group or team work. Violation of this policy will result in a *minimum* penalty of a zero for that assignment. Violation of this policy may include further disciplinary action, particularly if it happens more than once. If you have any question concerning this policy before submitting an assignment, please ask for clarification.

***Disagreeing with people:*** The topics discussed in class will be personal and political, and discussions are likely to involve disagreements. When engaged in carefully and respectfully, disagreement can be incredibly productive, and can open up new modes of thinking and communicating as well as new avenues for research. When engaged in carelessly, disagreement can result in hard feelings, extreme discomfort, and closing off of cognitive capacities. When you disagree with classmates, do so respectfully, with the intention of pushing the discussion forward rather than shutting it down.

***Safe space, brave space:*** I would like for this classroom to be a place where everyone can be their full self. I want you to feel like you can explore new ideas, share your unique perspectives, and talk about your feelings. Behaviors that can promote this kind of atmosphere include: listening to other people, valuing divergent perspectives, recognizing your power for unique insight as well as the partiality of your perspective. We all have limitations (sometimes these are called biases). In this course, we will often talk about bias and the ways in which it can lead to distorted, lopsided, or even unethical results. I ask that you avoid behaviors that can prevent people from feeling safe to be themselves. These include using hateful language, name-calling, or misgendering people.

***Inclusive Language Guidelines*****:** The Department of Science and Technology Studies aspires to create a learning environment in which people of all identities are encouraged to contribute their perspectives to academic discourse. This course has guidelines on language and terminology that aim to foster an inclusive and welcoming environment, strengthen academic writing, enrich discussion, and reflect best professional practices.

- Gender-inclusive and non-sexist language: Language is gender-inclusive and non-sexist when we use words that affirm and respect how people describe, express, and experience their gender. Gender-inclusive/non-sexist language acknowledges people of any gender. Students may share their preferred pronouns and names, and these gender identities and gender expressions should be honored.
- Non-racist language: The labels and definitions of racial and ethnic categories vary across history, societies, and even situations. In this classroom, we will avoid racial and ethnic slurs and strive to use words that affirm and respect how people describe, express, and experience their race, ethnicity, ancestry, or cultural heritage. Please review this website for an overview of racial category terms, which discusses why some terms are offensive and others are preferred: https://raceandpedagogy.ssc.wisc.edu/getting-started/terminology/. Please note that this website is already somewhat outdated because it does not capitalize Black. Today, in the US context, Black and White should be capitalized, just like Latino/Latina/Latinx, Asian, Polish, Navajo, or any other term referring to a racial or ethnic category. Students may share the terminology that they prefer to describe their racial and ethnic identities, and these preferences should be honored.
- Non-ableist language: Ableist language, which devalues people with physical and mental disabilities, permeates everyday language. However, there are many options for replacing common ableist rhetoric with more thoughtful alternatives. You are encouraged to read this guide and reflect on what you are communicating when you use ableist terms. https://www.autistichoya.com/p/ableist-words-and-terms-to-avoid.html

***Title IX*****:** Title IX is a federal law that classifies sex and gender-based violence and sexual harassment as Civil Rights offenses. Sexual misconduct includes, but is not limited to: sexual harassment, sexual assault, dating and domestic violence, intimate partner violence, and stalking. Sexual misconduct is not tolerated at Rensselaer, and it is a violation of Institute policy, and state and federal law. If you are the victim of a Title IX classified offense, you can file a report, find more information about what happens when you initiate a process, and other resources on the Rensselaer Title IX page. *As an RPI faculty member, I am required to report any disclosures of sex discrimination, sexual harassment, or sexual or relationship violence by employees and/or students to the Institute’s Title IX coordinators.*
