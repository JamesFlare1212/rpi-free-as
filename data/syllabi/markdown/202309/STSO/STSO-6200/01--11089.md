## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

This course provides a set of genealogies, discourses, situations, and trajectories that serves as a conceptual basis for comprehending current configurations and past practices in Science Studies (STS). Science Studies is a critical interdisciplinary formation that promotes self-awareness and reflexivity about how science is done, what sciences are undone, and how both done and undone science may be studied. Your engagement with and contributions to the premises of this course will be assessed through four arcs (detailed below). Graduate courses are designed around particular skills designed to broaden your commitments to and engagement with the field of STS.

Science studies has been pursued from myriad disciplinary and adisciplinary perspectives, each representing conceptual frameworks used in the fields of STS. These fields include but are not limited to anthropology, cultural studies, history, policy studies, and sociology of science and technology. We must navigate the various streams of scholarship that comprise STS: laboratory studies, sociology of scientific knowledge (SSK), social construction of technology (SCOT), actor-network theory (ANT), and the new political sociology of science (NPSS), among others. Part of the work we do together is tracing these approaches and the concepts and methods on which they are based through multiple possible STS futures, including feminist STS, postcolonial STS, and other, as yet unnamed STSs.

This course steers clear of naming “core” concepts or “canonical” texts. Your professor’s disciplinary agnosticism is a commitment to ensuring that you acquire a comprehensive grasp of our interdisciplinary field, which works in synergistic relationship with other knowledge formations. My attempt is always to clarify the genealogies, configurations, discourses, situations, and trajectories of STS without prescribing yours. We read “foundational”/“classic”/“postfoundational” texts. Our practical goal is intense immersion in a variety of science studies that you might encounter in our field.

**ASSESSMENT**

**Structured Provocation and Discussion Facilitation**

Provoking others to insight is one the most valuable skills practiced in graduate school.

On the date for which you sign up, you are responsible for introducing the reading materials for the day so as to provoke thinking and facilitate discussion for approximately one hour of the 2 ½ hour class period (factoring in a 20-minute break). Facilitation should draw upon the main points of our readings. You may choose to contextualize the texts; apply concepts raised therein to a specific analytic situation of your choosing; provide background or genealogy by drawing upon additional material; or generously critique the reading by drawing on other texts and contexts. Provocations integrate texts with *different* sources, intentions, or characteristics than those under “official” discussion. Provocations may include video, performance, installation, artistic, or digital projects that provide substantive examples of the work of STS in the world.

**Science Studies Lit Review: What’s It to Them 1?**

This 7-10-page paper summarizes, engages, and reflects on the position of the first 2 “future trajectories” books **and at least 1 text from STS pasts**. This paper is due October 31, and should precisely describe what the current of science studies looks like through the lens of these authors. What is the meaning and value of STS to these authors? Do they converge with or diverge from each other? What do they look like in relation to each other? Via what stories, theories, and methods do they accomplish their work? What do they look like in relation to other STS texts you have read? Judging from these works, what are possible future STS trajectories? In what directions do they propel STS?

**Science Studies Lit Review: What’s It to Them 2?**

This 7-10-page paper summarizes, engages, and reflects on the last 3 “future trajectories” books. This paper is due November 21, before your departure for Thanksgiving break, and should precisely describe what current science studies trajectories look like through the lens of these authors—just as you did with the previous assignment.

**Synthesis**

The ability to improvise and synthesize by “thinking on your feet” is essential to success in graduate school. This arc requires that you draw connections across the components of each of your classes. The oral synthesis of the previous class will be presented in the first 20 minutes of the next class. The point is for you to draws together our streams of discussion and to create continuity and convergence across class meetings.

**Final Written Assignment: Science Studies: What’s It to Me?**

Your final written assignment for this course is a position paper summarizing your current understanding of the breadth of science studies that comprise the field of STS, which by now you realize is comprised of interlocking “science studies.” Choose a theme around which to arrange your characterizations; add texts and contexts to those discussed in the course (you may include material from your previous “what’s STS to them” papers). Then reflect as fully as possible upon the body of knowledge presented in this course. This reflection should be approximately 12-15 pages, typed, double-spaced, polished, and deeply documented, written as a polished piece with your portfolio or dissertation proposal lit review in mind. It is meant to be useful to you as you progress through the program—to mark where you’ve been; what STS means to you; where you think you and the field are going; and what you make of that.

***Week 5* Situating Science Studies I: Place Matters**

**Tuesday, September 26**

Feminist transformation of science studies came early enough that it may be thought of as foundational to Anglo-American STS (US and UK), despite ongoing marginalization implied by the label. Feminist scientists and historians and philosophers of science, the latter schooled in logical positivism, began critique of both science studies and feminist movements. Their critical endeavor emphasized that the places where science is done were largely masculine domains dominated by modes of expertise and objectivity that foreclosed accounts founded in tacit knowledge, proximity and respect between researchers and researched, and situation. They became targets during the “science wars.” **Readings:**

1. Hilary Rose, excerpts from *Love, Power, Knowledge: Towards a Feminist Transformation of the Sciences;*
2. Donna Haraway, “Situated Knowledges” or excerpts of *Primate Visions*. LMS

## Learning Outcomes

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes (CLOs):

- 1\. Students will demonstrate comprehensive grasp of key conceptual and theoretical developments in the field of Science and Technology Studies through reflexive reading and discussion of key texts reviewing recent literature in the field.
