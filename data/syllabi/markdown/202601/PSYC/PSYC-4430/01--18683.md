## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

Jon Kabat-Zinn said that “Mindfulness means paying attention in a particular way: on purpose, in the present moment, and nonjudgmentally.” Throughout the day we tend to spend much of our time thinking about the past and the future. Mindfulness involves giving particular attention to our moment-to-moment experiences in a way that emphasizes observing, rather than judging or evaluating. Everyday activities such as driving a car or eating a meal provide us the opportunity to practice this focused, moment-to-moment attention. In addition, we can practice mindfulness through formal meditation. There is empirical evidence of the many health benefits of mindfulness meditation, including the potential to produce fundamental changes in brain structure and epigenetic change. In this class, we will discuss the historical and psychological foundations of mindfulness and empirical findings supporting its positive effects. Students will participate in structured exercises. This course is a very hands-on approach to mindfulness…you will be required to practice mindfulness and meditation and develop your mindful skills. It is also more of a discussion course.

## Academic Integrity

Student-teacher relationships are built on trust. For example, students must trust that teachers have made appropriate decisions about the structure and content of the courses they teach, and teachers must trust that the assignments that students turn in are their own. Acts that violate this trust undermine the educational process. The Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities and The Graduate Student Supplement define various forms of Academic Dishonesty and you should make yourself familiar with these. In this class, all assignments that are turned in for a grade must represent the student’s own work. In cases where help was received, or teamwork was allowed, a notation on the assignment should indicate your collaboration. Submission of any assignment that is in violation of this policy will result in a penalty of zero grade on that assignment.

Plagiarism

If you have any questions concerning this policy before submitting an assignment, please ask for clarification.

*Please Take Note…*

It is likely that at some point a topic may arise that makes someone uncomfortable. If someone in class voices their discomfort, please respect their wish to move on to another topic. Know that I may not always be able to move on right away though. I want to make sure that I have discussed ways to work through the challenge. Having said that, it is also likely that topics arise in class that are important to talk about so that we learn how to work through challenges in life. For example, I will be talking about my experience with cancer. Talking about cancer may make some students uncomfortable. Talking about sensitive topics such as cancer is important so that we can learn how to think about those challenges and work through them. The first semester I shared with the students that I was a breast cancer survivor, a student came up after class and said, “Prof. Traver, I’m a cancer survivor too, and I want to learn how to think like you.” We both cried and hugged and laughed.

Bottom line: yes, we may discuss things that some may find uncomfortable. If we don’t discuss them, we are avoiding those things. When we avoid things, we don’t develop the skills to handle the emotions, and the situation could escalate. This class is to help prepare you for crises and challenges in life, so that you can deal with them, or even recover more quickly from them. With that being said, if a topic or discussion or question in class is too much for you at this point in time, step away and come back when you are able.

**Academic Integrity and Plagiarism**

Academic integrity is a fundamental expectation in this psychology course and in the scientific study of behavior. All written work submitted in this class must reflect **your own original thinking**, while appropriately acknowledging the ideas and research of others using **APA citation format**.

**Plagiarism is defined as presenting another person’s words, ideas, data, or theoretical arguments as your own.** In a writing-intensive psychology course, this includes—but is not limited to—the following:

- Copying or closely paraphrasing text from journal articles, textbooks, websites, or other sources without proper APA citation
- Failing to use quotation marks for direct quotes, even when a citation is provided
- Submitting summaries or paraphrases that too closely mirror the original wording or structure of the source
- Using research papers, literature reviews, or assignments written by others or obtained online
- Reusing your own work from another course (self-plagiarism) without prior written permission from the instructor
- Submitting AI-generated text (e.g., ChatGPT or similar tools) as original writing unless explicitly authorized for a specific assignment

Because psychological writing relies heavily on research literature, students must take particular care to **distinguish their own analysis and synthesis from the findings and interpretations of others**.

**Citation Expectations:** All sources in your Draft 1 and Draft 2 papers must be cited in **APA style**, including empirical journal articles, theoretical papers, textbooks, lecture material, and online sources. Students are responsible for understanding proper paraphrasing, quotation, and reference list formatting.

**Consequences of Academic Misconduct:** Violations of this policy may result in:

- A zero on the assignment or the possibility of a required revision with grade penalty
- A second offense will be referred to the university’s Academic Integrity or Conduct Office, in accordance with institutional policy

Students are encouraged to use university resources such as the **Writing Center, library research support, and APA style guides**. If you are ever unsure whether your use of a source is appropriate, **ask before submitting the assignment**.
