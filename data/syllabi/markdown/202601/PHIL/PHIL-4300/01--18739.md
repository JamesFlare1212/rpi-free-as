## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

Concerns about the place of humans in relation to the natural world and the impact of our activities on the sustainability of the environment have come to the forefront in the last half century. These concerns have now taken on a high degree of urgency. Environmental Philosophy applies the insights and methods of Philosophy to a broad set of questions and concerns regarding the environment and our place in it, such as intrinsic, aesthetic, and instrumental value; anthropocentric and non-anthropocentric views of nature and conservation; moral virtue and the environment, and ecological feminism.

## Learning Outcomes

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes (CLOs):

- 1\. Students will develop the skill of carrying on meaningful and productive philosophical discussion.
- 2\. Students will develop and utilize the skill of writing in a philosophical manner, employing analysis and argumentation to support a thesis.
- 3\. Students will be able to identify ways in which the global biosphere as well as local ecosystems are under threat from human practices.
- 5\. Students will be able to explain major distinctions within environmental philosophy, such as the distinctions between intrinsic value and instrumental value, and between anthropocentric and non-anthropocentric approaches, and regarding whether species and ecosystems have value beyond that of the individual organisms that make them up.
- 6\. Students will be able to explain the positions known as Deep Ecology and The Land Ethic by way of articulating the main points of each of these movements, as well as being able to identify criticisms of these positions.
- 4\. Students will be able to identify and discuss some of the historical and intellectual causes and precursors to the current environmental situation.
- 7\. Students will be able to explain the basics of environmental virtue ethics, ecological feminism, and aesthetic value in nature.

## Course Calendar

### Calendar Details:

Week of 1/12: What is Philosophy? Role of arguments in Philosophy; What is Environmental Ethics?  
Week of 1/19: Capacity to wonder and how we relate to the environment.  
Week of 1/26: How we arrived at the current environmental situation; State of the global environment in the early 21st Century ("Earth on Edge" video).  
Week of 2/02: Discuss Earth on Edge video; Tragedy of the Commons; legacy of Rachel Carson's *Silent Spring*.  
Week of 2/09: Anthropocentric approaches; Influential voices from the Early Modern period; Climate change denial and its motivations. Video: "Science Under Attack." **Paper on Earth on Edge video due**.  
Week of 2/16: Kant, animals, and nature. Recent defenses of anthropocentric approaches to nature.  
Week of 2/23: Global environment, international inequality, and global distributive justice. **"Science Under Attack" paper due**. Is there a need for a non-anthropocentric valuation of nature? Attitudes to nature.  
Week of 3/02: Spring Break -- No Classes.  
Week of 3/09: Catch-up; **Review for Exam 1**.  
Week of 3/16: **EXAM 1**. What sorts of values exist in the world?  
Week of 3/23: Are anthropocentrism and Non-anthropocentrism really mutually exclusive? The Land Ethic (Ecological Holism) and criticisms of this approach.  
Week of 3/30: Arguments against assigning special moral status to ecosystems or species. **Third Paper due**. Deep Ecology and its critics.  
Week of 4/06: Environmental virtue ethics; Ecological feminism.  
Week of 4/13: Environmental aesthetics; Religion and Environmental Ethics.  
Week of 4/20: Catch-up. **Review for Exam 2**.  
Week of 4/27: **EXAM 2**.

### Academic Policies:

- ### Academic Integrity

  Student-teacher relationships are built on trust. For example, students must trust that teachers have made appropriate decisions about the structure and content of the courses they teach, and teachers must trust that the assignments that students turn in are their own. Acts that violate this trust undermine the educational process. The Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities and The Graduate Student Supplement define various forms of Academic Dishonesty and you should make yourself familiar with these. In this class, all assignments that are turned in for a grade must represent the student’s own work. In cases where help was received, or teamwork was allowed, a notation on the assignment should indicate your collaboration.

  Violations of academic integrity may also be reported to the appropriate Dean (Dean of Students for undergraduate students or the Dean of Graduate Education for graduate students, respectively).

  If you have any question concerning this policy before submitting an assignment, please ask for clarification. In addition, you can visit the following site for more information on our Academic Integrity Policy: [Students Rights, Responsibilities, and Judicial Affairs](https://info.rpi.edu/dean-students/student-rights-responsibilities-and-judicial-affairs).
