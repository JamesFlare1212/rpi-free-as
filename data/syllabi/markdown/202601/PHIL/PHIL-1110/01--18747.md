## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

This course invites you into the world of philosophical ideas and reasoning -- to join a great conversation that has unfolded over the past 2,500 years since philosophy began in Ancient Greece. We will explore such issues as the nature of Philosophy, whether reason can allow us to make better judgments about right and wrong and about better and worse ways of living, as well as the nature of consciousness and the relationship that exists between mind and body. This course will aim to help you develop your skills in reading philosophical texts and in discussing and evaluating philosophical concepts.

## Learning Outcomes

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes (CLOs):

- 1\. Students will be able to explain and communicate the essential characteristics of Philosophy and the uses and value of Philosophy.
- 2\. Students will be able to identify the main types of arguments and explain how arguments are used in Philosophy.
- 3\. Students will be able to articulate and explain major approaches within philosophical ethics, including Utilitarianism, Deontology (duty-based ethics), Virtue Ethics, and Ethics of Care.
- 4\. In the context of ethics, students will be able to explain and utilize the distinction between intrinsic value and instrumental value.
- 5\. Students will be able to articulate such concepts and categories within Philosophy of Mind as Substance Dualism, Property Dualism, and Physicalism (e.g., Type- and Token-Identity Theories, and Eliminativism).

## Course Calendar

### Calendar Details:

This is a tentative schedule and subject to change depending on the progress of the class.  
The course calendar is as follows:  
01/12/26 - 01/22/26: Questions asked in Philosophy; Beginnings of Philosophy, basics of arguments, necessary & sufficient conditions. Knowledge and how it might be acquired, difference between knowing and thinking. Descartes' Meditation I.  
01/26/26 - 01/29/26: Theory of knowledge, continued. Value and benefits of philosophy, Plato's Allegory of the Cave. Socrates and public philosophy. What draws people to philosophy?  
02/02/26 - 02/05/26: Allegory of Cave, continued; video: "Advertising at the Edge of the Apocalypse." Intro to Ethics; Intrinsic vs. Instrumental Value; Principle of Impartiality; Bentham's classical version of Act Utilitarianism.  
02/09/26 - 02/26/26: J.S. Mill's version of Utilitarianism; Strengths and criticisms of Utilitarianism; **Review for Exam 1**. **Exam 1**  
02/16/26 - 02/19/26: J.S. Mill's version of Utilitarianism; Strengths and criticisms of Utilitarianism. Begin discussing duty-based ethics (Deontology).  
02/23/26 - 02/26/26: Kant and duty-based ethics, hypothetical vs. categorical imperatives, autonomous vs. heteronymous will, 1st and 2nd formulations of C.I. Trolley Problem and ethics settings for autonomous vehicles.  
03/02/26 - 03/05/26: Spring Break -- No Classes.  
03/09/26 - 03/12/26: Virtue ethics and ethics of care. Applied ethical issue: Abortion.  
13/16/26 - 03/19/26: **Review for Exam 2. EXAM 2.**  
03/23/26 - 03/26/26: Buddhism.  
03/30/26 - 04/02/26: Search for meaning in a world of distractions. Video: "The Puzzle of Progress." Begin unit on Philosophy of Mind. Metaphysical positions on mind-body relationship.  
04/06/26 - 04/09/26: Descartes' *Meditation II*: classic statement of interactive dualism. Neuroscience, physicalism, and disorders of mind and perception (NOVA Secrets of the Mind video).  
04/13/26 - 04/16/26: Nagel's "Knowledge Argument" and Jackson's "Qualia Argument" against Physicalism, and David Lewis' response. Artificial Intelligence: Minds & Computers.  
04/20/26 - 04/27/26: Catch-up. **Review for Exam 3. EXAM 3**.

### Academic Policies:

- ### Academic Integrity

  Student-teacher relationships are built on trust. For example, students must trust that teachers have made appropriate decisions about the structure and content of the courses they teach, and teachers must trust that the assignments that students turn in are their own. Acts that violate this trust undermine the educational process. The Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities and The Graduate Student Supplement define various forms of Academic Dishonesty and you should make yourself familiar with these. In this class, all assignments that are turned in for a grade must represent the student’s own work. In cases where help was received, or teamwork was allowed, a notation on the assignment should indicate your collaboration.

  Violations of academic integrity may also be reported to the appropriate Dean (Dean of Students for undergraduate students or the Dean of Graduate Education for graduate students, respectively).

  If you have any question concerning this policy before submitting an assignment, please ask for clarification. In addition, you can visit the following site for more information on our Academic Integrity Policy: [Students Rights, Responsibilities, and Judicial Affairs](https://info.rpi.edu/dean-students/student-rights-responsibilities-and-judicial-affairs).
