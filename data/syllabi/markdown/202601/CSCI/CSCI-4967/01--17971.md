## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

This advanced theory course studies the design of "economic algorithms," such as student-school assignment, online ad auctions, and multi-candidate elections, with an emphasis on improving their simplicity and quantifying their complexity. We will ask: How can we make the algorithms' outcomes transparent and their properties clear? What happens if participants are not perfectly rational, or the designer has only limited information or control? Topics may include market and information design, social choice theory, behavioral economics, interactive mechanisms, and communication complexity. For each topic covered, we will introduce classic results, study recent research papers, and have a problem set or two focused on theory. Prior experience with mathematical proofs (or significant effort) is required. Each student will lead one lecture to present the findings of a recent research paper, selected by students based on interest. Final course projects may be theory-focused (e.g., presenting or extending the results of a recent paper) or more-applied (e.g., running simulations, analyzing data, or building an implementation informed by course themes). This course is cross-listed as an undergraduate and graduate course.

## Learning Outcomes

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes (CLOs):

- Develop students' skills in rigorous theoretical reasoning, and evaluating and assessing theoretical models. Learn and advance recent research results in mechanism and market design.

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes Assessment Measures:

Assessments: Several theory / proof based homeworks. One "recent results presentation" where the student leads a lecture on a recent paper. And: a final course project that makes some (potentially minor) research-level progress.

## Grading Criteria

### Criteria Details:

35% homeworks. 20% "recent results presentation". 45% final course project.

- ### Academic Integrity

  Student-teacher relationships are built on trust. For example, students must trust that teachers have made appropriate decisions about the structure and content of the courses they teach, and teachers must trust that the assignments that students turn in are their own. Acts that violate this trust undermine the educational process. The Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities and The Graduate Student Supplement define various forms of Academic Dishonesty and you should make yourself familiar with these. In this class, all assignments that are turned in for a grade must represent the student’s own work. In cases where help was received, or teamwork was allowed, a notation on the assignment should indicate your collaboration.

  Violations of academic integrity may also be reported to the appropriate Dean (Dean of Students for undergraduate students or the Dean of Graduate Education for graduate students, respectively).

  If you have any question concerning this policy before submitting an assignment, please ask for clarification. In addition, you can visit the following site for more information on our Academic Integrity Policy: [Students Rights, Responsibilities, and Judicial Affairs](https://info.rpi.edu/dean-students/student-rights-responsibilities-and-judicial-affairs).
