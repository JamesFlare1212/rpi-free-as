### Additional Information for Course Information Section:

This course introduces participants to intellectual property and the commercialization of medical and pharmaceutical technology that comes from academic research. Students will become familiar with fundamental concepts that will enable them to further explore career opportunities at the intersection of science, business, and law.

This course, developed jointly between Icahn School of Medicine at Mount Sinai (ISMMS) and Rensselaer Polytechnic Institute (RPI), explores the relationship between academic research, intellectual property, and commercialization of medical and pharmaceutical technology. The course will cover patent strategy, regulatory compliance, technology development, and commercialization of an invention. Lectures will be given remotely by instructors from both RPI and ISMMS and from patent attorneys, successful biotech entrepreneurs, and Mount Sinai Innovation Partners.

## Learning Outcomes

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes (CLOs):

- • Identify and articulate unmet needs within the biomedical field through observational and analytical techniques.
- • Develop innovative solutions through structured ideation sessions and concept development methodologies.
- • Conduct comprehensive market analysis, including competitive analysis and market sizing using tools like SWOT, Porter Five Forces, TAM, SAM, and SOM.
- • Utilize the Lean LaunchPad methodology to test hypotheses, conduct customer discovery, and develop Minimum Viable Products (MVPs).
- • Prepare and present a well-researched pitch focusing on market analysis, problem identification, and proposed solutions.

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes Assessment Measures:

The course final project brings together the major topics discussed in the course – intellectual property protection, regulatory approval, reimbursement, and the commercialization process. These areas will be addressed in the course final project in the form of a written assignment and presentation (PowerPoint or similar) given to the class at the end of the semester.

The written assignment should be 10-12 pages including figures. The length requirements below are meant as a guide and may be adjusted based on the nature of the technology. All of the content must be original and sections after the scientific background should focus on the topics covered in class. **No AI generated writing will be accepted.**

Section 1 – Scientific Background (2-3 pages) --- Due Date: Feb3

- Describe the technology in terms that a scientist/engineer/clinician can understand. Provide the background for the technology and a summary of its development. This section should be written concisely and as an original narrative (do not copy and paste and do not use AI generated text) for an audience with expertise in the field/subject matter, similar to a grant or research paper.

Section 2 – Intellectual Property (3-4 pages) --- Due Date: Feb10

- Summarize the current state of the IP related to the technology (if any exists). If the technology is patented, summarize what elements are protected in the claims. If the technology is not yet patented, indicate what are the key parts of the technology that are novel and non-obvious, and why. Explain what elements of the innovation might be patentable – or likely to be patentable over prior art.
- If the technology is already patented, review and comment on the patent prosecution history and how the claims may have been amended in response to patent office rejections based upon the initial patent application.
- Perform a prior art search and discuss relevant findings. Describe how the technology differs from the closest patented/commercialized technology.

Section 3 – Regulatory (3-4 pages) --- Due Date: Feb17

- Identify and summarize any FDA guidance documents that exist for your technology or other relevant standards or regulatory documents.
- Summarize the current regulatory status of the technology. If the technology is currently being marketed, describe the regulatory path that was used to get it to market. If the technology is not yet marketed, describe the appropriate regulatory path to be taken. Discuss two similar technologies that have been marketed and any hurdles that may be unique to your technology.
- Describe the studies required to get your product ready for submission to regulatory authorities. Include milestones relevant to both scientific studies and regulatory steps.
- Figure out what FDA branch would have oversight over your technology. List the five most important questions you would ask at a Q-sub meeting to ensure your planned studies will lead to approval.
- Discuss any market exclusivity you plan to utilize, if applicable, through the FDA.

Section 4 – Commercialization (2 pages) --- Due Date: Feb 24

- Describe the ultimate product you hope to commercialize. Who is the consumer and who will pay for your technology? How will the product be paid for? Include the appropriate CPT and/or ICD codes. Determine if there is an existing National Drug Code (if appropriate).
- What are the key claims or metrics that will be used to market the product and make it economically viable? Do you need help from any third parties to get that done?

Section 5 – Timeline (1 page)

- Make a timeline (should be a graphic timeline that can also be included in the presentation) that covers technology development (including any remaining experiments), patent prosecution-related milestones, regulatory studies/approvals, and commercialization plans.
- For major milestones, discuss potential problems that would require you to adapt and identify solutions.
- Submission of a draft of this section is optional, but it should be included in your final document

References

- List references for the scientific background and any other resources (including other papers or patents discussed as prior art) utilized in other sections.
- Submission of a draft of this section is optional, but it should be included in your final document.

Final Presentation

Prepare a 15-20 minute presentation (no less than 15 minutes, no more than 20 minutes) describing your chosen technology, the product you intend to create, the relevant IP landscape, the regulatory strategy, the commercialization strategy including reimbursement, and a timeline of key events related to these topics. Presentations should include 15-20 slides, but timing and content are the most important factors. While many of these slides are similar to those found in a pitch, these presentations should include more detail in all areas including the scientific background.
