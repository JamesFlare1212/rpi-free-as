## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

This course invites you into the world of philosophical ideas and reasoning -- to join a great conversation that has unfolded over the 2,500 years since philosophy began in Ancient Greece and in certain other places around the world. We will explore such issues as the nature of Philosophy, personal identity, art and beauty, the relationship between persons and society, and meaning and purpose in life. This course will aim to help you develop your skills in reading, evaluating, and discussing philosophical texts, as well as writing philosophical prose. It is designated as Communication Intensive.

## Learning Outcomes

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes (CLOs):

- 1\. Students will be able to explain and communicate the essential characteristics of Philosophy and the uses and value of Philosophy.
- 2\. Students will be able to identify and explain several conflicting positions on what constitutes personal identity.
- 3\. Students will be able to identify several philosophical issues and positions related to art and aesthetics.
- 4\. Students will be able to identify and discuss several philosophical issues regarding the relationship of the individual to society, and regarding democracy and citizenship.
- 5\. Students will be able to identify and discuss several Eastern and Western perspectives on meaning and purpose in life.
- 6\. Students will improve their skills at philosophical discussion.
- 7\. Students will develop their skills at philosophical writing.

## Course Calendar

### Calendar Details:

The course calendar is as follows:  
Week of 1/12: Overview of course; Beginnings of Philosophy; Pre-Socratics and Socrates; Basics of arguments, necessary & sufficient conditions.  
Week of 1/20: The nature and benefits of Philosophy; Plato's Allegory of the Cave. Video: "The Examined Life: What is Philosophy?"  
Week of 1/27: Video: "Advertising at the Edge of the Apocalypse." Plato's *Apology*: Socrates as philosophy on trial. Is knowledge possible: Descartes' *Meditation I*.  
Week of 2/03: Intro to philosophical issues concerning personal identity; Descartes (*Meditation II*) and Locke on what makes you a self. **Paper 1 due**.  
Week of 2/10: Hume and Reid on personal identity; Intro to aesthetics; Plato and Aristotle on the role of art. Tragedy and comedy.  
Week of 2/17: **Review for Exam 1**.  
Week of 2/24: **EXAM 1**. Can art be defined? Parables as art.  
Week of 3/03: Spring Break -- No Classes.  
Week of 3/10: Fear and horror in fiction. Aesthetic appreciation of music and nature.  
Week of 3/17: Philosophy looks at persons and society; Socrates argues the individual is subordinate to society; J.S. Mill and classic argument for society not interfering. Contractual and Non-contractual views of society.  
Week of 3/24: Nature of democracy & comparison with other forms of government; Education for democratic citizenship. Intro to Western/Eastern perspectives on meaning & purpose; Tolstoy's journey to meaning; Viktor Frankl on finding meaning in extreme circumstances.  
Week of 3/31: Two contemporary Western philosophers consider meaning in life. Technological progress vs. life satisfaction. Video: "The Puzzle of Progress." **Paper 2 due**.  
Week of 4/07: Ancient Greco-Roman thoughts on meaning in life: Epicureanism and Stoicism. To what extent does your personal sense of meaning depend on your hopes for the future fate of humanity?  
Week of 4/14: The Buddha on how one should live to escape unsatisfactoriness of life.  
Week of 04/21: Catch-up; **Review for Exam 2**.  
Week of 4/28: **EXAM 2**

### Academic Policies:

- ### Academic Integrity

  Student-teacher relationships are built on trust. For example, students must trust that teachers have made appropriate decisions about the structure and content of the courses they teach, and teachers must trust that the assignments that students turn in are their own. Acts that violate this trust undermine the educational process. The Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities and The Graduate Student Supplement define various forms of Academic Dishonesty and you should make yourself familiar with these. In this class, all assignments that are turned in for a grade must represent the student’s own work. In cases where help was received, or teamwork was allowed, a notation on the assignment should indicate your collaboration.

  Violations of academic integrity may also be reported to the appropriate Dean (Dean of Students for undergraduate students or the Dean of Graduate Education for graduate students, respectively).

  If you have any question concerning this policy before submitting an assignment, please ask for clarification. In addition, you can visit the following site for more information on our Academic Integrity Policy: [Students Rights, Responsibilities, and Judicial Affairs](https://info.rpi.edu/dean-students/student-rights-responsibilities-and-judicial-affairs).
