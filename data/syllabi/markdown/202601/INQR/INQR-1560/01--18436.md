## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

Media and Society explores topics in media studies providing a survey of the contemporary media terrain. Through a series of readings, lectures, and activity-based media labs, students will apply and work with the most recent research within the field of media studies. Topics include media industry and the political economy of media creation, how audiences are constructed, stereotypes and regimes of representation, concepts of the media and the public sphere, the politics of media technologies, social media and branded identity, and even ecological concerns. Students leave the course with the skills to apply analytical, critical, and cultural insights about media and its impact on society.

## Course Goals

### Goals:

The primary goal of this course is to help you better understand key issues in media studies and develop as a more informed and empowered technological consumer and technological content creator. You will also develop, via an exploration of thoery, how the media of communication affects human perception, understanding, feelings, and values; and how our interaction with media facilitates or impedes our chances of survival/liberation. Finally, you will understand the sociopolitical impacts of media production and creation including power, ideology, and sense making. Students will leave the course able to explain why use, misuse, determinism and convergence of media technologies are critical social issues not simply abstract terms.

## Learning Outcomes

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes (CLOs):

- LEARNING OUTCOMES • Analyze technological artifacts. Identify specific ways technological mediation impacts knowledge, needs, emotions, values, and experiences that are both individual and social-cultural in origin. • Engage. Engage complex ideas and difficult social issues. • Explain why media plays a role in defining people’s values and truth claims. • Practice and hone your speaking and listening communication skills in both one-on-one, small group, and large group settings. • Reflect on your role as a digital citizen and understand the ways that the media industry functions. • Begin to establish a toolkit of “practices” that you can take with you for designing your own content and analyzing the work of others. • Provide evidence for your claims while using logic to build strong arguments about media.

To help you achieve the goals and outcomes for this course, I will introduce you to research and theory about how technology is thought about and used. Based on the understanding of the theoretical background that supports this course, you’ll learn strategies you can use to communicate the concepts effectively with your audience for any communication practice—oral, written, or visual—in any situation, both in classrooms and in your career. These strategies are not algorithms or recipes that can be followed blindly: They are actions and choices people routinely take and make—sometimes consciously, habitually, and/or intuitively—in communicating effectively. In your assignments, therefore, you’re expected to:

- Demonstrate critical thinking concerning technology
- Organize communication about your theories and ideas coherently and cohesively
- Produce grammatically and syntactically appropriate written and oral communication
- Appraise the communication of peers
- Revise texts as part of the writing process and in response to peer and instructor feedback
- Reflect on your own work in terms of the learning outcomes

In grading all of your work, I’ll ask four basic questions:

- What is the level of personal development gained from attention to the topics and concepts offered in lecture?

### Additional Academic Integrity Course Policy and Penalty Information:

*The* *Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities* and *The Rensselaer Graduate Student Supplement*define various forms of academic dishonesty, and you should make yourself familiar with these. In this class, all assignments that are turned in for a grade *must represent your own work*. **If you plagiarize once,** you will receive an **F on the assignment.** If you plagiarize again, you will **fail the entire course** and may be reported to the *Dean of Students* as appropriate. If you have any questions concerning this policy before submitting an assignment, please ask me for clarification. The basics: when using information from another source **(including anonymous online sources**) always cite the original text or URL and distinguish it from your own commentary. If you directly cut and paste another person’s language, you need to use it as a **quotation**. *We will talk specifically about citation styles and how to make sure that you are giving “credit” when it is required of you in spirit of scholarly collaboration.* Regarding using AI (e.g. ChatGPT or other Open AI resources): I am ok with you using AI for editing and even helping articulate your ideas in clear ways. Meaning that it ok to ask, “can you help me make this clearer” or “can you check my grammar.” I mean what’s the difference between that and spell check. Do not use it to come up with ideas, write your essay, or outline the project. The processes are important for your thinking and I only care about these aspects of your work. Remember in the end, thinking is what makes us human and our work valuable.
