## Course Goals

### Goals:

The overarching goal of this course is to prepare students to independently read, evaluate, and produce novel frontier research in health economics, with a focus on the demand for health and health-related behaviors. To achieve that goal, by the end of this course, students will be able to:

1. **Evaluate** empirical research in health economics critically, with an emphasis on identification, data limitations, and interpretation of causal effects.
2. **Integrate** economic theory and empirical evidence to analyze the demand for health and health-related behaviors.
3. **Assess** the determinants of health behaviors and health outcomes, including but not limited to the roles of prices/costs, income, education, demographic characteristics, insurance coverage, and policy incentives.
4. **Communicate** health economics research effectively, through professional presentations and scholarly discussion.
5. **Design** original empirical research that addresses important questions in health economics and contributes to the health economics literature.

## Course Content

### Content Details:

**Course Outline** (subject to change based upon events out of my control):

**“Syllabus Day” - Jan 13**

**Part 1: “Measuring” Health & Health Behaviors - Jan 16, Jan 20** What do economists mean when they talk about “health?” Can and should health be viewed through an economic lens (both via demand for health and health’s impact on individual and societal welfare, such as via health-related externalities).

**Part 1a: “What I Wished I Learned in Grad School” - Jan 23, Jan 27** One class on writing (and responding to) referee reports and another on giving research presentations.

**Part 2: Demand for Health and Health Care – Jan 30, Feb 3, Feb 6, Feb 10** Economic models of health demand. Topics include latent health, health outcomes vs. behaviors, healthcare utilization, and how measurement *choices* affect identification and interpretation. We will also consider issues of prevention vs. treatment; time costs, and uncertainty. **Student presentations:** Feb 3

**Part 3: Applications: “Risky Health Behaviors” – Feb 13, Feb 20, Feb 24, Mar 10, Mar 13** (no class Feb 17 due to President’s Day, no class Feb 27, Prof Lennon is away)

This part of the class examines how individuals trade off health production and risk to produce health **outcomes**. Topics include the economics of drug use (nicotine, alcohol, etc.), obesity/diet, sleep, road safety, exercise, environmental conditions, etc. **Student presentations:** Feb 20

**Part 4: Applications: Insurance, Incentives & Moral Hazard - Mar 17, Mar 20, Mar 24, Mar 27** This part of the class examines the effects of health insurance on **behavior**. Topics include moral hazard, selection, effects of recent insurance policy reforms on health outcomes, etc. **Student presentations:** Mar 20

**Part 5: Applications: Policy, Inequality, and Social Environments – Mar 31, Apr 3, Apr 7, Apr 14, Apr 17** (no class Apr 10, Prof Lennon is away) This part of the class examines how behavior and outcomes are determined by broader social and physical environments. Topics can include public health effects, history, socio-economic status (income, education, place) and health, race and gender differences, discrimination in health care settings, etc. **Student presentations:** Apr 3

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes Assessment Measures:

The grading scale is 90-100 A, 80-89.9 B, 70-79.9 C, 60-69.9 D, <60 F. Plus/minus grading will be used in this course with plus grades will be assigned for scores in the top 3 points of a grade range while minus grades will be assigned for scores in the bottom 3 points of a grade range. For example, 82.6 points out of 100 is a “B minus” while 89.6 is a “B plus.” If your grade is not otherwise affected by the attendance policy above, your overall score is determined by the summation of your performance in the following areas;

1. **“Referee Reports” (40 points, 10 points per report)**

You will be assigned four different papers to “review.” Prior to the first report due date, one class will be dedicated to the “art and science” of referee reports, including how to write and respond to such reports. I will also provide a separate document that explains the assignment in greater detail. This is a skill that is often overlooked in graduate school but is critical to your success as a scholar. Graded as check/check minus (10 pts, 5 pts). Addresses Learning Goals (LG): 1, 2, & 4.

2. **Presentations (40 points, 10 points each)**

There will be four classes where each student briefly presents up to three assigned readings. These will generally be scheduled in the second class on a given topic area (see schedule at the end of this document). Before the first of these, I will take one class to talk about presenting (health) economics research. Again, I will provide a separate document that explains the assignment in greater detail. Learning how to present a paper in 15-20 minutes is an important professional skill. These presentations will be graded on a check/check minus basis, earning 10 points or 5 points as appropriate. LG: 1, 3, & 4.

3. **Research Extension Proposal (20 points)**

In lieu of a “final paper,” students will complete a research extension proposal. The goal here is to flex your research muscles, but to avoid an in-depth paper because of your interests being primarily outside of health economics. The proposal should identify an important issue in health economics and outline a credible empirical strategy and data source to address it. However, there are additional constraints. You ***must*** use an identification strategy (i.e., a source of exogenous variation) from a paper we have studied in class. Further information will be provided in a later document, talk to me if expectations are not clear. With that “borrowed” empirical strategy you may propose a *new* research question (e.g., a new outcome, ) or an extension that improves upon the existing paper (e.g., some additional analyses, perhaps using different data, that would make the paper more credible). Students are not expected to implement the analysis; instead, the emphasis is on research design, identification, and feasibility, reflecting the early stages of a PhD research project. LG: 2, 3, & 5.

**Late Policy:** If you submit an assignment after the due date, there is a 50% penalty starting immediately after the deadline passes. The penalty increases by 25% per day (or any part thereof). Therefore, any assignment that is more than 48 hours late earns a grade of zero. I understand that sometimes “life” happens. The goal of this policy, however, is to help you develop the skill to plan effectively, allowing time for unexpected events. Leaving assignments and readings until the last moment is a risk that you take at your own peril.

- ### Academic Integrity

  Student-teacher relationships are built on trust. For example, students must trust that teachers have made appropriate decisions about the structure and content of the courses they teach, and teachers must trust that the assignments that students turn in are their own. Acts that violate this trust undermine the educational process. The Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities and The Graduate Student Supplement define various forms of Academic Dishonesty and you should make yourself familiar with these. In this class, all assignments that are turned in for a grade must represent the student’s own work. In cases where help was received, or teamwork was allowed, a notation on the assignment should indicate your collaboration.

  Violations of academic integrity may also be reported to the appropriate Dean (Dean of Students for undergraduate students or the Dean of Graduate Education for graduate students, respectively).

  If you have any question concerning this policy before submitting an assignment, please ask for clarification.
