### Additional Information for Course Information Section:

This course provides students with a wide-ranging understanding of the environmental and social context of food, agriculture, and hunger. Drawing primarily on concepts and research from political ecology, critical food studies, and post-colonial studies, the class will use food, land, and nourishment as tools for examining structures of power, difference, and inequality across the economic scales of our lives - from our gut microbiomes to the global markets of the food system.

Each of you in this class already knows a lot about the agri-food systems that shape our lives. Maybe you have felt the impact of rising prices of just about everything at grocery stores and restaurants. Likely you know someone who works to manage their diabetes, their food allergies, their gut issues, or their sodium intake. You may have learned about nitrogen fertilizer run-off in rivers and streams, and the anoxic dead zones growing in already-overfished and warming oceans. You may have an elder or ancestor who faced famine and now encourages you to clean our plates. You, like 44 million other Americans, may experience food insecurity* this year. At the time of writing, nearly 300 million people across the world face acute levels of food crisis or famine - including more than half of the populations of Sudan, Yemen, and Haiti, and all of Gaza’s 2 million+ residents, most of whom are children. The impacts of hunger are recorded in our bodies intergenerationally; food, farms, and famine are political terrains that shape our actual bones. Why is it that when farmers produce far more than enough calories to feed each of the 8.1 billion people on this planet, so many of us are unable to access safe, nutritious, familiar, and meaningful foods? Why is so much of the most readily available food seemingly so bad for our bodies and for the earth? Is there a better way, and what might it take?

## Learning Outcomes

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes (CLOs):

- Generally interpret a range of agricultural landscapes, practices, ecologies, and political economic conditions.
- Use social theories and concepts to understand contemporary conditions and trends for food production, distribution, and consumption.
- Critically analyze and assess interdisciplinary research, media, technology and policy related to the agri-food system.
- Generate original hypotheses about the agri-food system—including possible solutions to problems related to hunger, environmental degradation, or rural poverty.

## Policies

### Academic Policies:

- ### Academic Integrity

  Student-teacher relationships are built on trust. For example, students must trust that teachers have made appropriate decisions about the structure and content of the courses they teach, and teachers must trust that the assignments that students turn in are their own. Acts that violate this trust undermine the educational process. The Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities and The Graduate Student Supplement define various forms of Academic Dishonesty and you should make yourself familiar with these. In this class, all assignments that are turned in for a grade must represent the student’s own work. In cases where help was received, or teamwork was allowed, a notation on the assignment should indicate your collaboration.

  Violations of academic integrity may also be reported to the appropriate Dean (Dean of Students for undergraduate students or the Dean of Graduate Education for graduate students, respectively).

  If you have any question concerning this policy before submitting an assignment, please ask for clarification. In addition, you can visit the following site for more information on our Academic Integrity Policy: [Students Rights, Responsibilities, and Judicial Affairs](https://info.rpi.edu/dean-students/student-rights-responsibilities-and-judicial-affairs).
