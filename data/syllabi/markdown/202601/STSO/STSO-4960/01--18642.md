## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

**Course Overview**

This course examines how societies have envisioned and constructed their futures—shaped by environmental and technological change, social upheavals, and cultural narratives. Drawing from philosophy, literature, the arts and social science, the course analyzes how such notions of the future – including collective imaginaries of threats, opportunities, and crises – shape policies, social organization, and scientific pursuits. Students will consider how cultural and technological expectations enable or limit social change. Finally, they will build on their expertise developed in the course to explore themes of sustainability and speculative design as vital tools for imagining and shaping diverse futures.

Students will be expected to develop analytical and creative writing projects that integrate approaches across the arts, sciences, and humanities, and which draw upon the historical and cross-cultural record.

## Learning Outcomes

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes (CLOs):

- 1\) Concept work: We will question central concepts we often take for granted, such as nature, culture and the everyday. To do this, we will analyze, synthesize, organize, and respond to college-level writing by authors who help us understand how these important concepts have been fashioned over time. This will help us pay attention to our surroundings in new ways.
- 2\) Community: We will participate in larger conversations about everyday life, the future and the role of the observer by proposing new arguments and ideas that respond productively to the arguments of others in formal presentations and in-class discussions. As we discover the pleasures of being part of a community of thinkers and creatives, we will learn the appropriate use of the work of others and, where necessary, specific citation practices.
- 3\) Agency: We will cultivate a sense of agency when tackling local and global issues by looking at the concrete world around us. We will also cultivate a sense of agency as writers by embracing writing as a series of rhetorical choices within larger situational contexts.
- 4\) Inspiration: We will discover ways art, including written works, can help us imagine things we never thought possible. In turn, we will spur curiosity, openness, and critical thinking by improving our ability to communicate effectively using writing, other media, and speech, considering audience and purpose.
- 5\) Creative play: We will get comfortable with the uncertain, destabilizing, sometimes painful, often exciting, creative, iterative processes between writing, thinking, and making. These processes include noticing, underlining, brainstorming, outlining, communicating, and revising.
- 6\) Clear communication: Throughout the course, we will continue to enhance our understanding of the central place of writing and communication in the learning process, in academia, in the art and design world, and in life in general. We will refer to these skills and objectives throughout the semester.

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes Assessment Measures:

**Grading & Assignments**

All education research supports that regular, steady work helps students develop the skills needed to succeed in their educational programs. This runs counter to ideas about innate skill and aptitude. Instead, practice is the goal. This course is designed to help you practice critical reading, research, and writing skills.

**Reading Responses** (30pts; 2 points each) – Students are encouraged to complete a total of 15 reading guides over the course of the semester (there are more readings than required Reading Responses, so students have some latitude in which readings they submit Responses for). Reading Responses must be turned in to the instructor at the beginning of class in which the reading is discussed. Late Reading Responses will not be accepted for credit. Reading Response guides are included as Appendix A (for non-fiction), B (for fiction). Students will be asked to read from their Responses in class each meeting; you must have your Responses on hand to discuss them during class to receive full credit.

**Please note: You do not need to complete all of the readings & viewings to receive a passing grade in this course. The syllabus is designed to allow for student choice and to facilitate your interests. This means that you can skip individual readings or whole weeks’ worth of content. But this also means that you need to plan appropriately. DO NOT wait until there are only the required number of Responses remaining; life events happen and if you regularly turn in Responses, you will have a buffer in case anything comes up.**

**Contribution** (20pts) - You must carefully read and think about the assigned text before each meeting so that you can be an active contributor to class discussions. When you contribute to class discussions, it is important that you address your peers as well as the professor. You must also participate actively in the peer review process.

When courses are held online:

\* Students are expected to be actively engaged in the online classroom. Please keep your video camera on. You may use the chat, raise hand, unmute your microphones and speak up, etc. when you have questions or would like to participate. If you cannot turn your camera or mic on, please get in touch with me prior to class.

\* Technology Resources: Student must have a working computer with a video camera, speaker and a microphone, and a working internet connection that allows them to be on video. Students should test their equipment before joining class. Students needing an accommodation should contact me at the beginning of the semester to discuss and work out needed adjustments. * Active engagement is an essential component of the learning process. Participation in courses includes active reading and discussion during class. Students are expected to engage as appropriate several times a session.

I evaluate contribution and participation according to the rubric below.

| B | Student demonstrates good preparation and contributes to discussions in relatively frequently and thoughtfully, engaging in class in an ongoing manner. |
| C | Student demonstrates adequate preparation and contributes thoughtfully but only when called on, infrequently engaging in class voluntarily. |
| D | Student does not engage in class, contributing little and only when called on. Student demonstrates that s/he is unprepared or inattentive (e.g. texting, chatting, or online). |
| F | Student is absent (unexcused), asleep, or present and disruptive. |

Peer Review -Peer reviews should be constructive based on the objectives listed in the assignments. You may use the General Peer-Review Checklist I will hand out as part of the tips and observations you offer your peers. Be sure to comment on the content and coherence of the argument and ideas, the organization of the larger paper, and the clarity of writing.

**Fieldnotes** (10pts) – Students observe their surroundings and speculate on what will change and how (landscapes, behaviors, objects). Students keep notes on theses detailed observations throughout the semester. These notes will be submitted twice (5pts mid semester and 5pts end of semester). They can be in paper form (scanned) or digital. You will be graded on completion. However, to receive a complete grade, notes must be detailed, creative (sketches are allowed and should be accompanied by a written description), and at times, connected to course content (practice drawing lines with concepts, in other words, connect the dots).

**Futures Proposal** (10pts - 5pts for write up and 5pts for presentation) – In groups of 2. Students will propose a new behavior or object. You will produce a three-page explanation of and justification for this proposal and present it to the class. Your presentation should include slides.

**Final Paper** (30pts) – Writing the Implosion – and then SPECULATE. This paper is an analysis of an object. It explores the connections brought forth by that object and then offers an argument for the future of that object. For this piece, you will research an object in the world and the world in an object. We will break down the different components of this project together, based on Dumit and Haraway’s work. 5pts of your final paper grade will be based on a 5-7 min oral presentation to the class. We will review presentation tips in class. 25pts will be based on your writing, and your ability to craft a compelling argument, making use of sources we’ve read in class as well as additional research.

Style Matters: All written work should be double-spaced, 12-point font, in Times New Roman, with 1 inch margins on all sides, and page numbers. Citation should look like this: (Author Year: Page), which follows Chicago style. Using a reference manager (e.g. Zotero) will make this easy. Failure to meet these standards will result in a reduced grade.

## Policies

### Attendance Policy:

Attendance Students are expected always to be present during class. Attendance will be taken at the beginning of each class. Excellence in submitted work will not make up for delinquency in attendance. **More than three unexcused absences will result in a lowering of your final course grade by one letter. More than seven absences will result in the failure of the course. All excused absences must be delivered to the professor via the Office of Student Life. If you will be absent, please let me know if possible.

Academic Integrity The Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities and The Rensselaer Graduate Student Supplement define various forms of Academic Dishonesty and procedures for responding to them. All forms are violations of the trust between students and teachers. Student-teacher relationships are built on trust. For example, students must trust that teachers have made appropriate decisions about the structure and content of the courses they teach, and teachers must trust that the assignments that students turn in are their own performance. Acts that violate this trust undermine the educational process.

The Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities and The Rensselaer Graduate Student Supplement define various forms of Academic Dishonesty, and you should make yourself familiar with these. In this class, all assignments that are turned in for a grade must represent the student’s own work. In cases where help was received, or teamwork was allowed, a notation on the assignment should indicate your collaboration. Submission of any assignment that is in violation of this policy will result in a penalty. If found in violation of the academic honesty policy, students may be subject to two types of penalty. The instructor administers an academic grade penalty, and the student is reported to the Dean of Students or the Dean of Graduate Education as appropriate. The first violation results in a 0 grade for that assignment. The second violation results in failure of the course. If you have any questions concerning this policy before submitting an assignment, please ask for clarification.

That said, we will open up conversation about when it may or may not be appropriate for you to make use of these advanced tools.

Chosen Names and Personal Pronouns Everyone has the right to be addressed by the name and pronouns that correspond to their gender identity, including non-binary pronouns, for example: they/them/theirs, ze/zir/zirs, etc. Rosters do not list gender or pronouns so you may be asked to indicate the pronouns you use so that I don't make assumptions based on your name and/or appearance /self-presentation (you are not obligated to do so). I will not however, tolerate repeated comments which disrespect or antagonize students who have indicated pronouns or a chosen name. Chosen name and personal pronouns may evolve over time, so if at any point during the semester you would like to be addressed differently, please let me know.
