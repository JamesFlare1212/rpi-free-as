### Additional Information for Course Information Section:

**Course Description**

This course will support the development of your senior thesis, the culminating project for the major in Science, Technology, and Society, and the major in Sustainability Studies. Building on the work you began last semester, we will conduct proposed research, analyze our findings, and complete a thesis. This work will culminate in either a) a refined complete thesis, or b) a publicly engaging artifact that demonstrates the significance of your research findings. We will support the work of the group as a writing community throughout this iterative research and writing process.

**Course Overview**

Each of us will conduct empirical research that contributes to research in the fields of Science and Technology Studies and Sustainability Studies. We will engage with the concepts and methods, as well as demonstrate our contributions to these interdisciplinary fields. Our class meetings will be formatted as a writing workshop. We will discuss our shared readings about the research and writing process, conduct regular peer review and writing exercises, and share our research and writing approaches, challenges and strategies. The semester will be broken down into three main sections. First, an initial period designed to read, discuss, and lay the groundwork for the data collection and conveying research findings in the thesis. The next stage of the semester will involve producing and submitting each section of the thesis. In the last stage of the semester, you will decide which final artifact you want to take with you as you leave college and launch into whatever comes next. You may decide to revise your thesis draft and submit a refined complete thesis. Or you may decide to produce a publicly engaging artifact of your own design.

Research and writing inform each other and will be most successfully accomplished in tandem. While we will focus on completing different sections of the thesis one at a time, in practice this involves a weekly schedule that includes, 1) data collection, 2) preliminary analysis, 3) reading and revisiting literature, and 4) writing. It is necessary to dig deeper into the pages you’ve produced as you collect new evidence, reflect on debates in the field, confront critiques of your work, and deepen your analysis.

## Course Goals

### Goals:

**Skills and objectives** that encompass our course goals, are the following:

1\) **Concept work:** We will question central concepts we often take for granted, such as nature, culture and the everyday, as well as other concepts central to your individual projects. To do this, we will analyze, synthesize, organize, and respond to college-level writing by authors who help us understand how these important concepts have been fashioned over time. This will help us pay attention to our surroundings and the data we’ve collected in new ways.

2\) **Community:** We will participate in larger conversations about everyday life, the future and the role of the observer by proposing new arguments and ideas that respond productively to the arguments of others in formal presentations and in-class discussions. As we discover the pleasures of being part of a community of thinkers and creatives, we will learn the appropriate use of the work of others and, where necessary, specific citation practices.

3\) **Agency:** We will cultivate a sense of agency when tackling local and global issues by looking at the concrete world around us. We will also cultivate a sense of agency as writers by embracing writing as a series of rhetorical choices within larger situational contexts.

4\) **Inspiration:** We will discover ways art, including written works, can help us imagine things we never thought possible. In turn, we will spur curiosity, openness, and critical thinking by improving our ability to communicate effectively using writing, other media, and speech, considering audience and purpose.

5\) **Creative play:** We will get comfortable with the uncertain, destabilizing, sometimes painful, often exciting, creative, iterative processes between writing, thinking, and making. These processes include noticing, underlining, brainstorming, outlining, communicating, and revising.

6\) **Clear communication**: Throughout the course, we will continue to enhance our understanding of the central place of writing and communication in the learning process, in academia, in the art and design world, and in life in general.

We will refer to these skills and objectives throughout the semester.

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes Assessment Measures:

**Additional notes for the assignments:**

**Reading Responses**: Students are encouraged to complete a total of 15 reading guides over the course of the semester (there are more readings than required Reading Responses, so students have some latitude in which readings they submit Responses for). Reading Responses must be turned in to the instructor at the beginning of class in which the reading is discussed. Late Reading Responses will not be accepted for credit. Reading Responses for this class should address how the reading helps you with your process. Students will be asked to read from their Responses in class each meeting; you must have your Responses on hand to discuss them during class to receive full credit.

**Writing Community Reflections:**

Twice during the semester, we will take class time to reflect on our writing community

experiences. This will be a time to consider your work not as an individual, but as a supportive and supported member of a community of researchers and writers. You will reflect on your work in this community, what you’ve learned from the bumps along the way in the research, writing, and revision process.

Format: 2 paragraphs- 2 pages, hand-written or typed, submitted in hard copy at the end of

class as well as on the LMS.

Writing Expectations: The expected format for the senior thesis is as follows:

Length: Thirty to fifty double-spaced pages.

Typical sections of a senior thesis (with approximate lengths for a 30-page paper) are listed below in this syllabus to be used as a template.

Style Matters: All written work should be double-spaced, 12-point font, in Times New Roman, with 1-inch margins on all sides, and page numbers. Citation should look like this: (Author Year: Page), which follows Chicago style. Failure to meet these standards will result in a reduced grade.

Contemporary standards of language use encourage gender-neutral language. Male-gender pronouns or words like “man” to refer to everyone are not empirically accurate. Your thesis should use gender-neutral language.

**Project Advising:** Students are required to work with another STS professor as a second mentor, to receive additional guidance and advice on the development of the project. The advisor will be consulted on the final project grade.

**Contribution**

You must carefully read and think about the assigned text before each meeting so that you can be an active contributor to class discussions. When you contribute to class discussions, it is important that you address your peers as well as the professor. You must also participate actively in the peer review process.

When courses are held online:

\* Students are expected to be actively engaged in the online classroom. Please keep your video camera on. You may use the chat, raise hand, unmute your microphones and speak up, etc. when you have questions or would like to participate. If you cannot turn your camera or mic on, please get in touch with me prior to class.

\* Technology Resources: Student must have a working computer with a video camera, speaker and a microphone, and a working internet connection that allows them to be on video. Students should test their equipment before joining class. Students needing an accommodation should contact me at the beginning of the semester to discuss and work out needed adjustments. * Active engagement is an essential component of the learning process. Participation in courses includes active reading and discussion during class. Students are expected to engage as appropriate several times a session.

**The final project grade will be based on the instructor’s evaluation of the completed senior thesis. A complete senior thesis is due in class on April 1. You will receive detailed critical feedback from Prof. Dib, your advisor, and a peer reviewer on this version of your thesis. You will then have about a month to revise, and a final version of the thesis is due on April 26, the last day of class. The evaluation of your thesis will revolve around the following questions, provided here with additional explanation:**

1. **Does the thesis make good use of STS and/or Sustainability Studies concepts and make an original contribution to our understanding of the social dimensions of science, technology, and/or the environment? The senior thesis is a lengthy research paper project in the field of your major, so must address and use appropriate theories and concepts.**

1. **Is the main research question and argument clearly articulated, and consistent throughout the paper? The thesis should explain the question(s) that you started with and your answer to the question(s).**
2. **Is sufficient empirical evidence examined, and brought in to support the main argument? The thesis should clearly explain your main findings and arguments, describing the evidence that supports your conclusions.**
3. **Were the methods employed appropriate for the topic the methods you used to investigate those questions? The thesis should describe a well thought out research design. Methods of data collection and analysis should be justified in the thesis and well-suited to the chosen topic and research question.**
4. **Was a substantial amount of original research conducted? The thesis should be richly substantiated with empirical evidence.**
5. **Are the findings of other scholars drawn on to support the main argument? The thesis should both draw upon and attempt to contribute to the relevant, peer-reviewed social science and humanities literature. Your bibliography should include, at minimum, 20 references, at least 7 of which are peer reviewed publications.**
6. **Are possible counter arguments effectively acknowledged? The thesis should grapple with possible alternative interpretations of the evidence and opposing theoretical perspectives.**
7. **Is the significance of the research findings clearly explained? Typically, a good conclusion discusses the broader relevance of the research and why it matters.**
8. **Is the paper well organized and written? It is a good idea to get help at the writing and success center to improve your writing. Be sure to follow the formatting guidelines above.**

**The instructor will provide a letter grade based on the evaluation of the finished thesis on the above criteria. Letter grades indicate the following:**

**A = Outstanding in all respects; YES to all questions above**

**B = Very good work; YES to most of the questions above**

**C = Satisfactory work; YES to about half of the questions above**

**D = Passable work; YES to only a few of the questions above**

**F = Unacceptable work; YES to only one or none of the questions above**

**Students will make a final presentation of their research at the end of the semester. The presentation will be 10 minutes in length and summarize the major findings and significance of the research.**

Academic Integrity The Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities and The Rensselaer Graduate Student Supplement define various forms of Academic Dishonesty and procedures for responding to them. All forms are violations of the trust between students and teachers. Student-teacher relationships are built on trust. For example, students must trust that teachers have made appropriate decisions about the structure and content of the courses they teach, and teachers must trust that the assignments that students turn in are their own performance. Acts that violate this trust undermine the educational process.

The Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities and The Rensselaer Graduate Student Supplement define various forms of Academic Dishonesty, and you should make yourself familiar with these. In this class, all assignments that are turned in for a grade must represent the student’s own work. In cases where help was received, or teamwork was allowed, a notation on the assignment should indicate your collaboration. Submission of any assignment that is in violation of this policy will result in a penalty. If found in violation of the academic honesty policy, students may be subject to two types of penalty. The instructor administers an academic grade penalty, and the student is reported to the Dean of Students or the Dean of Graduate Education as appropriate. The first violation results in a 0 grade for that assignment. The second violation results in failure of the course. If you have any questions concerning this policy before submitting an assignment, please ask for clarification.
