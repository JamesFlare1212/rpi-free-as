## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

It’s hot in here! The water is rising, there’s plastic in the fish, the pipes are breaking, and the storms are getting stronger. Our communities face a growing number of challenges, from the extreme (recovering from an oil spill, a hurricane, a factory closure, or a heat wave) to the mundane (repairing a roof, turning on the air conditioning, finding an apartment, taking a shower, breathing outside). For sustainability minded people - or for anyone paying attention - we see distressing daily signals of many interlocking ecological, social, and political crises. Why is it like this, what should we do, and how shall we do it?

This undergraduate course provides students with a wide-ranging understanding of the relationships between people, nature, power and difference. Drawing primarily on concepts and research from the fields of critical geography, anthropology, and postcolonial studies, and utilizing the analytical methods of political ecology and political economy, this class will prepare students to engage complex, intersectional socio-ecological issues related to uneven patterns of development, exposure, vulnerability, and opportunity.

## Learning Outcomes

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes (CLOs):

- Apply analytical tools from research in/on environmental sociology, critical geography, political ecology, political economy, and environmental justice to emergent environmental issues and strategies.
- Formulate timely research questions about social, environmental, and economic crises and development strategies at local, regional and global scales.
- Orient within emergent environmental issues by researching critical histories of places, regions, industries, economies, and development patterns.
- Communicate about the environment and society to a broader audience.

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes Assessment Measures:

Students enrolled in this course should be prepared to do a great deal of reading, writing, and speaking in small groups and as a class. This course is aimed at preparing students to do upper level work in the critical social sciences, to pursue graduate or professional research related to issues in sustainability or just transition or hold leadership in real life decisions as engineers and planners.

- ### Academic Integrity

  Student-teacher relationships are built on trust. For example, students must trust that teachers have made appropriate decisions about the structure and content of the courses they teach, and teachers must trust that the assignments that students turn in are their own. Acts that violate this trust undermine the educational process. The Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities and The Graduate Student Supplement define various forms of Academic Dishonesty and you should make yourself familiar with these.
