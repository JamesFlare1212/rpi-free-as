## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

This course provides a comprehensive introduction to the applications of quantum computing, specifically tailored for students in business, finance, ITWS, and management. Students will explore the fundamental concepts of quantum computing, including qubits, superposition, and entanglement, quantum circuits, and the mathematical foundations of linear algebra and probability that are needed to start with Quantum Computing and apply them on use-cases. The curriculum emphasizes practical, hands-on experience through lecture and lab sessions using IBM's Qiskit SDK on both quantum simulators and Rensselaer Polytechnic Institute's (RPI) on-campus 127-qubit IBM Quantum System One. A significant focus is placed on analyzing real-world business and finance use cases, such as portfolio optimization, risk analysis, and quantum machine learning, preparing students to understand and leverage the next generation of computational technology.

Introduce students to relevant methods to recognize and apply quantum computing algorithms, techniques and interpretation to develop students' strategic thinking skills, combined with a solid technical foundation. Develop ability to apply critical and analytical methods to formulate and solve science, engineering, medical, and business problems with an applied approach to evaluate the applicability of the relavent quantum computing techniques and benchmark their results with classical computing equivalents. Students will examine QC applications with examples using modern QC simulator and run those applications on a real QC (IBM Rensselaer) to develop their technical capabilities and discuss the results through presentations and posters. By the end of the course, students can effectively communicate analytic findings to non-specialists via visual presentation such as posters.

Current QC enterprises are developing fast and moving toward analytics-driven approaches for core business functions. In the government and corporations, QC and their applications become a prominent topic with respect to cybersecurity and many other related filed. The investments in advanced quantum computing capabilities could potentially be more broadly leveraged today and greater than any prior government investments in computing. Emphasis is now placed on disruptive quantum computing technologies have become priority in financial, health and defense sectors.

This course is designed for juniors, seniors, and graduate students from the Lally School of Management (particularly Business Analytics), the Information Technology and Web Science (ITWS) program, and the Master of Science in IT program. It is also open to all RPI students with the requisite background who are interested in the practical application of quantum computers. The curriculum is structured to be accessible and valuable to a mixed cohort of students with both business and technical backgrounds, reflecting the interdisciplinary nature of the emerging quantum industry. This course will be a stepping-stone to existing other quantum courses at RPI, and we are designing this course to “fill-the-gap” of essential foundation needed to have a smooth learning process for quantum computing especially for non-Computer Science or EECS major students.

Upon successful completion of this course, students will be able to:

- Explain the fundamental principles of quantum computing, contrasting them with classical computation in both conceptual and mathematical terms. Students will articulate the concepts of qubits, superposition, and entanglement and identify the classes of problems where quantum computers offer a potential advantage.
- Apply the mathematical formalisms of linear algebra to describe quantum states and operations. This includes proficiency with vector spaces, tensor products, and Dirac (bra-ket) notation to model the behavior of quantum systems precisely for a given problem.
- Construct, execute, and analyze quantum circuits using the Qiskit Software Development Kit (SDK) also IBM Quantum Machine Learning (QML) Ecosystem. Students will gain hands-on proficiency in programming QML algorithms and running them on both a laptop-based IBM Quantum Simulator and the on-campus RPI IBM Quantum System One hardware, interpreting the probabilistic results of quantum measurements.
- Evaluate the application of key quantum algorithms to solve specific, high-value problems in finance and business. This includes formulating well-known business quantum applications in the areas of related problems, such as portfolio optimization and risk analysis using/applying algorithms like the Quantum Approximate Optimization Algorithm (QAOA), Variational Quantum Eigensolvers (VQE) etc.
- Students will learn to assess the current development state of QC, what the potential is with QC, and how it can be applied in industry, like finance/business, et,c and also the limitations of near-term quantum hardware.
- Students will be able to distinguish between realistic applications and industry hype (quantum-

hype), and what realistic applications are, and where this current QC development is heading,

based on industries like IBM, Google, Microsoft's development in this field.

## Course Goals

### Goals:

- Introduce students to relevant Quantum Computing methods to recognize and apply algorithms, techniques, and interpretation of the results.
- To develop students' strategic thinking skills, combined with a solid technical foundation of quantum computing applications and model-driven quantum inspired machine learning models.
- Develop the ability to apply critical and analytical methods to formulate and solve science, engineering, medical, and business problems using applicable quantum and classical computing methods.
- Students will examine real-world examples using modern quantum computer simulators as well as learn how conduct computation on a real quantum computer and build applications.
- By the end of the course, students can effectively communicate analytic findings to non-specialists.

***A special note on plagiarism.***

Please see the definition of plagiarism given below, as outlined in The Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights. To be clear, any time material that is not the students’ own is used, it should be in quotes and contain a citation. If a student paraphrases work from another, it also requires a citation. Furthermore, even when citations are used, others’ work cannot be used as a student response on an assignment. In the case of software, we will address the topic of “copying” which is considered a common practice in software development in relation to ethics and conduct of software development.

***“Plagiarism.*** Plagiarism is defined as representing the work or words of another as one's own through the omission of acknowledgment or reference. Examples include using sentences verbatim from a published source in a term paper without appropriate referencing, or presenting as one's own the detailed argument of a published source, or presenting as one's own electronically or digitally enhanced graphic representations from any form of media.*”* - ACADEMIC INTEGRITY (Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights & Responsibilities, November 2017 rev.)
