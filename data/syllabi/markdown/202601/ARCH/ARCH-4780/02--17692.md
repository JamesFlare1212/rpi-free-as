### Additional Course Description :

**1.0** **Overview** Cinetecture: No Location/On Location/South by Southwest

**Cinetecture** is a vertical studio focused on the intersection of film, architecture and urban studies. Key themes include:

**Theories of Space and Time:** How film manipulates the perception of space and time compared to the physical experience of architecture.

**Urban Projections:** The way cities and the built environment are represented, interpreted, and utilized in moving images.

**Aesthetics and Design:** Analyzing the architectural aesthetics within films and how cinematic techniques (camerawork, editing, lighting, set design, sound design) create specific spatial and emotional effects.

**Historical Context**: Examining the historical discourses around the emergence of cinema and its influence on architecture in the late 19th, early 20th and early 21st centuries.

**Experiential Design:** Exploring immersive encounters between people, media and places, often involving new media and digital fabrication techniques.

**Architecture to Cinema Analogies**

**Plan**=Plot/Order

**Elevation**=Cast/Character/Surface

**Section**=Script/Mis en Scene

**Exploded View**=Special Effects

Modernity in architecture and the emergence of photography are concurrent events, spurred by the industrial revolution in Europe and The United States. The first known photograph depicted a building as seen from another building. And for 130 years, cinema—photography’s lively cousin—has existed alongside, in collaboration with, has mimicked and has imagined *architecture.* Even when a story is filmed on location, there is the inescapable rectangle in various proportions depending on the camera aspect ratio, linking the viewer back to a mere portion of the visual field. It is reduced and cropped from our own ovoid periphery, which follows the compound curvature of the human eye. In this sense, the projected light of cinema is always a meeting place of the technological and the biological.

Whether images are produced in the highly controlled laboratories we call *studios* or generated from *1s and zeros* in the digital realm, architecture is a necessity for these processes. The original term *camera* is derived from the Italian word for ‘room’ or chamber. Light enters the camera, exposing sensitive chemical surfaces in little rooms. The physical chemistry of coated plastic has largely been eclipsed by digital means, but the principle is the same: light reveals and is captured to be manipulated later in post-production. And when shot on a location like a preexisting building or on an artificial set (constructed with the express purpose of continuing the illusion of a story), architecture serves that story.

Cinema can be understood as the ultimate dance of the representation of space in the non-space of the flat picture plane: viewable, ideally, with the controlled atmosphere of yet another architecture: the space of the movie theater, an extension of traditional live theater. This is a continuation of one of the main ideas of the Renaissance: to place the viewer at the center of a window onto the world.

Finally, there is the dance of truth and desire invoked by the subjectivity of architecture: what does it mean to look up or down, go from here to there and experience the space of a room responding to the seer who sees it? We measure the spaces of these kinetic stories to our own experience of the world.

And when it doesn’t line up with that, we declare it a dream, a fantasy, and we are taken out of *this* *world.* Escape, passage, art, mental health break: cinema means different things to different viewers.

Cinema is a vast spectrum of social structures, from the production of films to the public gathering of audiences. In this course we will look at the microcosm of film production, its spaces, sets, locations, stages, editing suites and architectures where many people assemble—in the dark—to experience lives imagined, worlds conjured, discussions sparked. Welcome to the in-between realm of **Cinetecture.**

- **1** **On Cinema**

What does it mean to participate in cinema in 2026? We are now deeply entrenched in the era of the small screen. But for a medium, artform and industry that began small (Edison *Videolas,* private viewing booths and small-scale projections), cinema quickly became a massive endeavor, both in the making and the viewing. As Vaudeville-era theaters converted to project light, screens grew, formats multiplied (and changed proportions) and the first space race was on: who would tell the biggest stories and showcase them to the most people? I remember when I first saw David Lynch’s *Blue Velvet* (1986) on the biggest screen in Manhattan: 3,000 people quietly exited the theater, all of us wondering what we had just witnessed. Many of us were forever changed that day. It happened to me again with Steven Spielberg’s *Schindler’s List* (1993), Douglas Sirk’s *Imitation of Life* (1959) and Hitchcock’s *Vertigo* (1958).

**1.2 Sound + Vision**

Carrying certain traditions over from live theater, organists accompanied early silent films, as did small musical groups, and occasionally, entire orchestras. Acoustic design for theaters advanced quickly after Al Jolson’s *The Jazz Singer* introduced ‘the talkies’ to viewers in 1928. In 1929, Hitchcock created Britain’s first talkie, *Blackmail.*

These advances in acoustics accompanied many changes in formats: early films were almost square (3:4 proportions), and when VISTAVISION (between 1:66 and 2.00:01) was introduced in 1954, audiences became even more enveloped by the imagery. CINERAMA soon followed (2.59:1 and 2.65:1) and later IMAX in the 2000s. To see a film on a periphery-filling screen and an advanced sound system (DOLBY was introduced in 1965 and THX in 1983, DTS in 1990), makes for a sensory overload: IMAX has evolved to virtually place the viewer in the eyepiece of the DP (director of photography).

**1.3 Why Hitchcock? Why Architecture?**

In this course I argue for Hitchcock’s cinema as a sophisticated and inclusive *architectural practice.*

This semester’s research and design projects will explore the art of Alfred Hitchcock via the urban condition, modes of cinematic architecture and the technical advances that make him the greatest architect of 20th century cinema. The narrative structures of Hitchcock’s films often move the protagonists from urban contexts to wilderness and vice versa. These allegories track naïve and/or innocent characters as they move toward self-awareness in pursuit of justice, redemption and love.

These transitions are framed and evolve through the careful integration of costumes, music, lighting design, editing and camera work: in short, the stories are guided into our consciousness by an *auteur.*

Famously averse to shooting on location, Hitchcock refined and invented techniques for controlling shifts in scale, perspective, and space—all part of his reliance on the studio for a kind of ‘world building.’ For example, as a way of keeping location costs down, Hitchcock developed backlit film transparencies at the scale of architecture. Similarly, a hybrid of matte painting, live action, rear-projected film and occasional animation lend a dream-like quality to most of his storytelling. Having emphasized his preference for the soundstage, he was nonetheless a master of location production; witness the power and influence of the crop duster sequence in *North by Northwest,* arguably one of the most studies in the history of movies. It has it all: long takes, crane shots, eerie quiet followed by shattering explosions,

Stillness, ominous background activity, surreal rear projection and iconic images of Cary Grant’s Rofer O. Thornhill in the ultimate case of mistaken identity: While not the original *wrong man,* he shines.

**1.4 Learning from Architecture: The Endless Loop of Experimentation Emerging from Convention**

His techniques of sonic and visual abstraction: defamiliarization, continuous takes, color saturation and disorienting perspectives, all have analogues in the operations of the modern city. His themes of voyeurism, doppelgangers, mistaken identity, and paranoia are hallmarks of the modern human condition, our collective age of anxiety. He made the first film to address psychoanalysis; *Spellbound* (1945); shot four ‘single set’ films: *Lifeboat* (1944), *Rope* (1945), *Dial ‘M’ for Murder,* (1950) and *Rear Window,* (1956); and his dark comedy Frenzy (1972) looked at the mostly urban phenomenon of the serial killer. From *The 39 Steps* (1935) to *I Confess* (1953) to *Psycho* (1960), Hitchcock torqued the city grid as symbolic of both freedom (anonymity) and oppression (chaos).

**1.5 Criticism and Capitalism**

The Master of Suspense has also been disparately viewed as a misogynist, sadist, humorist, feminist patriot, Anglophile, and cultural critic. He reinvented the ways in which Hollywood pictures are financed and how actors and directors could earn “points” on the royalties after their own initial investments. One can see the business aspect of George Lucas’ Star Wars franchise as building off the financing and wild success of *Psycho,* funded entirely in advance by Hitchcock and using his television production crew. Until *The Blair Witch Project* (1999), *Psycho* had the greatest ROI of any film. It grossed over $50 million worldwide on an initial investment of $800,000. Profits were driven by an unprecedented advertising campaign (featuring Hitchcock himself) and a strict ‘no late admission’ policy.

In *The Poetics of Space* (1957), Gaston Bachelard asserts that “if one would ask us, what is the most precious benefaction of the house, we would say: the house accommodates dreaming, the house protects the dreamer, the house allows us to dream in peace”. The claim can easily be made that the primary role of housing is safety—including protection from the elements, but also as an affront, literally, to society. It is an emblem of the culture but can also be seen as being apart from it. This is Hitchcock’s main thesis: that the most sinister thing is not a far off, foreign threat, but is right next door. In early 2026 America, his fears are playing out for all to see and share. Add ‘oracle’ to Hitchcock’s list of titles.

**2.0 Order of the Semester/Program Description**

**2.1 NO LOCATION** Weeks 1-3

Our first projects are research-based: a series of viewings, readings, exercises, and deconstructions of films, most by Hitchcock, and a few other essential films like Welles’ *Citizen Kane* and Kubrick’s *2001: A Space Odyssey.* You will sometimes work solo and at other times in small teams. Your investigations will be discussed as a group*. Like architecture, cinema is a team sport, where individual visions can only be realized by collaborative groups.* The hope is that these exercises will constitute your foundation as a critical consumer of cinema.

**2.2 ON LOCATION** Weeks 4-8

Project 2 is largely individual work informed by the both the English tradition of the pastoral sublime and in other global traditions of contemplating *nature* and *the land.* The project is to design a landscape intervention on the western slope of the RPI campus, facing Troy and the Hudson River. Examining the European romantic tradition of **the folly**, students will translate their critical film studies into drawings and models that abstractly demonstrate important concepts from Hitchcock’s cinema. The follies will be platforms for viewing, experiencing light and shadow, spaces for public gathering, outdoor classrooms, performance, audience, contemplation.

Mobility, seating, shading, and landscaping will distinguish this from a standalone building, but as protection from the elements will be a modest component, this part of the project will have limited heated envelope. Each student will present a digital scheme and a series of thoughtfully lit photographs of study models. We will refer to these as *models for,* not *models of,* which are more typically *final models of a completed design*. Our goal at the midterm is *discovery.*

**2.3 SOUTH BY SOUTHWEST (LOCATION, LOCATION, LOCATION)** Weeks 9-16

The final project, in teams of 3, is *The William Devane Center for Cinetecture Studies* (WDCCS),

a building situated on the hillside between EMPAC to the south and The Pittsburgh Building to the North.

the Eastern border is the road behind Folsom and the 8th street border to the western edge of campus.

Addressing the need for more physical space for the School of Architecture, the Center will serve as the permanent home for the CRAIVE Lab, administered by Prof. Jonas Braasch. While EMPAC is a valued media production facility on campus, it is not equipped to permanently house the 360-degree cyclorama of sound and video. Similarly, the NYS Writers Institute could have an off-site (not in Albany) location for the film studies aspect of their program. A modest screening room, offices, conversation pit, classrooms and an archive will round out the program for the center. Finally, the WDCCS will add vitally need physical space to the SoA, as the program outgrew the physical confines of Greene over a decade ago.

Transition studies involving physical models, video screens, video projection and light projection/reflection with be generated in controlled environments with abstracted scale models.

**3.0 Site**

As mentioned above the site is the steep hillside between the south façade of Carnegie and north façade of EMPAC (to the south side of campus, almost to Congress Street. The border to the east side of campus is the road behind the Folsom Library. The western border is 8th Street. It is a pity that so few campus members are granted access to the President’s Room in EMPAC, as the balcony offers the most spectacular view of Troy and the Hudson River. This project will likely offer more people access to similarly breathtaking sunsets, observation of the four seasons and ways to negotiate the hillside physically, visually and acoustically.

The hillside in and of itself hides a remarkable feat of engineering: rumor has it that there are many pilings driven into the hillside to prevent it from sliding down onto 8th street. Rob Carney will illuminate.

- **1** **Site and Building Considerations**

1. A) The site borders (and links) multiple campus programs and the project will facilitate easier access by the residents of Troy.
2. B) Currently, a paved road serves both cars and pedestrians behind Folsom. It is a dangerous place for pedestrians with no railing and no space to walk other that the road. The project will afford pedestrians many places to sit, rest and navigate the hillside down to 8th
3. C) Even EMPAC does not provide much public outdoor event space. An increase in the public participation of campus events will be facilitated by the project.
4. D) Hang out spaces are key to establishing a lively urban condition. There will be informal gathering spaces that could be used for the practice of free speech/public address and the enactment of spontaneous and planned performances.
5. E) The project will provide additional classroom and fabrication space for the SoA. As with the LRC located in downtown Troy and CASE co-located in Brooklyn, the SoA needs satellite spaces in which to make, test and deploy innovative architectures.
6. F) The site is one of the largest undeveloped regions of campus. As such, our project will not ‘fill’ the hillside but be guided by this openness.
7. G) Finally, to clarify, Project 2.2 is not a building, per se, and will have a more open program. Project 2.3 will have a sealed envelope and a set of given programs, with student teams inventing least one additional program per student.

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes (CLOs):

- Outcome 1 Research 1a. Ability to use/develop specialized knowledge – Students will conduct and implement interdisciplinary research into the cinematic Alfred Hitchcock, developing an awareness of aspects of his practice, his innovations, and his influence on generations of filmmakers, artists and architects.

By developing effective communication skills, shred research, project oversight and rotating leadership (on each task there will be a team leader and 2 team subordinates; each student plays both roles in a revolving fashion), the team will learn to work efficiently while still remaining experimental. In this way each team members’ interests and strengths will be leveraged for the best possible project outcome.

**3b. Ability to collaborate with a community partner** - Students will demonstrate the ability to work effectively with our partners: The SoA, EMPAC and the NYS Writer Institute. By being mindful of the specific goals and audiences of these entities, students will collaborate with them directly, assuring mutually shared expectations and outcomes. Close contact will enable smooth negotiation of obstacles, program changes and other unanticipated outcomes, as well as agreed upon goals.

**Outcome 4** Civic and Global Engagement

**4a. Ability to engage the civic realm** – Students will demonstrate the ability to engage local/civic issues as well as Institute priorities, both of which are shared concerns in the Capital Region. An engaged citizenry makes for more informed design and this kind of transparency is cost effective and more efficient, saving on costs associated with the initial misinformation—or lack of information/public sentiment. In this sense, there is a community design aspect to the project.

**4b. Ability to engage the global realm** – Students will demonstrate the ability to engage in social and ethical issues that continue to define our present ‘age of anxiety.’ As film can create bridges between cultures, it is essential to have multiple tools for the communication, translation and the dissemination of design ideas that may seem radical—even objectionable—to some groups.

**Outcome 5** Design Execution

**5a. Midterm Review** – Students will—as individual designers—research and present project 2.2 in ways that demonstrate their ability to reflect the learning outcomes enumerated and elaborated above, that is, outcomes 1-4. The means of demonstrating this will be a series of diagrams, writings, and abstracted physical study models (involving video displayed via projection and on screens) that help to illuminate a proposed landscape architecture intervention. The project will also be realized via drawing sets and model photography.

**5b. Final Review** – Students will—in teams of 3—research and present project 2.3 in ways that demonstrate their ability to reflect the learning outcomes enumerated and elaborated above, that is, outcomes 1-4. The means of demonstrating this will be a series of diagrams, writings, and a final model of their proposed building and landscaping. The project will also be realized via drawing sets, a short video and previously generated model photography by the individual team members.

### Attendance Policy:

**8.0 ATTENDANCE**

A word on studio etiquette. In my studio you are expected to refrain from cell phone use, unless you are documenting work-in-progress. Research should take place via the laptop and by reading the books that I provided and that you seek out. Similarly, you are expected to arrive on time and stay for the duration of studio. Last semester I encountered students wandering off during crits—and sometimes nit returning until much later. As a young art student I eventually learned that the super power of crits was not that someone focused on YOUR work, it was that you sat through 15 OTHER STUDENT’S crits and began to absorb the full value of a non-linear, project-based education. Architecture is a team sport, and it is best played in dialogue, not monologue. Finally, it is your responsibility to budget your time and have work ready to present. For example, I would much rather have you in class than have you waiting for prints to emerge from the VCC! Proofread and print your work early, show up on time for your teammates and contribute material, vocally and with constructive critical input. As the semester advances, I am much more interested in your collective input than my own voice.

**7.0 PROCESS NOTES**

Assignments are just that: work given by the professor. They are the manifestation of certain objectives or goals deemed important by both the individual teacher, the specific educational program, and in the case of architecture, NAAB and NCARB. But they are only a small part of your total education, which—if you are lucky—will be an ongoing process and far outlast your time in school. Be curious about the projects and the assignments as a metaphor for the world and its opportunities and challenges. On a personal note: after my first year at the Rhode Island School of Design, I said to myself that if I could make art over the summer without assignments from my teachers, I might have a chance of becoming an artist. Similarly, if you are able to invent other “unassigned” aspects for the work you present—either individually or in teams—you will take possession of the project and make it your own.

STUDIO POLICY:

Pin-ups

Periodically students will be required to present their work publicly in a formal review process. These presentations are a critical part of the architectural education. It is an opportunity for the student and the faculty to judge the project and critically evaluate the process of design. It may be appropriate at points in the semester to evaluate only selected projects to convey information relevant to the entire class.

Desk Critiques

The design studio environment involves two-way communication. The role of the studio critic is to evolve and strengthen the student’s design process. Every time the student meets with the faculty, he or she must have new iterations of work based on previous conversations. If the student has not produced new work, the faculty reserves the right to skip the critique and continue with other students who have produced new material.

SoA Lectures

Attendance is MANDATORY at all School of Architecture’s lectures regardless of whether lectures occur in-person or on-line.

Attendance

Attendance is required at all class meetings during the semester. Two (2) unexcused absences from class will result in the lowering of the grade by one (1) letter. Four (4) unexcused absences from class during the term result in an automatic F. Whether an absence (health, immediate family death, officially approved field / sport trip, etc.) is excused or unexcused is determined by the Students Experience Office (4th Floor Academy Hall, x8022, se@rpi.edu), upon presentation of required documentation, according to established policies and procedures (see http:// http://se.rpi.edu/policies/ea/).

Students are required to work in studio during studio hours and have the required materials and basic tools to develop the assignments. Work includes participation in site visits, design and development, pinups, presentations, discussions, and field trips. Failure to work in class with undivided attention and disruptive behavior (tardiness, leaving early, lack of participation, general socializing, etc.), as well working on assignments from other classes during class, will count as absences.
