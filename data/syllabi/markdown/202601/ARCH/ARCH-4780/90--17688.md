### Content Details:

**THE STACKED CITY** ***New* Urban Villages at Cordoba City Historical Core COURSE OVERVIEW**

The urban laboratory that the Latin American city has historically been, provides the ground to speculate in the future of city life itself. The crisis of historical city centers is a global phenomenon. On one hand, like Cordoba’s case, they have fallen into disrepair and decadence (is that true?). In the other, the “successful cases” have yielded to global tourism and/or finance. Both cases express different sorts of movements and displacements that have removed everyday life (and local people) from these vital cores. **PREMISE 1: THE URBAN PROJECT / EL PROYECTO URBANO**

If there were an overall thesis for this studio, we would like to put forward an argument for an architectural practice that does not surrender to the disciplinaries forces and policies of urbanism and urban planning; on the contrary, it claims back the urban (and even territorial) sphere as the core of architecture’s agency. Our is a call for an Urban Project [UP].

The Urban Project does not replace traditional urbanism or planning, nor does it refer to large emblematic works. It understands that cities develop through long-term articulated public and private complex urban operations aimed to serve as catalysts for social and economic development.

The Urban Project aims to mediate the modernistic oppositions between planning, urbanism, and architecture. UP neither intervenes on the urban totality, nor exclusively on the architectural object. It does however have the capacity to affect the former and its imperative is to resolve the latter, giving rise to new relationships that help overcoming the plan - project dichotomy.

The articulation of the binomial architecture/city does not invalidate architecture as an object but expands its scope by including the relationships and exchanges that the project constructs with and from its context. It understands the architectural piece not as an autonomous element, but as a constitutive part and as a key tactical element on the transformation of the larger whole – the city.

Programmatically, UP tends towards hybridity, proposing new lifestyles through mixed programs interventions and interrelated architectural interventions of diverse scale. It concentrates its actions in a sector of the city, aiming to transform its phisnomy, behavior and significance in the collective imaginary, impacting its immediate area and developing tendencies that will affect the general urban structure.

**PREMISE 2: NEW WAYS OF LIFE / NUEVAS FORMAS DE VIVIR**

“(…) How do the shifts in social rituals, demographic change, the technological transformation of jobs, the dissolution of the classic office, and the break-up of the classic nuclear family affect building? Building is tailored to two forms of existence: singles or families. But this is a normative socio-political construct. We do not know of a convincing type of building that would be suitable for six octogenarians who are otherwise unwilling to move into a retirement home. We do not know of a type of building where three single parents could live with their children and two gay friends, unless they had enough money for a mansion and install additional bathrooms there. Nor do we know of a convincing new type of building where people live for a longer period of time and where they can also accommodate refugees or transients more easily than would otherwise be possible. Building policies also shape society by discouraging such lifestyles. Good architecture, however, is always an encouragement to try out new ways of life. What forms might a public square, a house, an apartment take, beyond those we are familiar.” (Niklas Maak)

How can we, as architects, respond to this dilemma?

**PREMISE 3: BUILDING & DWELLING**

In 1962, Dutch architect N. John Habraken challenged the prevailing approach to mass housing with his influential book "Supports: An Alternative to Mass Housing." This work critiqued the repetitive, monolithic structures erected in the postwar era to address housing shortages. Habraken argued that traditional residential architecture often overlooks the inhabitants' ability to shape their living environments. He proposed a paradigm shift, viewing dwellings not as finished products for consumption but as ongoing processes shaped by inhabitants' daily activities and interactions.

The act of building and the act of dwelling. Central to Habraken's concept was the idea of "support" and "infill." He advocated for a separation between the base building, controlled collectively by the community, and the customizable elements such as interior divisions, closets, and kitchens, known as "infill." This approach empowered users to adapt their living spaces according to their needs.

Beyond housing, Habraken's principles extend to broader urban and territorial considerations. Cities, he argued, are dynamic entities that continually adapt to human needs. He envisioned the built environment as a multi-layered structure comprising urban structure, urban blocks, buildings, infill components, and furniture. Each layer operates on a different time scale and involves various levels of control and responsibility.

This layered approach will later align with Stewart Brand's "Shearing Layers" concept, which depicts buildings as hierarchical systems with components evolving at different speeds. The site remains permanent, while the structure, skin, services, space plan, and furniture have varying life cycles ranging from years to centuries.

**PROJECT BRIEF + DELIVERABLES**

Students will develop a schematic proposal of an urban scale piece of architecture for a site next in the core historical city center, above (and within) the current Galeria San Martin -- the entire half block North facing the Central Plaza.

The scale of the program will respond to the urban demands of the context. Its components should consist of 50% residential and 50% a program to be defined by the students according to their site analysis.

Projects will not be necessarily constrained by the limits of the parcela / lot, as the project could be understood as a proposal for a consortium enterprise. Teams will study the general site condition and propose a general strategy to be composed in equal terms by residential program and complementary program derived from the analysis of the urban condition.

Tectonics: as a complementary argument, our studio will explore and update the notions of “support and infill” as the base for flexible and incremental collective inhabitation.

## Learning Outcomes

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes (CLOs):

- Site Analysis. Analyze conditions of a central urban area and establish conditional basis for the development an urban architectural project.
- Tectonic Studies. Explore the tectonic relations between the support and infill for its application in incremental housing development.
- Typological Explorations. Develop a series of typological speculative studies proposing new types of housing and communal living that address contemporary lifestyles (single parenthood, same genre families, co-housing aging, co-parenting, etc.)
- Environmental Response. Develop an integral environmental response through a project that addresses both human and non-human inhabitation.
- Future Envisioning: develop advanced imagery situating the architectural proposal and speculating on its future use and context.
- Immersive Learning: Engage in collaborative experience with peers from international university, integrating diverse educational backgrounds and perspectives. Collaboratively undertake the development of an architectural project, fostering teamwork and cross-cultural exchange to enhance the design process.

### Additional Academic Integrity Course Policy and Penalty Information:

The following statement regarding academic dishonesty is taken verbatim from The Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities Intellectual integrity and credibility are the foundation of all academic work. Academic dishonesty is, by definition, considered a flagrant offense to the educational process. It is taken seriously by students, faculty, and Rensselaer and will be addressed in an effective manner.

If found in violation of academic dishonesty policy, students may be subject to two types of penalties: the instructor administers an academic (grade) penalty, and the student may be subject to the procedures and penalties of the student judicial system outlined in this handbook.

Since academic dishonesty is a violation of the Grounds for Disciplinary Action, the student may be subject to any of the following szanctions: disciplinary warning; disciplinary probation; disciplinary suspension, expulsion and/or alternative actions as agreed on by the student and hearing officer. It should be noted that no student who allegedly commits academic dishonesty will be able to drop or change the grade option for the course in question.

The definitions and examples presented below are samples of types of academic dishonesty and are not to be construed as an exhaustive or exclusive list. The academic dishonesty policy applies to all students, undergraduate and graduate, and to scholarly pursuits and research. Additionally, attempts to commit academic dishonesty or to assist in the commission or attempt of such an act are also violations of this policy.

Academic Fraud: The alteration of documentation relating to the grading process. For example, changing exam solutions to negotiate for a higher grade or tampering with an instructor’s grade book.

Collaboration: Deliberate facilitation of academic dishonesty in any form. For example, allowing another student to observe an exam paper or allowing another student to “recycle” one’s old term paper or using oneanother’s work in a paper or lab report without citing it as another’s work.

Copying: Obtaining information pertaining to a graded exercise by deliberately observing the paper of another student. For example, noting which alternative a neighboring student has circled on a multiple-choice exam.

Cribbing: Use or attempted use of prohibited materials, information, or study aids in an academic exercise. For example, using an unauthorized formal sheet during an exam.

Fabrication: Unauthorized falsification or invention of any information in an academic exercise. For example, use of “bought” or “ready-made” term papers, or falsifying lab records or reports.

Plagiarism: Representing the work or words of another as one’s own through the omission of acknowledgment or reference. For example, using sentences verbatim from a published source in a term paper without appropriate referencing, or presenting as one’s own the detailed argument of a published source, or presenting as one’s own electronically or digitally enhanced graphic representations from any form of media.

Sabotage: Destruction of another student’s work. For example, destroying a model, lab experiment, computer program, or term paper developed by another student.

Substitution: Utilizing a proxy, or acting as a proxy, in any academic exercise. For example, taking an exam for another student or having a homework assignment done by someone else.

SUBMISSION OF ANY ASSIGNMENT THAT IS IN VIOLATION OF THIS POLICY WILL RESULT IN A PENALTY OF “NO CREDITS” IN THE FIRST CASE (AND THE ASSIGNMENT, PROJECT, OR EXAM CONCERNED) AND AN AUTOMATIC “F” (FAIL) FOR THE ENTIRE COURSE IF THE ISSUE REPEATS A SECOND TIME.
