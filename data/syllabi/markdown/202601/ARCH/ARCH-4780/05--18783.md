## Course Content

### Content Details:

The studio will focus on the relationship between light, projection and Space.  
We will unpacks it through literary and physical explorations; testing different ways we can use projection mapping, light and material to draw and sculpt environments.  
CONSTRUCTING ATMOSPHERE - LIGHT  
In the studio we will test different lighting conditions to uncover their spatial potential and atmospheric affects, we will also look at these lighting conditions as seeds of ideas and theses that can unfold through space and time.  
Will will see how do we design light as a spatial condition? How do we work with projection, be it from a solar source or an electric light? How can this study help us develop a way of designing architecture?  
Light is a primary component we conjure when we envision an architectural atmosphere. The dictionary definition suggests at least two meanings for the word atmosphere: one relates to the properties of air particles, and the other to a pervading tone or mood. Etymologically, the word contains the Greek term used to define vapor (atmos).’  
When light travels in air, it is invisible; once it hits particles in the air, such as vapor, we become aware of both the light path and the vapor itself. As a medium light is invisible until it intersects with a medium which can be as small as a spec (as seen in this project by Anthony McCall, where air vapor make the path of the light visible) a screen or architecture or even a human body.  
Through interaction with material light is revealed, reflected, blocked. In order to create an atmosphere, architecture and light must interact; this is the core of the research we will investigate this term.  
We will create studies in different environments, utilizing haze, to understand the full spatial potential of the projection, and study working with light in relationship to different material properties. Throughout the semester, we will develop atmospheric models and experiment within studio developing formal tests in material and light.  
Light experiments: We will begin by creating spatial experiments responding to some conditions that relate to light, and geometry related to Iceland Geology, referring to immersive art installations  
Analysis in 2 and 3 dimensions: We will then analyze these studies, making the intangible aspects visible and possible to serve as design, and address different aspects that may come up regarding light and health and wellbeing.  
1:1 Light installation: We will work with light in a 1:1 scale, outside of the school. I have been speaking to both Collar Works gallery and or Mt. Ida Church to house an installation around the time of our mid-review. This will be finalized later in the semester.  
ICELAND SLOW SAUNA - SITE AND PROGRAM  
We will test our spatial ideas through the site and program taken from the competition Iceland Slow Sauna, and the studio deliverables will work towards allowing you to submit a proposal (Deadline May 11, 2026) if you choose.  
The competition is for the design of an atmospheric space of a small Sauna and greenhouse space, located in Iceland, near the Lake Mývatn  
SITE  
“Iceland is a land of extremes, where fire and ice continually shape one another. Glaciers carve valleys while volcanoes create new ground; rivers run beside lava fields; geothermal steam rises through moss-covered earth. It is a place where nature remains visible in its raw processes, not smoothed or concealed. The climate is harsh, but the landscapes are luminous, offering moments of clarity and silence that feel otherworldly. To build here is to respond to conditions that are at once fragile and immense, where every intervention is measured against elemental forces of wind, snow, stone, and light.” Slow Sauna Competition Brief  
This is also a radical temporal scape – winter and summer are very different from one another. We will need to design for long dark winters and summers are filled with light shifting along the horizon.  
“Mývatn is not only a landscape of geology but also of rhythm. Winters are long and dark, while summers are brief but filled with almost continuous light. These seasonal extremes shape daily life and invite rituals of warmth, recovery, and connection to nature. It is a place where slowing down becomes instinctive, where the horizon stretches wide, and where architecture must be humble enough to belong yet strong enough to endure.” Slow Sauna Competition Brief  
“The sauna site lies next to búfa – Private Homestay,the residence and guest house run by Óli and Bianca, nestled into the Mývatn region’s dramatic terrain. búfa is perched among black lava rock fields, carved by ancient flows, its form set against craters, moss beds, and the distant silhouette of volcanic ridges. “  
Slow Sauna Competition Brief  
PROGRAM: SAUNA + GREEN HOUSE  
As the brief suggests - In Iceland, warmth is not a luxury; it is a way of life. Thermal life is part of the ground: fissures breathe, water steams, stone holds heat. There is a long history of Icelanders gathering around geothermal water. This is the cultural understanding of “sauna” in Iceland grows: fixed in a broader thermal ritual—heat, breath, cleansing, conversation—rooted in geology and weather.  
“In Iceland, warmth is not a luxury; it is a way of enduring and belonging. Thermal life here begins in the ground itself: fissures breathe, water steams, stone holds heat. For centuries Icelanders have gathered around geothermal water to restore the body and to socialize—first in natural hot pools like Snorralaug at Reykholt (documented since medieval times), later in the ubiquitous public pools that anchor daily life across the country. This is the cultural soil from which our understanding of “sauna” in Iceland grows: less a fixed Finnish typology than a broader thermal ritual—heat, breath, cleansing, conversation—rooted in geology and weather.” Slow Sauna Competition Brief  
Participants are asked to design a retreat that unites a sauna and a greenhouse into one continuous architectural experience. This is not a project of monumentality, but of intimacy, presence, and atmosphere. The building should express care for its users and respect for the surrounding landscape, offering a rhythm of transition between heat, air, light, and rest.  
The retreat must combine two essential elements:  
Sauna —  
designed for 2-4 people, approximately 12-16 m²,  
allowing freedom in ritual logic (dry, steam, hybrid, or reinterpreted).  
Greenhouse —  
directly connected to the sauna, approximately 10-15 m², functioning as a recovery chamber, light-filled resting zone, and seasonal space for cultivation.  
Competition Brief Suggested Deliverables  
Presentation Deliverables Set  
Context plan (suggested scale 1:500 or 1:1000)  
Elevations (suggested scale 1:50)  
Primary sections (suggested scale 1:50)  
Primary floor plans (suggested scale 1:50)  
Enlarged sections and elevations highlighting key spaces or relationships (suggested scale 1:10)  
Details (suggested scale 1:5): envelope, key materials, site or landscape  
Axonometrics providing information on building systems or illustrating key architectural concepts  
Diagrams: circulation, public versus private space, lighting, landscaping Perspectives: primary interior spaces, primary site locations  
Please note that the PRESENTATION DELIVERABLES SET listed above is a suggestion only. Participants can choose to use a selection from it, or propose a completely different set that would explain their design in the most efficient manner.  
FINAL REVIEW  
In our final review we will explore the full spatial potential of your sauna proposals, as well as your conceptual light studies. Throughout the semester (and in our review) we will have access to testing our exe prints in haze, understanding the spatial potential latent within the sauna atmosphere and allowing light to be a fully three dimensional element.

## Learning Outcomes

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes (CLOs):

- OUTCOME 1 Research: 1a. ability to use / develop specialized knowledge - Students will have to control lighting conditions such as shadow, reflection, color and projection. They will demonstrate the ability to use of light and material strategically to reach a required/ desired affect; 1b. ability to use / develop broad and integrative knowledge - Students will develop a way to diagrammatically draw and model intangible conditions such as light projection, drawn ray diagrams and temperature analysis, 3D printed analysis.
- OUTCOME 2 Intellectual Design Skills: 2a. ability to design - Students will have the ability to control and draw lighting conditions and implement this knowledge in the design of 1:1 light tests and installation as well as the design of a Sauna proposal; 2b. ability to design using information resources - Students will learn to identify and analyze existing site conditions through the use of professional-level base documentation and demonstrate the ability to use this technical information in the design of strategic interventions into an existing site with precision, clarity, and impact; 2c. ability to design engaging diverse perspectives - Students will have the ability to critically address scale-ability of light investigations and the construction of a spatial experience in 1:1; 2d. ability to design with ethical reasoning - Students will deign considering climate and thermal awareness, they will be mindful of energy use and incorporating ideas pertaining to light and health as they develop their design; 2e. ability to design with quantitative fluency - Students will learn to evaluate a "real word" program derived from the Iceland Slow Sauna Competition, translate a qualitative client "wish list" into a quantitative program brief, and propose modifications/amendments to the brief and site allocations. Students will demonstrate the ability to interpret and synthesize different types of information (written/numerical/visual), establish and calculate numerical areas/space allocations, analyze multiple "test-fit" scenarios, and determine the project's scope of work, including the types, amounts, and extents of the competition design brief changes to be made; 2f. ability to design with communicative fluency - Students will learn to differentiate between different constituencies/audiences (architectural, client, and general public) and demonstrate the ability to articulate a design problem, problem-solving methods, and solution in these different contexts.
- OUTCOME 3 Collaboration: 3a ability to collaborate with team - students will work in teams of two to develop site analysis. Students will have the option to continue to work as a team or individually for the 1:1 light installation as well as potentially in the final competition project; 3b ability to collaborate with a community partner - Students will develop projects in relation to the competition brief, will attend Q and A to make sure they fully understand client perspective. Students will also work with faculty and community partner on a midterm 1:1 light installation.
- OUTCOME 4 Civic and Global Engagement: 4a. ability to engage the civic realm - Students will study implications of light on health, and will demonstrate the ability to use\&nbsp; light projection and\&nbsp; to impact on public space and impact the perception of the environment, and well-being on the inhabitants; 4b. ability to engage the global realm - Students will demonstrate the ability to engage with a complex global site, understand and appreciate how different environmental, geological and cultural setting impact the conditions of the design project.
- OUTCOME 5 Design Execution: 5a. Midterm Review - Students will demonstrate the ability to implement their learning in Outcomes #1-4 in a clear, complete, and compelling way in a presentation that includes lighting studies and analysis, 1:1 light installation; 5b. Final Review - Students will demonstrate the ability to implement their learning in Outcomes #1-4 in a clear, complete, and compelling way in a presentation that includes diagrams, analysis, orthographic drawings, axonometrics, and renderings as per Slow Sauna Competition guidelines as well as lighting detail demonstration.

### Other Course Policies:

Studio Culture:  
Pin-ups  
Periodically students will be required to present their work publicly in a formal review process. These presentations are a critical part of the architectural education. It is an opportunity for the student and the faculty to judge the project and critically evaluate the process of design. It may be appropriate at points in the semester to evaluate only selected projects in order to convey information relevant to the entire class. In this situation it is important for each student to witness and participate in the review process. Even if your project is not being reviewed, the criticism and discussion should inform the evaluation of your own progress and design. Failure to attend one or more pin-ups for the full class period may result in a grade deduction.  
Desk Critiques  
The design studio environment involves two-way communication. The role of the studio critic is to evolve and strengthen the student’s design process. Every time the student meets with the faculty, he or she must have new iterations of work based on previous conversations. If the student has not produced new work, the faculty reserves the right to skip the critique and continue with other students who have produced new material.
