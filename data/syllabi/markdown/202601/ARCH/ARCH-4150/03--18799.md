### Additional Information for Course Information Section:

**CATALOGUE COURSE DESCRIPTION**

Reflecting on the knowledge and understandings acquired in all the previous courses in the history, theory, and criticism sequence, this course is a critical inquiry into the principal ideologies and premises of the most substantive architectural practices in the contemporary world. As such, the content of the course must necessarily evolve as the intellectual and cultural parameters of both theory and practice in the contemporary world change. The principal aim of the critique of various contemporary positions is to reinforce in students the importance of developing and critically maintaining their own values, vision, and voice in the design and making of architecture that was initiated in ARCH 2150, The Ethos of Architecture. Fundamental to this process is to encourage the students’ awareness of the necessity to challenge their own subjectivities, biases, and presuppositions.

**OVERVIEW**  
The course introduces students to main contexts that help frame and influence architectural theory and design practices from the last quarter of the 20th century and onto current times. Along the semester students are invited to critically explore ways in which theory influences design and design influences thinking, as well focusing on the tools that are co-opted and invented to produce or materialize these exchanges. It is a required undergraduate course, open to graduate students. This course has 3 credits and meets on Wednesdays 10am-12:50pm.

**COURSE OBJECTIVES**

The goals of this course are to broaden the students’ knowledge of past and current design trends and practices; to give them the wherewithal to critique their own assumptions and predispositions regarding these trends and practices; and finally, to assist them in articulating critical design positions of their own.

**COURSE DESCRIPTION**

The course departs from a number of well-established given discourses of the last quarter of the 20th century, that critically analyze and articulate architectural disciplinary thinking with particular types of representation tools and practices, specifically as tools and materials of this and other disciplines shift into digital media.

The course evolves through overviews on evolutions of cultural framings of the designed ‘object’, an evolution of representation tools (‘drawing’), and on the relationship between design at the building and urban scale (‘cities’), as these intersect with current drivers of data and information ecologies. The course further introduces students to current and near future productive challenges derived from the acknowledgement and invention of new bodies, new subjects and new materials in the design equation.

The course attempts to provide the student with a toolset of inventions that are responses to challenges in inquiries, as well as make visible provocative contexts for the architecture disciplinary pattern of thinking to deploy itself to and forth. In this way, we hope to effectively imbue students with the ability and will to open up the field’s potential to broader contexts of the real world and its contemporary demands.

After attending the lecture, the class’ sections engage in an in-person discussion on its contents and topic of the week established by the course.

**DELIVERABLES & COURSE REQUIREMENTS**

**1. Weekly Comment/Question on 1 Reading + 1 Response to Comment**

The students, in all the weeks they do not prepare a reading for discussion, should submit a brief comment or question to one of the weekly readings. *All readings comments/questions should be posted in the online platform, by the previous Tuesday, 11.59pm EST.* Select 1 weekly reading and in approximately 200 words pose 1-2 important questions or comments. These questions or comments may be raised during panel discussion by the professor, the students and the course assistant. You are encouraged to submit posts in a timely manner. These submissions will be recorded/noted by the course assistant. Additionally, post a response/question to one (1) of your colleagues posts (within same section).

**2. Discussion Group Participation (See “READING AND DISCUSSION GROUP” in Syllabus for guidelines)**

Each Wednesday 11:30am-12:50pm meet with your respective Instructor sections to discuss the lecture and readings, tailoring the discussion toward completion of the Discussion Report. All students should come with their notes/marginalia/sketchbook to discuss connections between lecture and the readings, answer the weekly prompted discussion questions, and pose one (or more) of your own new questions as a team to bring back for the final all-class discussion at the end of the semester. Participation will be indicated by the Discussion Leader on the Discussion Report.

**3a. Visual Map Presentation (See “READING AND DISCUSSION GROUP” in Syllabus for guidelines)**

Each week, (11:30-noon) 4-6 students in each Instructor’s section will present a one page visual map that identifies main themes in the readings and lectures and connects them together in an annotated 'mind-map'. Format is to be determined by the Instructor and student and may be handwritten/sketched and scanned; or completed digitally. Each student presentation should be 1-3 minutes, with time for class questions and instructor feedback. Upload to online platform by the beginning of class.

**3b. Lead Discussion / Discussion Report (See “READING AND DISCUSSION GROUP” in Syllabus for guidelines)**

Students who present a visual map for the week will also be a discussion leader for the week. After visual map presentations, the section will break into two smaller discussion groups, each with three presenters/discussion leaders to facilitate the discussion. Each discussion leader should review the Slack channel prior to discussion and identify at least three student posts/questions to discuss in further detail. Following discussion, the three discussion leaders in each group should work together to create a Discussion Report (See respective Form with Guidelines). Credit group member names for their specific contributions and offers of arguments in the discussion. The intent is to complete the majority of the Discussion Report during discussion hours (Wednesday 11:30am-12:50pm). Discussion Reports are due on the respective Wednesday at 11.59pm EST. They are submitted to the respective online platform.

**4. Marginalia/notes/sketchbook photocopies (see “MARGINALIA “ in Syllabus for more details)**

Each week the lecture and readings are organized around a given thematic topic. Instead of merely skimming the texts, we encourage a process of close reading to help retention, which means you may have to read the article more than once. This can be done through marginalia / markups on the printed page, digital markups in a PDF or digital sketchbook, or separately with notes and drawings in a physical sketchbook.

*Refer to Section Instructor for preferred submission format.*

**5. Final Project Paper**

*Individual Work*. Reflecting upon the topics and themes presented in the course, your final paper should be written in the format of a Manifesto for Design. Give your manifesto a unique title that speaks to the intent and values you will be discussing. The manifesto should be a new critical argument declared by you, or an expansion of arguments presented in class, supported by sources as evidence. The manifesto should cite from at least (3) readings from the semester, in Chicago Style footnote citations. The manifesto must declare at least three values you deem necessary and important in the practice of design. The manifesto should be clear about influences, relatability and impact of the ideas and concepts on design at multiple scales (local, urban, global, planetary, or any combination). In writing the manifesto, students would do well to recall prior History, Theory, Criticism courses during your time at Rensselaer. Students should deeply interrogate how you can bring these values into practice, be it in your studios (current and future), as well as into your career beyond Rensselaer. This manifesto will be revisited during your Final Project seminar and studio in 5th year. The manifesto can include images, diagrams, but this is not required.

## Grading Criteria

### Criteria Details:

Please see table in link for assignment/assessment grading: **[https://rpi.box.com/s/hun8z5rw3y1wcwlyg7g644ufxc8ihvyt](https://rpi.box.com/s/uxywdpni9vv2eui4o5ri97zo2w3icypb) GRADING CRITERIA**

**A (94%-100%) / A-(90%-93%) work ("Superior")** shows a comprehensive and mature grasp of the material presented. It demonstrates a student's capacity to consider issues fairly but critically, in new contexts and with reference to broader insights about modern architecture. "A" work demonstrates superior writing skills, proper citations, an aptitude for originality and flair, and an unwavering willingness to go beyond the standard arguments, clichés, etc. "A" work suffers from very few (usually no) errors relating to grammar, spelling, referencing, paragraph development and sentence structure.

**B+ (87%-89%) / B (83%-86%) / B- (80%-82%) work ("Good")** shows a solid grasp of the material presented. However, it typically lacks the originality, nuance and detail of "A" work. "B" work shows that the material has been read and studied, but it is less impressive than "A" work, specifically in terms of the level of reflection, curiosity, textual support and/or analytical insight. "B" work may also suffer from several errors relating to grammar, spelling, citations, paragraph development and sentence structure. A less solid grasp of the material and/or the more writing errors will push the grade down.

**C+ (77%-79%) / C (73%-76%) / C-(70%-72%) work ("Average")** shows some understanding of the material but is generally marred by analytical gaps, factual inaccuracies and/or missed opportunities for greater clarity. It also suffers from frequent writing problems and/or inadequate/improper citations.

**D+ (65%-69%) / D (61%-64%) work ("Inferior")** shows very limited understanding of the material; very limited evidence of reading; inadequate reflection; poor writing and inadequate and/or improper citations. "D" work is poorly structured, weak and/or partial in conception and delivery.

**Failure ("Unacceptable").** So limited, poorly organized or misinterpreted as to justify a clear fail. Often characterized by very poor presentation, organization and writing, and inadequate and/or improper citations.

Collaboration: Knowingly facilitating and/or contributing to an act of academic dishonesty. For example, allowing another student to observe an exam paper or allowing another student to “recycle” one’s old term paper or using another’s work in a paper or lab report without giving appropriate attribution.

Copying: Obtaining information pertaining to a graded exercise by deliberately observing the paper of another student. For example, noting which alternative a neighboring student has circled on a multiple-choice exam.

Plagiarism: Representing the work or words of another as one’s own through the omission of acknowledgment or reference. For example, using sentences verbatim from a published source in a term paper without appropriate referencing, or presenting as one’s own the detailed argument of a published source, or presenting as one’s own electronically or digitally enhanced graphic representations from any form of media.

Cribbing: Use or attempted use of prohibited materials, information, or study aids in an academic exercise. For example, using an unauthorized formal sheet during an exam.

Fabrication: Unauthorized falsification or invention of any information in an academic exercise. For example, use of “bought” or “ready-made” term papers, or falsifying lab records or reports.

Sabotage: Destruction of another student’s work. For example, destroying a model, lab experiment, computer program, or term paper developed by another student.

Substitution: Utilizing a proxy, or acting as a proxy, in any academic exercise. For example, taking an exam for another student or having a homework assignment done by someone else.

**Use and Citation of Generative AI software *- Preliminary Guidelines, in Progress***

The reproduction of copyright works for certain limited, educational purposes, is typically considered Fair Use and does not constitute copyright infringement. Being a relatively new, complex and evolving phenomena, policy and other regulatory instruments applying to generative AI software are adapted as necessary by department and institutions.

In light of the production and public discussion of CDA work at the School of Architecture at RPI, this course coordination has the mandatory guidelines below relative to the use of Generative AI in the classrooms and CDA work. Students shall apply any additional guidelines, principles and formats provided by the respective Section Instructors. Failure to meet these Guidelines, Principles and Formats may result in disciplinary action equivalent to violations of academic integrity.

The School of Architecture uses 3 main principles - Transparency, Traceability, Accountability - to guide academic integrity best practices for the use of Generative AI tools for its course work. These 3 principles are adopted - and adapted to the School of Architecture context - from ACM Technology Policy Office's "PRINCIPLES FOR THE DEVELOPMENT, DEPLOYMENT, AND USE OF GENERATIVE AI TECHNOLOGIES", June 2023

[https://www.acm.org/binaries/content/assets/public-policy/ustpc-approved-generative-ai-principles](https://www.acm.org/binaries/content/assets/public-policy/ustpc-approved-generative-ai-principles)

**TRANSPARENCY** Faculty and students shall be forthcoming of generative AI use, in general and particular instances, when applicable, and cite the circumstances and methods of use.

**Transparency Guidelines/Recommendation**

Course Content: where generative AI has been used to produce part of or the whole work, disclosure must happen at some point before the end of content delivery.

Seminar Presentation or Discussion: where generative AI has been used to produce part of or the whole work, disclosure must happen at some point before the end of content delivery/ discussion.

Desk Critics between Faculty and Student: where generative AI has been used to produce part of the work, disclosure must happen at some point before the end of discussion of the respective project.

Printed/Fabricated/Projected Work: Faculty and Student should be forthcoming of any and all, total or partial, work produced by generative AI software.

Review Context: where generative AI has been used to produce part of the work, disclosure must happen by the student at some point before the end of discussion of respective project.

Other non-specified contexts: where generative AI has been used to produce part of or the whole work, disclosure must happen at some point before the end of content delivery/discussion.

**TRACEABILITY** When citing materials presented in the classroom, in a review or included in the FP Book, Faculty and students shall provide references and citations, when applicable, that enable tracing back originals of both sources and processes by using the Chicago Manual of Style for citation for Generative AI Tools, for both Text and Image.

**ACCOUNTABILITY AND RESPONSIBILITY**  
Faculty and Students will be held accountable for decisions made by algorithms they use, even if it is not feasible to explain in detail how those algorithms produced their results. Responsibility will apply to entire work, not just for the individual parts that make up the whole.
