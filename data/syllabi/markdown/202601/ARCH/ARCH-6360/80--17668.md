## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

As an extension of the “Integrative Advances Seminar” taught in the fall semester, the “Interdisciplinary Research Studio” is structured around each student’s already defined individual research project. The goal this semester is to define and execute a systematic **research methodology** of your subject matter to arrive at **specific knowledge** or **product** that can be replicated and transmitted to an interdisciplinary audience. As a theoretical discussion we will approach the issue of Research in Design. We will evaluate the similarities and differences that Research in Design has with Research in Science or Engineering. From a more practical perspective, students in this studio will develop the technical tools necessary to address the topic of research that they have identified. The goal for students is to be able to articulate a coherent research methodology that is measurable and clear **and** demonstrate its execution.

All students regardless of their specific topic of inquiry and methodology created, will have to address through their research the bigger issues that are outlined below.

The world’s population reached nearly 8.2 billion as of mid-2025 implying that the world has added approximately one billion (~973 million) inhabitants over the last twelve years. Today, the world’s population continues to grow, albeit more slowly than in the recent past. Ten years ago, the global population was growing by 1.20 per cent per year. Today, it is growing by 0.85 per cent per year, yielding an additional 70 million people annually. The world’s population is projected to increase by slightly more than 330 million people over the next 5 years, reaching 8.6 billion in 2030, and to increase further to 9.7 billion in 2050 and 10.2 billion by 2100. (*2024 revision report, United Nations*). For the United States, according to the U.S. Census Bureau, United Nations, Pew Research and other sources, the U.S. will grow to 361 million people by 2050

– up 11% from our 2017 population of 326 million. (Global population grows to 9.7 billion by 2050.) And upwards of 90% of future Americans will live in cities and urbanized areas (up from 80.7% in 2010). In other words, by 2050 there will the same number of Americans living in cities as there are in the entire nation today!

When considering the built environment as a whole - Material Extraction, Manufacturing, Transportation,

Construction, Active life, Maintenance and Disposal - the building industry has the largest carbon footprint of any other human activity on the planet. According to the U.S. Energy Information Administration (EIA), the building sector consumes nearly half (36.9%) of all energy produced in the United States. It was responsible for nearly half (33.4%) of U.S. CO2 emissions in 2010. By comparison, transportation accounted for 38.6% of CO2 emissions and industry just 28%. More importantly, at a global scale, according to a 2018 study by the United Nations, more people live in cities, with 58% of the world’s population residing in urban areas. In 1950, 30% of the world’s population was urban, and by 2050, 67% of the world’s population is projected to be urban. If we consider that continuing population growth and urbanization are projected to add 1.7 billion people to the world’s urban population by 2050 and 2.0 billion by 2100, **what are strategies that will allow the future growth of the urban built environment on the planet to become more** **resilient****?**

If we assume the same urban distribution by 2100 (67%) globally, this will mean that from 4.8 billion people that live in cities today, we will have 6.8 billion living in cities by 2100. This for us would mean - with a conservative estimate - that by 2100 (*this is in the next 75 years*) we would have to almost double the amount of cities on the planet. What are alternatives to the current practices of the building that will make the building industry more energy efficient and environmentally friendly? Where will all the material for such a huge construction project come from? How are we going to provide power for double the amount of building infrastructure and what does the overall growth mean about the quality of living in the future cities? How can we use information as infrastructure for the future city in order to cope with the complexities arising from such intense city densification?

These are some fundamental questions that we are trying to address through design and research in this studio.

**In the age of the Anthropocene, it is more relevant than ever before to consider any human activity in a**

**larger context of production and consumption (processing) of MATERIAL, ENERGY and INFORMATION at scales far greater than those of the immediate object of inquiry.**

In this second installment of the studio, we will still use the following as a definition: **Architecture is the “effective” distribution of MATERIAL, ENERGY AND INFORMATION in space for cultural affect.**

## Course Goals

### Goals:

- **Define a rigorous, replicable design-research methodology.** Each student will translate their existing research direction into a clearly framed question, scope, assumptions, and measurable criteria for success. You will explicitly articulate what counts as evidence in your project and position your approach relative to research norms in science and engineering—highlighting where they overlap and where design research operates differently.
- **Execute a systematic pilot study that produces evidence.** Students will run a structured research workflow (data gathering, testing, modeling, prototyping, comparative analysis, etc.) that generates verifiable outputs rather than only speculative claims. The process will be documented step-by-step so the methodology can be repeated, evaluated, and improved by others.
- **Develop and apply technical tools through the lenses of Material, Energy, and Information.** Students will build or adapt the tools necessary to investigate their topic—whether computational pipelines, simulation workflows, material experiments, sensing/measurement setups, or fabrication protocols. Every project will connect its inquiry to the broader socio-ecological systems of the built environment (from extraction and manufacturing to operation and disposal) and identify leverage points toward resilience and reduced impact.
- **Produce a transmissible knowledge product for an interdisciplinary audience.** By the end of the semester, each student will deliver a research outcome that can be replicated and communicated beyond architecture—such as a tested prototype, a validated workflow, a dataset + method, a tool, or a set of guidelines grounded in evidence. You will demonstrate not only what you made, but what you learned, how you learned it, what the limitations are, and why it matters for future urban growth and the Anthropocene-scale realities of material, energy, and information.

### Additional Academic Integrity Course Policy and Penalty Information:

The Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities defines various forms of Academic Dishonesty and procedures for responding to them. Academic dishonesty violates the trust between students and teachers. Students must trust that teachers have made appropriate decisions about the structure and content of the courses they teach.

Teachers must trust that the assignments students turn in are their own performance. Violations of this trust undermines the educational process. Cheating, plagiarism (defined as the lack of attribution or citation of the work of others), willful damage or theft of another person’s work violate academic integrity. The first instance of the breach of this trust will merit a failing grade in this course and the violator(s) will be reported to the Dean of Students for disciplinary action.

The definitions and examples presented below are samples of types of academic dishonesty and are not to be construed as an exhaustive or exclusive list. The academic dishonesty policy applies to all students, undergraduate and graduate, and to scholarly pursuits and research. Additionally, attempts to commit academic dishonesty or to assist in the commission or attempt of such an act are also violations of this policy.

- Academic Fraud: The alteration of documentation relating to the grading process. For example, changing exam solutions to negotiate for a higher grade or tampering with an instructor’s grade book.
- Collaboration: Deliberate facilitation of academic dishonesty in any form. For example, allowing another student to observe an exam paper or allowing another student to “recycle” one’s old term paper or using one another’s work in a paper or lab report without citing it as another’s work.
- Copying: Obtaining information pertaining to a graded exercise by deliberately observing the paper of another student. For example, noting which alternative a neighboring student has circled on a multiple- choice exam.
- Cribbing: Use or attempted use of prohibited materials, information, or study aids in an academic exercise. For example, using an unauthorized formal sheet during an exam.
- Fabrication: Unauthorized falsification or invention of any information in an academic exercise. For example, use of “bought” or “ready-made” term papers, or falsifying lab records or reports.
- Plagiarism: Representing the work or words of another as one’s own through the omission of acknowledgment or reference. For example, using sentences verbatim from a published source in a term paper without appropriate referencing, or presenting as one’s own the detailed argument of a published source, or presenting as one’s own electronically or digitally enhanced graphic representations from any form of media.
- Sabotage: Destruction of another student’s work. For example, destroying a model, lab experiment, computer program, or term paper developed by another student.
- Substitution: Utilizing a proxy, or acting as a proxy, in any academic exercise. For example, taking an exam for another student or having a homework assignment done by someone else.
