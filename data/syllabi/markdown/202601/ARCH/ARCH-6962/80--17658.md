## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

This seminar introduces architecture and computational design students to the design and prototyping of smart, responsive systems for the built environment (smart cities, smart buildings, IoT networks, and interactive installations). Using a cybernetic lens—sensing, control, and actuation as feedback loops—students prototype real‑world events as inputs and design outputs as a dialogue between people, devices, objects, and spaces. The course treats control and management as design problems: how rules, thresholds, and emerging learning behaviors shape agency, participation, and governance in responsive environments. Students explore post‑human communication protocols in which environments become conversational partners, and they develop human‑centered interaction scenarios that support decision‑making and design. Finally, students learn techniques to record and measure reality into digital media—photogrammetry, scanning, and 3D point clouds—and connect these representations to interactive systems (Unity3D/XR), pushing the boundary of what interactions can happen in and with the environment.

## Course Goals

### Goals:

1. Explain core concepts of cybernetics (feedback, control, communication) as applied to built systems.
2. Design and prototype IoT pipelines: sensor input → data processing → messaging → local “broker” → actuation/visualization.
3. Implement and compare responsive protocols (state machines, rule‑based logic, Proportional–integral–derivative (PID) controller, event streams) for interaction and decision support.
4. Construct tangible–virtual interfaces that mediate conversations between humans and environments.
5. Communicate systems through analytical drawings, system diagrams, and live demos.

## Course Content

### Content Details:

Across the semester, students will:

- Prototype real‑world events as inputs (sensors, data streams) and designed outputs as a smart dialogue/conversation (light, motion, sound, XR visuals).
- Model closed‑loop behaviors and responsive protocols for decision‑making and design (rules, states, thresholds, and simple learning behaviors).
- Develop tangible–virtual interfaces that stage conversations among people, machines, and spaces.
- Translate physical environments into digital media using photogrammetry/scanning and 3D point clouds, and use these representations as interactive context.
- Represent systems via diagrams, drawings, and working demos.
- Culminate in a team tangible–virtual system prototype plus an individual design application that frames societal value and articulates a future human–computer/building conversation.

This course is co‑listed for undergraduate (4000 level) and graduate (6000 level) students. Undergraduates will complete individual and group prototypes but are not required to frame their work as thesis‑driven research. Graduate students will extend the design investigations into a research‑oriented project that complements their graduate work (e.g., thesis). They will articulate a research question, review relevant literature, and document their final prototype in a research report.

### Additional Academic Integrity Course Policy and Penalty Information:

**STATEMENT OF ACADEMIC INTEGRITY**

The Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities defines various forms of Academic Dishonesty and procedures for responding to them. Academic dishonesty violates the trust between students and teachers. Students must trust that teachers have made appropriate decisions about the structure and content of the courses they teach.

Teachers must trust that the assignments students turn in are their own performance. Violations of this trust undermines the educational process. Cheating, plagiarism (defined as the lack of attribution or citation of the work of others), willful damage or theft of another person’s work violate academic integrity. The first instance of the breach of this trust will merit a failing grade in this course and the violator(s) will be reported to the Dean of Students for disciplinary action.

The definitions and examples presented below are samples of types of academic dishonesty and are not to be construed as an exhaustive or exclusive list. The academic dishonesty policy applies to all students, undergraduate and graduate, and to scholarly pursuits and research. Additionally, attempts to commit academic dishonesty or to assist in the commission or attempt of such an act are also violations of this policy.

- Academic Fraud: The alteration of documentation relating to the grading process. For example, changing exam solutions to negotiate for a higher grade or tampering with an instructor’s grade book.
- Collaboration: Deliberate facilitation of academic dishonesty in any form. For example, allowing another student to observe an exam paper or allowing another student to “recycle” one’s old term paper or using one another’s work in a paper or lab report without citing it as another’s work.
- Copying: Obtaining information pertaining to a graded exercise by deliberately observing the paper of another student. For example, noting which alternative a neighboring student has circled on a multiple- choice exam.
- Cribbing: Use or attempted use of prohibited materials, information, or study aids in an academic exercise. For example, using an unauthorized formal sheet during an exam.
- Fabrication: Unauthorized falsification or invention of any information in an academic exercise. For example, use of “bought” or “ready-made” term papers, or falsifying lab records or reports.
- Plagiarism: Representing the work or words of another as one’s own through the omission of acknowledgment or reference. For example, using sentences verbatim from a published source in a term paper without appropriate referencing, or presenting as one’s own the detailed argument of a published source, or presenting as one’s own electronically or digitally enhanced graphic representations from any form of media.
- Sabotage: Destruction of another student’s work. For example, destroying a model, lab experiment, computer program, or term paper developed by another student.
- Substitution: Utilizing a proxy, or acting as a proxy, in any academic exercise. For example, taking an exam for another student or having a homework assignment done by someone else.
