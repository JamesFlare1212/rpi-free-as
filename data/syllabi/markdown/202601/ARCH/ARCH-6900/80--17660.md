## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

How do we sense, measure, and visualize environments in an age of planetary computation? This graduate seminar examines the historical and contemporary entanglements between data, ecology, and design, tracing a path from 19th-century pioneers of visualization to today's smart forests and computational planets. We will explore how data is never "raw" but always already cooked—shaped by the instruments that collect it, the mathematics that transform it, and the visualizations that make it sensible.

Beginning with the chronophotography of Étienne-Jules Marey, the moral statistics of Florence Nightingale, and the pedagogical visualizations of Emma Willard, we will investigate how techniques for inscribing time, space, and movement laid foundations for contemporary environmental sensing. Through close readings of vector mathematics and field theory, we will examine how abstract mathematical concepts become tools for representing environmental forces. We will critically engage with questions of precision, error, and uncertainty—asking not just how accurately we can measure environments, but what gets lost in the pursuit of exactitude.

The course situates these technical histories within broader ecological and political contexts. Drawing on scholarship from science and technology studies, media theory, and the environmental humanities, we will examine: smart cities and forests as emerging data infrastructures; the museum as a space of material decay and digital preservation; multispecies sensing and more-than-human data worlds; and the role of data in environmental justice movements. We will pay special attention to how data visualization operates as both a tool of power and a means of resistance—from colonial mapping to community air monitoring, from corporate dashboards to indigenous counter-cartographies.

Throughout, we will approach "data ecologies" not as neutral technical systems but as fraught territories where epistemology meets politics, where the virtual meets the material, and where human and nonhuman worlds become entangled in new formations. Students will develop critical frameworks for analyzing data infrastructures while imagining alternative possibilities for sensing, visualizing, and designing with environmental data. The seminar asks: How might we create data practices that enable more just and livable futures in an era of environmental crisis?

This course is designed for graduate students interested in the intersections of architecture, urbanism, environmental studies, digital media, and science and technology studies. No specific technical background is required, but students should be prepared to engage with readings from multiple disciplines and to think critically about the role of data in shaping contemporary environmental knowledge and action.

## Course Goals

### Goals:

This seminar is designed to support graduate students in developing the intellectual and methodological foundations for their thesis and dissertation research. Rather than prescribing a fixed body of knowledge, the course invites students to engage critically with readings that illuminate the cultural, technical, and ecological dimensions of data—broadly understood as both material and epistemological phenomenon.  
The course pursues three interrelated goals:

- **First**, students will use the seminar as a site for building and refining the literature that will inform their own research. The readings and discussions are intended not as ends in themselves but as scaffolding for the scholarly work each student is undertaking independently.
- **Second**, given the course's thematic focus on data ecologies, students are asked to develop conceptual frameworks for understanding the data they encounter in their own projects—whether as designers, researchers, or both. This means attending not only to what data represents but also to how it is produced, circulated, and embedded within broader social and environmental systems.
- **Third**, this is a reading-intensive seminar. Students should expect to engage substantively with assigned texts each week and to participate actively in collective discussion and critique. The quality of the seminar depends on each participant's preparation and willingness to think aloud with others.

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes Assessment Measures:

**ASSIGNMENTS**

All assignments should be submitted to the shared course folder.

**Weekly Reading Response (12.5% of Grade)**

Each week, students will submit a written response to the assigned readings. These responses should demonstrate careful engagement with the texts and may raise questions, identify tensions, or draw connections to other material.

**Weekly Commentary with Added Readings (12.5% of Grade)**

Alongside the reading response, students will contribute a brief commentary that introduces one or more supplementary readings of their own choosing. These additions should speak to the week's themes while also advancing the student's individual research interests. Include full citations and a short explanation of relevance.

**Exercises (12.5% of Grade)**

Throughout the semester, students will complete a series of brief exercises designed to prompt reflection and generate material for group discussion. These exercises will be assigned periodically and should be submitted together with the weekly reading response and commentary. This requirement also covers the exercises for the last two class meetings.

**Discussion Leadership (12.5% of Grade)**

Each student will be responsible for leading one class discussion during the semester. This includes framing the week's readings, preparing questions or provocations, and facilitating conversation among participants.

**Final Paper (50% of Grade)**

*Doctoral* students will submit a paper of approximately 20 pages that applies the conceptual themes of the course to their own dissertation research.

*Master's* students will submit a formal and detailed outline of their thesis, demonstrating how the course material has informed the structure and argument of their project.

- ### Academic Integrity

  Student-teacher relationships are built on trust. For example, students must trust that teachers have made appropriate decisions about the structure and content of the courses they teach, and teachers must trust that the assignments that students turn in are their own. Acts that violate this trust undermine the educational process. The Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities and The Graduate Student Supplement define various forms of Academic Dishonesty and you should make yourself familiar with these. In this class, all assignments that are turned in for a grade must represent the student’s own work. In cases where help was received, or teamwork was allowed, a notation on the assignment should indicate your collaboration.

  Violations of academic integrity may also be reported to the appropriate Dean (Dean of Students for undergraduate students or the Dean of Graduate Education for graduate students, respectively).

  If you have any question concerning this policy before submitting an assignment, please ask for clarification.
