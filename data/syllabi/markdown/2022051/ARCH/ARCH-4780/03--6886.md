## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

**MATERIAL MATTERS**

*“[Gottfried] Semper was to divide built form into two separate material procedures: into the tectonics of the frame, in which members of varying lengths are conjoined to encompass a spatial field [the term tectonic derives from tekton (Greek for carpenter or builder), than in turn stems from the Sanskrit taksan that refers to the craft of carpentry and to the use of the axe]; and the stereotomics of compressive mass that, while it may embody space, is constructed through the piling up of identical units (the term stereotomic deriving from the Greek term for solid, stereos for cutting). (…)*

*We tend to be unaware of the ontological consequences of these differences; that is to say, **of the way the framework tends towards the aerial and dematerialization of mass, whereas the mass is telluric, embedding itself ever deeper into the earth. The one tends toward light and the other towards dark**. (…)*

*Semper’s emphasis on the joint implies that fundamental syntactical transtition may be expressed as one passes from the stereotomic base to the tectonic frame, and that transition constitute the very essence of architecture (…). There is a spiritual value residing in the **“thingness”** of the constructed object, so much so that the generic joint becomes a point of ontological condensation rather than a mere connection.”*[1]

Semper was contesting the Vitruvian principles of *firmitas, utilitas, venustas, veritas* by arguing that “the archetypical origin of all built form was textile production, with the knot serving as the primordial joint”.

A few years later, in 1900 at the Architectural League of Chicago, Frank Lloyd Wright will declare that “a building for the first time may be lightly fabricated, complete, of mono material – literally woven into a pattern or design as was the oriental rug”. Wright will articulate this idea almost two decades years later into his text-tile wall system[2] implemented in several of residential projects, most famously in “La Miniatura” (The Milliard House, Pasadena, California, 1923).

Recovering from the modernistic condemnation as “a crime” (Adolf Loos), in the last decades ornament has slowly made his way back into architectural discussion. Among many others, the influence of the Herzog & De Meuron’s ouvre, Farshid Moussavi’s books (The Function of Ornament and The function of Form), and the development of new parametric design tools have expanded the focus of inquiry to the *performance* – not only aesthetic, but structural and environmental – of ornament.

Our studio aims to participate in this discussion by overriding the stereotomics / tectonics (and ornament) dilemma through the proposal of radical **new wall building systems based on 3-dimensional slip cast ceramics units.** These new systems should consider and integrate:

1. **Design / Fabrication**: the building unit system should derive as “an authentic ornament from the process of fabrication”; therefore, the unit shape/form should be *design-specific to the ceramic slip casting fabrication process*, taking advantage of the opportunities of the vessel / void space condition proper of this ceramic technique.
2. **Design / Effect + Performance**: the system should seek an aesthetical effect but also strives for structural and environmental performance as integral to the wall system. The structural argument should be clear and the system to be presented should be able to stand by itself. As per its *performance*, it should make a precise statement (per example: analogous to a cooling finned tube, it could be inferred that the increasing of wall surface could be a way for passive convective cooling as the air travels along the surface the wall).

**Haptic Thinking**

*The building appears first as a perceptual object that embraces a shape; that means, engaging ontology*[3]

The studio will operate under the premise of “haptic thinking” where objects not only embody ideas, but they are thought themselves. Therefore, prominence will be given to making and craft. All argumentations will be articulated mostly through fabrication. Make, then talk.

*“Craft is rarely mentioned today in architecture. This may signify nothing more than changing academic fashions: other words like "joint", "tectonic" and "phenomenology" are also infrequently found. On the other hand, these ellipses may be clues, signs of cultural change. The substitution of terms like "piece" and "connection" for "joint" may indicate a rejection of a grammar of architecture. The disappearance of "tectonics" may suggest that the association of a grammatical or hierarchical model of building components with an ethical or cognitive framework is no longer conceivable. The decline of "phenomenology", contrasted with the feverish discussion of sensibility and material properties, suggests that there may no longer be any desire to imagine a "science" of direct experience.*

*In the context of a discipline that is desperate to experience materiality, what accounts for the absence of "craft"? Craft is knowledge of material properties and techniques, but it is more than that. Craft applies this knowledge to non-routine fabrication tasks. Craft is adaptive behavior. Variability within limits is the essence of craft. As design intent, materials, methods, and outcomes become standardized, craft disappears and is replaced by technical expertise”.*[4]

As noted, the material focus will be on slip casting techniques and methods aiming to speculate about the possibilities of “vessel space”, the negative body[5] shaped by the mold and the continuous surface of the slip material.

The path that goes from clay to ceramics traces the history of masonry. Also, in the path from the unit to the multiple, a significant part of the history of building has been written. This segment of the studio aims to merge low and high-tech approaches to develop critical and creative investigations of different casting techniques and materials with a focus in mold-design and mold-making. Even though the physical result would be of three-dimensional building units that challenge the idea of what a “brick” and a “wall” could be, there is a deeper intention of searching for an epistemological inversion in the way that architectural form is thought and designed.

[1] Kenneth Frampton, “Rappel a l’Ordre: The Case for Tectonics”. See LMS

[2] See “Frank Lloyd Wright and the Text-tile tectonics” at Studies in Tectonic Culture: The Poetics of Construction in XIX & XX Century Architecture, by Kenneth Frampton.

[3] Yaneva, Albena. *Made by the Office for Metropolitan Architecture: an ethnography of design*. Rotterdam: 010 Publishers, 2009. pp 61

[4] Crembil, Gustavo and Lynch, Peter. Journal of Architectural Education, # 62 / 4, May 2009. pp 49

[5] As expressed in “The Uses of Not” a poem in the *Tao Te Ching* (by Lao-Tzu, around 600 BC): “Thirty spokes/ meet in the hub./ Where the wheel isn't/ is where it's useful./ Hollowed out,/ clay makes a pot./ Where the pot's not/ is where it's useful./ Cut doors and windows/ to make a room. / Where the room isn't,/ there's room for you. / So the profit in what is/ is in the use of what isn't”

## Learning Outcomes

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes (CLOs):

- Develop and improve her/his “haptic thinking” skills; understand the importance of craft in relation to architecture; and have a practical notion of high and low technological ways of thinking and making.
- Understand the notion of “vessel void”, as an inverse way to speculate and think about space; as well articulate and develop complex conceptual paradigms, such as the notion of “body”, in particular their application to architecture.
- Identify and interpret constraints and opportunities to be exploited as conceptual grounding in their design proposals; claim the manufacturing and fabrication as design scales pertinent to the field of architectural design; and develop basic proposal(s) that weave space and tectonics into comprehensive piece of architecture.

## Grading Criteria

### Criteria Details:

Grades will be established according to the following policy:

- A (EXCELLENT): Demonstrates a high level of curiosity, independent motivation, and a thorough, nuanced understanding of the topic at hand. Concepts are applied accurately; data are interpreted correctly; drawings are abundant, clear, and effective; content shows depth of thought and resourcefulness; written language is thoughtful, reflecting careful attention to the material, as well as clear, concise, and free of grammatical and spelling errors; oral presentations are coherent, logically-sequenced, and well-practiced; all assertions are supported with cited evidence; all work is clearly organized and visually compelling.
- B (GOOD): Demonstrates moderate curiosity, fairly consistent motivation, and a good understanding of the topic at hand. Concepts are applied fairly well; data are interpreted correctly; drawings are generally good but at times less clear or effective than desirable; content is substantial; written language is generally clear but less concise than ideal and contains minor grammatical or spelling errors; oral presentations are competent; all work is fairly well-organized and visually appealing.
- C (AVERAGE): Demonstrates limited curiosity, limited motivation, and an adequate understanding of the topic at hand. Concepts are sometimes mis-applied; data are interpreted with minor errors; drawings are limited in number, extent, and clarity; written language contains adequate content but is difficult to follow in places and/or contains significant grammatical and spelling errors; oral presentations are mildly disorganized and somewhat challenging to follow in places; work is in general mildly disorganized and visually inadequate.
- D (INFERIOR): Demonstrates minimal curiosity, motivation, and understanding of the topic at hand. Concepts are mis-applied; data are interpreted with significant errors; drawings are careless, unclear, or ineffective; written language contains little content, is thoroughly difficult to follow, and/or contains numerous grammatical and spelling errors; oral presentations are limited in scope, unpracticed, and extremely difficult to follow; most work is presented carelessly.
- F (FAIL): Fails to meet minimal expectations

### Other Course Policies:

**Shop Culture:** A fabrication-based studio consists not only of learning specific techniques and processes, but also about learning how to work in a shared shop and studio space. There are safety rules and regulations, as well proper behavior and working etiquette. This is a large class working with messy materials. There will be one rule that will be strictly enforced by faculty and shop coordinator: **cleaning while working and leaving the place organized and clean before you leave**. Messy and careless attitude will be noted and will affect your grade. Three warnings will result in lowering of your grade for one full letter.

**Pin-ups:** Periodically students will be required to present their work publicly in a formal review process. These presentations are a critical part of architectural education. It is an opportunity for the student and the faculty to judge the project and critically evaluate the process of design. It may be appropriate at points in the semester to evaluate only selected projects in order to convey information relevant to the entire class. In this situation it is important for each student to witness and participate in the review process. Even if your project is not being reviewed, the criticism and discussion should inform the evaluation of your own progress and design.

**Desk Critiques:** The design studio environment involves two-way communication. The role of the studio critic is to evolve and strengthen the student’s design process. Every time the student meets with the faculty, Students must have new iterations of work based on previous conversations. If the student has not produced new work, the faculty reserves the right to skip the critique and continue with other students who have produced new material.

**Archiving**: At the conclusion of this course, all students will be required to archive their work digitally by uploading it to the School of Architecture server. In addition, selected physical material will be collected and stored in the transition time between semesters to be photographed by the Publications Department. Please refer to the specific instructions for achieving processes found at the following link: http://www.arch.rpi.edu/2011/12/publications-archiving/. Grades for the semester will not be released until this material is received and reviewed. This means student will receive an I (incomplete) grade that will automatically convert into an F at the beginning of the Spring 2013 semester.

### Additional Academic Integrity Course Policy and Penalty Information:

**Academic Integrity:** The Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities defines various forms of Academic Dishonesty and procedures for responding to them. Academic dishonesty violates the trust between students and teachers. Students must trust that teachers have made appropriate decisions about the structure and content of the courses they teach. Teachers must trust that the assignments students turn in are their own performance. Violations of this trust undermine the educational process. Cheating, plagiarism (defined as the lack of attribution or citation of the work of others), willful damage or theft of another person’s work violate academic integrity. The first instance of the breach of this trust will merit a failing grade in this course and the violator(s) will be reported to the Dean of Students for disciplinary action. Please refer to The Rensselaer Handbook of Students Rights and Responsibilities (http://www.rpi.edu/student_life/hndbk04-06.pdf) for further details.

**Policy on collaboration and cheating/plagiarism:** Interdisciplinary collaboration is a critical component of this course. All assignments that are turned in for a grade must accurately represent the student’s own work, and properly cite authorship of work that was not solely completed by the student. Any miss-representation of work shall be evaluated and could result in a failing grade for the assignment. A subsequent infraction by the student will result in a failing grade for the course.
