### Other LMS Information:

Students in the School of Architecture at Rensselaer Polytechnic Institute will study how formal compositions cross potentially conflicting contexts and utility in their design studio this summer. In *Architecture and Disjunction*, Bernard Tschumi untangled form and use when he challenged us to imagine pole vaulting in the chapel, bicycling in the laundromat, sky diving in the elevator shaft. Of course buildings transition program over time in more mundance ways as churches become condominiums, factories become tech offices, and malls become schools.

**Act I - The Intertidal Ecological Armoring Unit**

*“We’ve spent the past one hundred years dredging out everything for shipping and hardening the edges. Now we have a different climate, and we need a different approach.”*

*- Kate Orff*

Much urban coastline exists as hard sea walls or riprap, large piled stones or other durable material used armor the coast against erosion and wave action. These have replaced long gone conditions in which the coasts existed in softer forms with vegetations and other life that mitigated against storm surges. Several investigators are exploring how to design and test armoring units that can reintroduce and support biological systems into the protected shore.

In this first act, students will study what happens within the eco-system of intertidal waters, using New York Harbor as a site for intervention. Understanding what used to grow there and what could grow there, students will compose three-dimensional units to fit into existing shoreline riprap meeting performance criteria of ecological coastal armoring units, which, when placed in intertidal waters, create micro-environments for small aquatic creatures and algae. Geometries will yield miniature tidal pools and promote plant growth through voids and surface articulations. The objects must perform yet have visual interest as they will be visible along the shoreline. Part of the challenge will be moving from concept into fabrication, which will be a design exercise in itself. We intend to install these in selected locations around New York Harbor and a marine biologist will observe the efficacy of the proposals over time.

**Act II - The Object in the Landscape**

*“Relationality has long had a good press well beyond the arts. Widespread sympathy for dynamic relations over dreary substances marks the general intellectual mood of our time.”*

*-Graham Harman*

What if we displace the object from it’s intended context and by doing so negate it’s own performance-driven justification? In act two, the same geometry will be used to test sculptural and scenography effects in context of natural landscape settings of rural New York, specifically Art Omi. Primary importance will be placed on visual interest, with emphasis on viewing the objects in the round yet the project will have a different enagement with this new context regardless of the presence of visitors. Art Omi has agreed to review and consider objects for placement in their sculpture park. Students will visit Art Omi early in the summer to see where they might be sited; locations will be coordinated with Art Omi’s curators. Curator input will be invited during early stages of formal prototyping before final forms are fabricated. The fabricated items will be delivered to Art Omi in the first two weeks of August for installation.

At this stage, one might ask: will the kelp enjoy the objects in equal measure to the art appreciator?

**Act III - The Enlarged Object**

*“The term mute icon is, in itself, a dichotomy:* mute*, referring to the inability to speak, express or represent, and* icon *as a sign, symbol or or representation that speaks clearly. The mute icon neither deftly communicates, nor is it wholly inhibited and silenced.”*

*-Marcelo Spina and Georgina Huljich*

In the final act, the students will produce high-resolution renderings, montaging their model into the photograph of an urban setting to test the efficacy of form at the building scale. Each group may select a site of their choice but must be able to obtain photos (in person or via the internet) and determine the massing of surrounding buildings in order to represent context. Figuration is determined but orientable; scale is ambiguous and left for interpretation. How to surfaces become articulated to suggest or subvert scale reading?

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes Assessment Measures:

The student will be able to demonstrate the ability to independently propose, refine, and develop a comprehensive design project.

The student will be able to demonstrate knowledge and mastery of the theoretical and applied research methodologies and practices used during the design process.

The student will be able to demonstrate the capacity to engage in both research and design work in such a way that each are beneficial to the other.

The student will be able to demonstrate knowledge and mastery of the various issues, problems, and themes that inform the design project.

The student will be able to demonstrate analytic ability through writing, diagramming, and drawing.

The student will be able to demonstrate creative ability in the design and development of the project as well as a capacity to document and present their work in a clear and effective way.

The student will be able to demonstrate an ability to communicate arguments in lucid written prose.

The student will be able to demonstrate a working knowledge of the use of citations and attributions in scholarly writing and presentations.

The student will be able to demonstrate effective research skills, including the ability to explore sources of knowledge external to the discipline of architecture.

## Grading Criteria

### Criteria Details:

Letter grades and modifiers will be based on the following phases:

10% Intertidal Study

30% Study Models

30% Fabricated Form

30% Renderings + 3d Model

Total: 100%

Grades will be established according to the following policy:

A (EXCELLENT) The design work is exceptional in every way. It has been undertaken with uncommon insight and energy and the ideas have been pursued with criticality and creativity. The project shown a strong ability to integrate systems and exceed the functional requirements. The results demonstrate the ability to innovate.

B (GOOD) Project is a creative and energetic response to the program, the work is timely, complete and well presented. Performance above the norm; accurate and complete; beyond the minimum requirements of the course; work demonstrates marked progress and initiative.

C (AVERAGE) Satisfactory work that meets minimum requirements and demonstrates satisfactory comprehension, communication skills, and effort; demonstrates little initiative to investigate the problem without substantial input from the instructor; work shows little improvement. Building systems are treated in a default manner and are not significantly transformed with respect to other systems.

D (INFERIOR) Unsatisfactorily meets minimum requirements; demonstrates minimum comprehension, communication skills, and effort at an inferior level; initiative lacking; improvement not noticeable. The work is not produced and submitted on time, or is incomplete. The various building systems show minimal integration.

F (FAIL) Does not meet minimum requirements; fails to adequately demonstrate comprehension, communication skills, and effort. major project presentations may be missed or have inadequate or missing components or the work does not demonstrate the care and thoroughness befitting a professional education. Systems show no integration.

### Additional Academic Integrity Course Policy and Penalty Information:

The Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities defines various forms of Academic Dishonesty and procedures for responding to them. Academic dishonesty violates the trust between students and teachers. Students must trust that teachers have made appropriate decisions about the structure and content of the courses they teach. Teachers must trust that the assignments students turn in are their own performance. Violations of this trust undermines the educational process. Cheating, plagiarism (defined as the lack of attribution or citation of the work of others), willful damage or theft of another person’s work violate academic integrity. The first instance of the breach of this trust will merit a failing grade in this course and the violator(s) will be reported to the Dean of Students for disciplinary action.
