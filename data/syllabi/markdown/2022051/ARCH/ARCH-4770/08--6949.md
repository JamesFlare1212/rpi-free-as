## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

**Manhattan Spaceport**  
***The Brief***  
SpaceX has become the worldwide leader in commercial space flight. The company’s goal, to make spaceflight affordable to the masses, is ambitious but not impossible given the young company’s achievements. Founded by Elon Musk in 2002, SpaceX has already developed a reusable launch system to reliably carry both cargo and people into orbit through its Falcon and Dragon launch systems. In May of 2020, SpaceX became the first private company to launch humans into orbit and the first private company to send humans to the International Space Station.  
SpaceX is developing “Starship” as a heavy lifting and reusable spacecraft to carry passengers and cargo into orbit, to the moon, and eventually to Mars. But most consequential to everyday life, Starship will be capable of quick low earth orbit flights from one side of the planet to the other.(3.) This would mean that any place on the planet could effectively be reached in under 49 minutes. In this context, SpaceX would require terminals in most major cities. These would be “spaceports”, where passengers bound for orbit, the moon, Mars, or even just the other side of the planet would go to prepare for departure.  
Similar to the emergence of the airport in the early twentieth century, this poses an opportunity for architects to imagine a brand new building typology. A spaceport however would be more than just an airport for space travel, it would also act as a branded experience for SpaceX and its goal of widely accessible human spaceflight. It could become the gateway to a whole new human experience.  
***The Project***  
Design a terminal or “space port” for the SpaceX corporation on the Hudson river waterfront in Manhattan. This facility will serve as a departure gate for passengers headed to space or low orbit transit to another major city. The program would consist of a waiting area, changing facilities, small medical facilities where passengers would be cleared for flight, offices and support space for SpaceX staff. Beyond this site specific facility, the design problem is more prototypical - The ideas and aesthetics developed for this site could be replicated for other Space X terminals around the globe.  
***The Site***  
The site is located in Midtown Manhattan on the Hudson River waterfront. The launch facility would need to be located in the Atlantic Ocean, away from high population density for safety and regulatory concerns. The Spaceport Terminal will serve as a departure point for the boat that would take passengers to and from the launch site. Therefore it would ideally be located along the waterfront and near an urban center. This makes midtown Manhattan the ideal location for the Manhattan Spaceport.  
There has recently been a surge of development in this area of Manhattan. This resurgence follows decades of stagnation in the development of Midtown. Spurred in part by the massive investment by Related and other developers, the area between Penn Station and the waterfront is undergoing an intense transformation. Notable developments include SOM’s Moynihan Station, SOM’s Manhattan West development, and KPF’s Hudson Yards.  
The site also lies near two other important urban features: the terminus of the DSR and Field Operations’ High Line park which could offer an opportunity for second level pedestrian connection to the spaceport, and the Hudson River Greenway which has become a vibrant waterfront pedestrian and cycling thoroughfare. The Greenway also connects the site to many other prominent waterfront destinations such as the recently completed Heatherwick project, the Little Island, the USS Intrepid Air and Space museum, the Manhattan cruise ship terminal, Chelsea piers and the recently refurbished pier 25 and 26.  
***Methodology and Assignments***  
To motivate and inspire the design of the Manhattan Spaceport we will study a variety of aesthetic inputs. We will analyze current SpaceX designs as well as historical spacecraft and equipment. We will also look at fictional and semi-fictional representations of the aesthetics of spaceflight through film, art, and other media.  
Assignment 1: Precedent Collage and Analytical Overdrawing  
Students will select two precedents from a collection of either real spacecraft and equipment to fictional spacecraft from art and film. Using a selection of these images they will produce a collage with the goal of dissociating the selected precedent object from its context and finding a compelling aesthetic motif. Students will then analyze their collage for key geometries that will be used for 3D development in the following stages of the project development.  
*Learning Outcomes:*  
Students will demonstrate the ability to evaluate 2D images for aesthetic qualities and to recombine them with various tools including Adobe Photoshop and Illustrator to create an original composition.  
Assignment 2: 3D Spatial Development  
Students will use various methods to bring their 2D composition into a 3D spatial proposal. These methods include folding, relief, and projection. The 3D spatial model that is developed from this exercise will be site-less and scaleless. These are not necessarily architectural but may hint at an interior spatiality or an inhabitability. Utilizing laser cutting, 3D printing, and other fabrication techniques, students will create multi-media physical models from these 3D Spatial studies.  
*Learning Outcomes:*  
Architectural design largely relies on the designer’s ability to move from different modes of representation to another as well as understanding spatial concepts as they translate between 2D and 3D media. Students will be evaluated based on their ability to translate 2D ideas from their collage into 3D ideas through different methods and strategies mentioned above.  
Assignment 3: Architectural Proposal  
Using the spatial model developed in Assignment 2, students will make an architectural proposal on site. They may use the spatial model as an inspiration for massing, detail, overall aesthetic, or all three. The primary deliverables for this assignment will be a plan, section, renderings of their proposal in context, detail and interior renderings as needed. Students will also develop the program of their architectural proposal according to the concept they would like to put forward for the project.  
*Learning Outcomes:*  
Students will be evaluated on their ability to translate their spatial object into an architectural proposal. Students will produce the above mentioned drawings and renderings as a documentation of this translation.

## Learning Outcomes

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes (CLOs):

- Students will demonstrate the ability to evaluate 2D images for aesthetic qualities and to recombine them with various tools including Adobe Photoshop and Illustrator to create an original composition. This is intended to help students talk through high level design concepts at a simplified 2D level before we develop these ideas into 3D.
- Architectural design largely relies on the designer’s ability to move from different modes of representation to another as well as understanding spatial concepts as they translate between 2D and 3D media. We will develop students' ability to translate 2D ideas from their collage into 3D ideas through different methods and strategies such as projection, folding, and relief.
- Finally, students will be evaluated on their ability to translate their spatial object into an architectural proposal. Students will produce a series of architectural details based on their 3D spatial model which was developed from their 2D composition. This sequence will help students to understand how to move from abstract aesthetic goals to concrete architectural proposals.

## Grading Criteria

### Criteria Details:

Grades will be established according to the following policy:

- A (EXCELLENT): Demonstrates a high level of curiosity, independent motivation, and a thorough, nuanced understanding of the topic at hand. Concepts are applied accurately; data are interpreted correctly; drawings are abundant, clear, and effective; content shows depth of thought and resourcefulness; written language is thoughtful, reflecting careful attention to the material, as well as clear, concise, and free of grammatical and spelling errors; oral presentations are coherent, logically-sequenced, and well-practiced; all assertions are supported with cited evidence; all work is clearly organized and visually compelling.
- B (GOOD): Demonstrates moderate curiosity, fairly consistent motivation, and a good understanding of the topic at hand. Concepts are applied fairly well; data are interpreted correctly; drawings are generally good but at times less clear or effective than desirable; content is substantial; written language is generally clear but less concise than ideal and contains minor grammatical or spelling errors; oral presentations are competent; all work is fairly well-organized and visually appealing.
- C (AVERAGE): Demonstrates limited curiosity, limited motivation, and an adequate understanding of the topic at hand. Concepts are sometimes mis-applied; data are interpreted with minor errors; drawings are limited in number, extent, and clarity; written language contains adequate content but is difficult to follow in places and/or contains significant grammatical and spelling errors; oral presentations are mildly disorganized and somewhat challenging to follow in places; work is in general mildly disorganized and visually inadequate.
- D (INFERIOR): Demonstrates minimal curiosity, motivation, and understanding of the topic at hand. Concepts are mis-applied; data are interpreted with significant errors; drawings are careless, unclear, or ineffective; written language contains little content, is thoroughly difficult to follow, and/or contains numerous grammatical and spelling errors; oral presentations are limited in scope, unpracticed, and extremely difficult to follow; most work is presented carelessly.
- F (FAIL): Fails to meet minimal expectations

### Attendance Policy:

Students are required to work in the studio during studio regular hours and have the required materials and basic tools to develop the assignments. Work includes participation in site visits, design and development, pinups, presentations, and discussions. Failure to work in class with undivided attention and disruptive behavior (tardiness, leaving early, lack of participation, general socializing, etc.), as well working on assignments from other classes during class, will count as absences.

Attendance is mandatory to all scheduled online meetings via live video conferencing. Students must prepare in advance to attend these sessions and participate otherwise you will be counted absent. Inability to access the internet or computer is not excusable. If a student has extenuating circumstances, she/he must notify your faculty member in advance. Two (2) unexcused absences from class will result in the lowering of the grade by one (1) letter. Four (4) unexcused absences from class during the term result in an automatic F.

Whether an absence (health, immediate family death, officially approved field / sport trip, etc.) is excused or unexcused is determined by the Students Experience Office (4th Floor Academy Hall, x8022, se@rpi.edu), upon presentation of required documentation, according to established policies and procedures (see http://se.rpi.edu/policies/ea/).

### Other Course Policies:

**Shop Culture:** A fabrication-based studio consists not only of learning specific techniques and processes, but also about learning how to work in a shared shop and studio space. There are safety rules and regulations, as well proper behavior and working etiquette. This is a large class working with messy materials. There will be one rule that will be strictly enforced by faculty and shop coordinator: **cleaning while working and leaving the place organized and clean before you leave**. Messy and careless attitude will be noted and will affect your grade. Three warnings will result in lowering of your grade for one full letter.

**Pin-ups:** Periodically students will be required to present their work publicly in a formal review process. These presentations are a critical part of architectural education. It is an opportunity for the student and the faculty to judge the project and critically evaluate the process of design. It may be appropriate at points in the semester to evaluate only selected projects in order to convey information relevant to the entire class. In this situation it is important for each student to witness and participate in the review process. Even if your project is not being reviewed, the criticism and discussion should inform the evaluation of your own progress and design.

**Desk Critiques:** The design studio environment involves two-way communication. The role of the studio critic is to evolve and strengthen the student’s design process. Every time the student meets with the faculty, Students must have new iterations of work based on previous conversations. If the student has not produced new work, the faculty reserves the right to skip the critique and continue with other students who have produced new material.

**Archiving**: At the conclusion of this course, all students will be required to archive their work digitally by uploading it to the School of Architecture server. In addition, selected physical material will be collected and stored in the transition time between semesters to be photographed by the Publications Department. Please refer to the specific instructions for achieving processes found at the following link: http://www.arch.rpi.edu/2011/12/publications-archiving/. Grades for the semester will not be released until this material is received and reviewed. This means student will receive an I (incomplete) grade that will automatically convert into an F at the beginning of the Spring 2013 semester.

### Additional Academic Integrity Course Policy and Penalty Information:

**Academic Integrity:** The Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities defines various forms of Academic Dishonesty and procedures for responding to them. Academic dishonesty violates the trust between students and teachers. Students must trust that teachers have made appropriate decisions about the structure and content of the courses they teach. Teachers must trust that the assignments students turn in are their own performance. Violations of this trust undermine the educational process. Cheating, plagiarism (defined as the lack of attribution or citation of the work of others), willful damage or theft of another person’s work violate academic integrity. The first instance of the breach of this trust will merit a failing grade in this course and the violator(s) will be reported to the Dean of Students for disciplinary action. Please refer to The Rensselaer Handbook of Students Rights and Responsibilities (http://www.rpi.edu/student_life/hndbk04-06.pdf) for further details.

**Policy on collaboration and cheating/plagiarism:** Interdisciplinary collaboration is a critical component of this course. All assignments that are turned in for a grade must accurately represent the student’s own work, and properly cite authorship of work that was not solely completed by the student. Any miss-representation of work shall be evaluated and could result in a failing grade for the assignment. A subsequent infraction by the student will result in a failing grade for the course.
