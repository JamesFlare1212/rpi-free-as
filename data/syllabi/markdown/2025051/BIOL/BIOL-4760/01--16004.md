## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

This course is the first of a two-semester sequence of molecular biochemistry. Here we will focus on the chemistry, structure, and function of biological molecules, macromolecules, and systems. Topics that will be covered include protein and nucleic acid structure, enzymology, mechanisms of catalysis, regulation, lipids and membranes, carbohydrates, bioenergetic, and carbohydrate metabolism.

## Learning Outcomes

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes (CLOs):

- At the end of the course a successful student will be able to: • identify and describe the structure, properties, and functions of the four classes of polymeric biomolecules (proteins, nucleic acids, lipids and polysaccharides) and their monomeric building blocks, and apply this knowledge to predict the behavior of biomolecules in novel situations. • comprehend and explain the specificity of enzymes, describe the factors that influence enzyme kinetics, discuss how the activity of enzymes can be modulated by activators, inhibitors, and environmental factors, and analyze data related to enzyme function to draw meaningful conclusions. • Understand and describe the roles of ligands, receptors, enzymes and other proteins in signal transduction pathways, apply this understanding to predict cellular responses to various stimuli, and analyze the potential consequences of disruptions to these pathways. • Describe the process of glucose metabolism, explain how it leads to the generation of ATP, articulate the regulation of these metabolic pathways, and synthesize this knowledge to explain how changes in metabolic activity might affect overall cellular function. • Identify and describe the activity regulation of key enzymes involved in electron transport and oxidative phosphorylation, and apply this knowledge to predict the effects of perturbations in these processes on cellular energy production. • demonstrate the ability to communicate complex biochemistry-related topics, tailoring the content and style of communication to suit different audiences (e. g., general public vs undergraduate biology majors), and construct cogent arguments based on biochemical evidence.

*In this class, all assignments that are turned in for a grade must represent the student’s own work. Please reach out to Dr. Fraser or Nnaemeka Awah for support if you encounter difficulties as you navigate this course. You may use AI programs e.g., ChatGPT or other similar large language models to assist with learning additional details of concepts covered in class or to generate examples of how the theories that we discuss are applied in real world examples. Additionally, ideation and brainstorming are allowed. Despite this, please note that the material generated by these programs may be inaccurate, incomplete, or otherwise problematic. Also, keep in mind that overuse of these tools will stifle your own independent thinking and creativity.*
