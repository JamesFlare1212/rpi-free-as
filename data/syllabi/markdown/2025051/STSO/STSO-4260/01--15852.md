## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

All of us know something, intimately and generally, about the state of the global agri-food system. Maybe we have felt the impact of rising prices of just about everything at grocery stores and restaurants. Likely we know someone who works to manage their diabetes, their food allergies, their gut issues, or a diet-related disease. We may have learned about nitrogen fertilizer run-off in rivers and streams, and the anoxic dead zones growing in already-overfished and warming oceans. We may have an elder or ancestor who faced famine and now encourages us to clean our plates. We, like 44 million other Americans this year, may experience hunger and food insecurity. As we start this summer term, 300,000 children in Gaza face imminent death by starvation, and 1.1 million children, if they are allowed to outlive this war, will feel the impacts of malnutrition for the rest of their lives. Food, farms, and famine are political terrains that weave through our very bones.

Why is it that when farmers produce far more than enough calories to feed each of the 8.1 billion people on this planet, so many of us are unable to access safe, nutritious, culturally relevant foods? Why is so much of our food seemingly bad for our bodies and for the earth? Is there a better way, and what might it take?

This course provides undergraduate students with a wide-ranging understanding of the environmental and social context of food, agriculture, and hunger. Drawing primarily on concepts and research from critical geography, critical food studies, and post-colonial studies, the class will use food, land, and nourishment as tools for examining structures of power, difference, and inequality across the economic scales of our lives - from our gut microbiomes to the global markets of the food system. Students enrolled in this course should be prepared to do a great deal of reading, writing, and speaking. This is an advanced undergraduate course, aimed at preparing students to do graduate level work and professional research.

## Learning Outcomes

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes (CLOs):

- Recognize and apply key social science concepts for analysis of food and agricultural issues, such as: race, class, gender, commodity chains, agri-food systems, food justice, globalization, neocolonialism, neoliberalism, etc.
- Use social theories and concepts to interpret, explain, and critique the socio-cultural, political, economic, and philosophical factors that influence contemporary food production, distribution, and consumption.
- Critically analyze and assess interdisciplinary research, media, and policy related to the agri-food system.
- Generate original hypotheses about the agri-food system—including possible solutions to problems related to hunger, environmental degradation, or rural poverty.

Attendance will be taken at the start of class and will count toward your participation score (see above). This class will primarily follow a discussion format. ### Other Course Policies:

CLASS AGREEMENTS

- **Class attendance is important and required.** I ask each of you to stay present in class, limit distractions, and participate actively in the discussion. I recognize that participation may look different for each of us. Listening actively to each other is the most important form of participation. Likewise, contributing to the notes (see next point), responding to open questions to the class, and fully engaging the assigned materials are important opportunities to participate meaningfully in class.
- **We will maintain collective notes**, to notice and track key themes, questions, articulations, assumptions, and other ideas that emerge from the assigned materials and the class discussions. Each of us will need to contribute to the notes, including the professor.
- **Let’s be on time**. It’s disruptive to everyone in the class when we are consistently late. Managing our time is a tough skill to develop. I will excuse reasonable absences and latenesses, especially if you communicate in advance. If I start to notice a pattern or correlation between attendance, understanding, and engagement, I will invite a conversation and consider an impact on your participation grade.
- **Make space / Take space.** If you tend to talk a lot, raise your hand first, interject - consider making space for others to speak. Likewise, if you tend to hang back and wait for a pause in the conversation before you contribute, make sure to take up space in the classroom and add your voice proactively.
- **Expect iteration:** it takes practice to build literacy and dexterity in complex social, ecological, and economic concepts. Revision and editing are essential skills. Expect to revisit topics and ideas, build on small changes, and articulate related ideas in new, familiar, or overlapping ways.
- **Ask for what you need.** I care about your mental and emotional wellbeing, and I want you to be successful in this class and across the board. That can look different for each of us. If you are falling behind, struggling, or concerned about how class is going, let’s work together before you feel like it’s a crisis. If a crisis sneaks up on you, let's work together then too. I’m not here to punish you - we are here to learn as much as we can.
- **Don’t let generative AI rob you of your vital human capacity to read, write, and develop your brain.** I understand the value of AI as a launch pad, a proofreader, etc, but I urge you not to use it to replace your own voice, ideas, and comprehension skills. I’d rather read something creatively and imperfectly your own than whatever the algorithm thinks a professor is looking for. Let’s be humans together in real time. Don’t let AI be a crutch for your one wild and precious mind!

Inclusive Language Guidelines:

The Department of Science and Technology Studies aspires to create a learning environment in which people of all identities are encouraged to contribute their perspectives to academic discourse. This course has guidelines on language and terminology that aim to foster an inclusive and welcoming environment, strengthen academic writing, enrich discussion, and reflect best professional practices.

Gender-inclusive and non-sexist language: Language is gender-inclusive and non-sexist when we use words that affirm and respect how people describe, express, and experience their gender. Gender-inclusive/non-sexist language acknowledges people of any gender (e.g. first year student versus freshman, chair versus chairman, humankind versus mankind). It also affirms non-binary gender identifications, and recognizes the difference between biological sex and gender expression. Students may share their preferred pronouns and names, and these gender identities and gender expressions should be honored.

Non-racist language: The labels and definitions of racial and ethnic categories vary across history, societies, and even situations. In this classroom, we will avoid racial and ethnic slurs and strive to use words that affirm and respect how people describe, express, and experience their race, ethnicity, ancestry, or cultural heritage. Please review this website for an overview of racial category terms, which discusses why some terms are offensive and others are preferred: https://raceandpedagogy.ssc.wisc.edu/getting-started/terminology/. Please note that this website is already somewhat outdated because it does not capitalize Black. Today, in the US context, Black and White should be capitalized, just like Latino/Latina/Latinx, Asian, Polish, Navajo, or any other term referring to a racial or ethnic category, Of course, there are many more racial and ethnic categories than those discussed on this website, and our language is always changing. Students may share the terminology that they prefer to describe their racial and ethnic identities, and these preferences should be honored.

Non-ableist language: Ableist language, which devalues people with physical and mental disabilities, permeates everyday language. However, there are many options for replacing common ableist rhetoric with more thoughtful alternatives. You are encouraged to read this guide and reflect on what you are communicating when you use ableist terms. https://www.autistichoya.com/p/ableist-words-and-terms-to-avoid.html

Please let me know if something said or done in the classroom, by either myself or other students, is particularly troubling or causes discomfort or offense. While our intention may not be to cause discomfort or offense, the impact of what happens throughout the course is not to be ignored and is something that I consider to be very important and deserving of attention. If and when this occurs, there are several ways to alleviate some of the discomfort or hurt you may experience:

- Discuss the situation privately with me. I am always open to listening to students' experiences, and want to work with students to find acceptable ways to process and address the issue.
- Discuss the situation with the class. Chances are there is at least one other student in the class who had a similar response to the material. Discussion enhances the ability for all class participants to have a fuller understanding of context and impact of course material and class discussions.
- Notify me of the issue through another source such as your academic advisor, a trusted faculty member, or a peer. If for any reason you do not feel comfortable discussing the issue directly with me, I encourage you to seek out another, more comfortable avenue to address the issue.

Sexual Misconduct and Title IX:

Title IX is a federal law prohibiting sex discrimination. The Title IX section of the Education Amendments Act of 1972 states, “No person in the United States shall, on the basis of sex, be excluded from participation in, be denied the benefits of, or be subjected to discrimination under any education program or activity receiving Federal financial assistance.” Under Title IX, sexual misconduct is considered a form of sex discrimination.

Sexual misconduct includes, but is not limited to: sexual harassment, sexual assault, dating and domestic violence, intimate partner violence, and stalking. Sexual misconduct is not tolerated at Rensselaer, and it is a violation of Institute policy, and state and federal law.

Faculty and other employees are considered mandated reporters when it comes to experiences of interpersonal violence (sexual assault, sexual harassment, dating or domestic violence, and stalking). Disclosures of interpersonal violence must be passed along to Rensselaer’s Title IX Coordinators who can help provide support and academic remedies for students who have been impacted. For more information or to report sexual misconduct, see

https://sexualviolence.rpi.edu/.
