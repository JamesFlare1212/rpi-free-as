**General Guidelines for the Laboratory Notebook**

Include a Table of Contents at the beginning of the notebook and update it when you start each new experiment. (Some notebooks have the Table of Contents on the inside front cover.)

- In order to facilitate use of your Table of Contents, each page in your notebook must be numbered. If you buy a notebook without numbered pages, you must number them in ink yourself.
- Always write in pen, not pencil.
- Write legibly. Do not use abbreviations.
- Keep your notebook as organized as possible. This is harder than it sounds, since all data, notes, and calculations should be done directly in the notebook, not on scratch paper first. The notebook is both your scratch paper and your final organized document. Strive for readability and organization by planning ahead when possible.
- Do not erase an entry or use a correction fluid. If you make a mistake, draw a single line through the error and continue writing. All corrections should be initialed and dated.
- Do not remove an original page. If the entire page is incorrect, draw a single diagonal line through the page.
- Only the carbon copy (not the original) should be removed from the notebook and submitted for grading.
- Start a new page for each new experiment.
- Write your name, section number, experiment number, title and date at the top of every page.
- All data is to be entered directly into the notebook.
- Don't leave gaps or blank space in the lab notebook. When you finish the last page of each experiment, put a corner-to-corner diagonal line through any remaining blank space, then date and initial the area. The purpose of this is that no one can go back in and add false details at a later date. Since each section (pre-lab and experimental data) will be turned in at a different time, each section needs to be started on the top of a separate page, so you will likely need to strike through blank space often.

Arguably, the most important aspects of notebook keeping are accuracy and completeness. What this means is that your notebook contains enough detail that someone completely unfamiliar with your work but with good background knowledge in chemistry can pick up your notebook and understand exactly what you did and why you did it, and could, given no other information, write your post-lab report for you. The notebook also needs to have enough procedural detail that any such chemist could exactly replicate your experiment, if necessary.

For grading of notebooks, 5 points will be allocated to good “notebook etiquette” (headers in place, pages signed, errors and blank space properly dealt with, legibility, no abbreviations, etc.). The other 5 points will be allocated for completeness. What makes your pre-lab write-up complete will vary from experiment to experiment. For more hints and tips for keeping a good notebook, see Appendix II (p. 912-913) in the Lehman text.  
**Pre-lab Write-ups**

The pre-lab is completed in your laboratory notebook and is your guide to the procedures you will conduct in the lab. Your lab textbook may not be brought into the lab, so you should be sure that the pre-lab contains all the information you will need to complete the experiment successfully. The pre-lab should, on average, be at least two pages long so that you may provide enough details on how the experiment will be executed. The following should be incorporated:

- Your name, section number, experiment number, title and date at the top of every page.
- A concise description of the objective of the experiment (1-2 sentences).
- Any relevant balanced chemical equation(s) using appropriate structures.
- Complete table of reagents, including: molecular weights, amounts expressed in both moles and a useful measurable quantity (volume for liquids, mass for solids), densities, mp/bp, solubility in solvents used, etc. This data should suffice for you to set up a limiting reaction calculation. Go back to your balanced equation(s) and label the limiting reagent(s).
- Any relevant calculations. These may include molarities of solutions you will prepare, theoretical yields of synthetic steps, and so forth. Many of these will need to be re-done after the experiment using actual amounts used (e.g. the procedure calls for 1.0 grams, but you measure out 1.012 grams), so include the setup of each calculation in your notebook for use as a template for more calculations later.
- Detailed procedure that you will follow. Number the steps to make them easy to reference while writing observations later. Flow charts often are an excellent way to organize your plan, especially when sequential isolation or purification steps are involved. Sketches of your glassware also are useful in visualizing (organizing) steps in a synthetic sequence. Also indicate the physical techniques and spectroscopic procedures that will be used in identifying a material and determining its purity. What else? Your objective is to incorporate succinctly all operational information that you will need to perform the experiment.
- Reference(s). You should reference the experimental procedure appropriately and any other sources you use to find information.

After you enter the lab, please give the copy of your pre-lab (NOT the original − the original stays in your notebook) to one of the TAs who will check for completeness and unlock your equipment drawer. The pre-lab requires you to prepare in advance and know precisely what you will be doing during the laboratory period. *Because this is a safety issue, a student will not be allowed to work in the lab without submitting a complete pre-lab.* As for grading, a complete pre-lab earns 10 out of 20 available pre-lab points. Up to 10 additional points will be added if there are no errors in the equation(s) and structures, or in the detailed procedure.

For more hints and tips for pre-lab write-ups, see Appendices II, IV and V (p.912-923) in the Lehman text.  
**Recording Observations During the Experiment**

After entering the lab, but before beginning the experiment, check the lab white board for additional instructions or procedure changes before you begin the experiment. While performing the experiment, you will write additional 1-2 pages of data and notes. As you do, make sure that each page follows the general notebook guidelines above, including your name, section number, experiment number, title and date at the top of every page.

For each step in the detailed procedure of your pre-lab, reference the step by its number, then indicate in your notebook:

- Completion of the step. Any step involving the use of instrumentation should include the model of the instrument used and enough information about settings and parameters to allow reliable replication by someone with good background knowledge in chemistry.
- Any step involving complicated glassware setup should include a sketch of that setup. If you thought ahead and sketched the setup in your procedure (recommended), there is no need to replicate the sketch.
- Any deviation from the procedure you originally wrote. (Do not alter what you originally wrote in your copy of the pre-lab report.) Also note any unforeseen problems that you encountered and what you did to resolve them, or at least to minimize any adverse impact.
- All measurements made, including actual amounts of reagents used (e.g., you will rarely be successful at weighing out exactly the mass called for in your procedure), actual reaction times, measured boiling or melting points, etc.
- Observations of the result of the step completed. These may include color, temperature or consistency changes, separation of layers, formation of a precipitate, disappearance of a solid as it dissolves, evolution of gas, etc. Remember, if nothing happens, it's still worth noting! "No change" is a valid observation and should be recorded.
- If the completed step is extraction, indicate all extraction layers formed and removed (a flowchart is very useful).
- It will sometimes be most convenient to document results using sketches (e.g. a TLC plate, denoting R*f* values). Any time the result of a step is a physical object (TLC plate, IR spectrum printout, gas chromatogram printout, etc.), indicate that the object was produced and give it a descriptive title in your lab notebook. For paper objects like IR spectra and gas chromatogram printouts, write the title, your name, date, and the corresponding page number in your notebook on the piece of paper.

Always remember to:

- Record all raw data in the notebook. For example, it is not acceptable to simply record the mass of a recovered product, since that is a calculated quantity and not raw data. You must record “mass of empty beaker” and “mass of beaker plus recovered product”. Then calculate the mass of product recovered.
- Include the appropriate number of significant digits for each number you record, and label each number well enough for clarity. This includes units and the compound or solution you are referencing. (For example: “Weighed out 3.01 grams of benzoic acid.”)
- Sign and date each page, then have it witnessed by a TA or instructor. Tear out the copy of your notes and turn it in to a TA before you leave the lab. They will file it; you keep the original.
- For grading of notebooks, 5 points will be allocated to good “notebook etiquette” (headers in place, pages signed and witnessed, errors and blank space properly dealt with, legibility, etc.). The other 5 points will be allocated for completeness. What makes your data record complete will vary from experiment to experiment.

**Post-lab Reports**

Post-labs are due the next lab meeting after you finish the experiment. For some labs we will allocate extra time if you (as a group) need a little more time during the next lab period for weighing a product, determining a melting point, or recording a spectrum. This post-lab report (generally 1-3 pages) **should be typewritten**. Make sure that the top of each page has a header containing your name, section number, experiment number, title and date.

The typical post-lab report should include the following sections:

- **Data/Results:** Include the raw experimental data (all raw numerical data obtained directly from the experiment, before it is processed; for example, mass of product(s), GC retention times, and other spectrometric parameters). Also include calculations of theoretical and percent yield and/or percent recovery and any other calculations based on the raw data (see Appendix IV (p. 918-922) in Lehman for help). If you synthesized a compound, you should include a physical description of the product (color, physical state), evidence of purity (mp, TLC R*f* value, etc.), percent yield, and all physical constants, spectral, and analytical data obtained for the product. For the identification of an unknown compound, include the results of all tests, physical constants, and spectral data. Attach copies of NMR and IR spectra (fully interpreted) and of gas chromatograms (fully labeled) which you used to establish identity or purity. Draw a clearly labeled and accurate sketch (or include a picture) of any developed TLC plates.
- **Discussion and Conclusions:** Provide a brief interpretation of your results and draw conclusions. Did you achieve the objective of the experiment? Did you prove the identity of the unknown compound? Explain how you arrived at your conclusions, showing clearly how your conclusions and reasoning are supported by the experimental evidence. Chemical mechanism(s) may be appropriate to clearly show the experimental reaction. If necessary, discuss possible sources of error. For example, if you obtained an impure or unexpected product, or a very low or high (>100%) yield, you should propose reasons for the outcome. Also, include your thoughts on improving the outcome if you were to repeat the experiment.
- **Exercises:** Provide answers to assigned questions in Lehman.
- **References:** Provide full references to any sources of data or information you used. Provide citations in the body of the text as information from references is used.

For more hints and tips for post-lab reports, see Appendix III (p. 913-917) in the Lehman text.

We will combine our copies of your pre-lab, lab notes, post-lab, and appended material as your final lab report. Grading rests primarily on the post-lab report plus appended data (if any), although this may be reviewed in the context of the other information present in your packet. For example, both your conclusions and how you arrived at them are important grading criteria, whereas the yield and purity of your product will already have been graded separately. The actual point allocation for the 20 pt. contribution of your post-lab report necessarily will vary for each lab experiment.

When it comes to scientific communication, aim for completeness, clarity, and brevity. Completeness and brevity may seem like conflicting goals, and when the two do conflict, completeness is more important. *However, an unnecessarily long or verbose post-lab report may lose style points, since some portion of the post-lab grade will always be allocated for following good scientific communication guidelines*.

Late lab reports will receive a penalty of one point per day late. Lab reports will not be accepted more than one week late, and you will receive a zero for any report that is more than one week late.

For more hints and tips for post-lab reports, see Appendices III (p. 913-917) in the Lehman text.

Analyze the data you have, not the data you think you are supposed to have. If the data in your notebook and the data reported in your post-lab report do not match, you are guilty of falsification of data. Any time your notebook and report do not match, and it seems to be an honest mistake or oversight, you will be penalized ten points on that experiment. Any time your notebook and report do not match and it seems to be intentional falsification of data, you will be penalized as described in the “Academic Integrity” section of this syllabus. Check and double check your data!

**References and Sources of Chemical Information**

Any time you need data about a compound or reaction, you need to cite the source of your information. Be careful to use reliable, professional sources of chemical information. Good sources include the CRC handbook, the Merck Index, PubChem, and ChemSpider. Wikipedia nearly always has accurate chemical information; however, since it is editable by anyone in the world, it is not considered an acceptable information source. By all means, start with Wikipedia, but verify the information using another source by following in-text reference links and end-of-article-links. Do not cite Wikipedia as a reference for your reports.

**Product Yield and Purity**

Your product should be turned in at the end of the laboratory period. You must label the vial legibly with your name, section, product name, boiling point/melting point, product mass and date. If you lost your sample or used your entire product for analysis, you must submit an explanation for the absence of sample for some credit. In general, as long as any product is obtained, you will earn at least 8 out of 15 points. Since the criteria for a good yield or purity depend on the reaction or experiment completed, the points given for different yield (%) and purity will also vary by experiment.  
**General Lab Technique**

Your performance in the lab, including attention to safety and cleanliness will be evaluated. The laboratories are scheduled for three hours, and it is expected that students can complete them in the allotted time. The need for additional time is usually indicative of poor laboratory technique and lack of adequate preparation. Reasons you may lose technique points include, but are not limited to:

- Not being prepared for lab. This is usually evidenced by an inability to complete the experiment in the allotted time.
- Lack of experimental independence. It is expected that you will have questions and occasionally need help from instructors and TAs during the semester; that's why they're there! However, a consistent pattern of dependence on help from TAs indicates a lack of experimental independence.
- Disregard for safety protocols. This might include completing work at the lab bench which should be completed in the hood, refusing to wear goggles or other safety equipment, or disregarding instructions for the proper disposal of leftover reagents.
- Lack of cleanliness. You are personally responsible for making sure your immediate work area and associated glassware are clean and ready for the next person to use.
- Inability to work with others. Approximately twenty of you will be working in the same lab at the same time, and you need to be respectful and cooperative toward all people in the lab at all times.

**Quizzes**

A quiz will be given following the completion of each experiment. Each quiz will consist of several questions to test your understanding of the techniques and concepts connected with the experiment that you performed. The source material for the questions will be the assigned reading from Lehman for the experiment, recitation notes and related material in your Organic Chemistry lecture textbook. Make-ups will not be given.  
**Safety**

Safety is the most important goal of every laboratory exercise! Never complete an experimental step if you do not know what you are doing, why you are doing it, and how to minimize any risks involved. When in doubt, ask!

Safety rules must be observed in this laboratory course at all times. Any of the laboratory staff can immediately dismiss a student who poses a danger to themselves or others. This includes proper attire. A student who is not “prepared” to do the experiment definitely poses a safety hazard. Ignoring the safety rules also will be grounds for dismissal.

Remember that all injuries must be reported immediately to a TA or lab supervisor, and that cleanliness/ tidiness and proper disposal of leftover chemicals are part of good safety practices. No student should leave the Walker Lab facilities during the lab periods, without getting permission from a lab supervisor. Student checkout entails a TA-approved laboratory bench and hood cleanup followed by a TA locking his/her equipment drawer.

For more about laboratory safety, see pages 13-30 in the Lehman text.

### Additional Academic Integrity Course Policy and Penalty Information:

**Academic Integrity**

The Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities defines various forms of Academic Dishonesty and you should make yourself familiar with these. In this class, all assignments that are turned in for a grade must represent the student’s own work. In cases where help was received, or teamwork was allowed, a notation on the assignment should indicate your collaboration. If you have any questions concerning this policy before submitting an assignment, please ask for clarification.

Each submitted report must be your own work and not copied from, or shared with, other students except when specifically authorized by your instructor. During all quizzes you are only allowed to use/access writing implements and a molecular modeling kit. Access to any other materials or objects (cell phones, calculators, laptops, written notes, etc.) is strictly prohibited.

In this course, academic dishonesty includes “fudging” laboratory data such as weights, melting points, etc. The fabrication of scientific data and information is an egregious violation of the underpinnings of scientific inquiry and the scientific method. Students are also expected to carry out all experimentation and data analysis independently, i.e., without referencing information collected by or conclusions deduced by others.

A violation of this policy will be treated as outlined below.  
**Penalties for Academic Integrity Violations**

A first instance of academic dishonesty will result in a grade of 0% on the experiment during which the dishonesty took place and the incident will be reported to the Dean of Students Office. A second instance of academic dishonesty will result in automatic failure of the course without option to withdraw, and again the incident will be reported to the Dean of Students Office. Procedures for appealing an academic integrity decision can be found at http://doso.rpi.edu/update.do? artcenterkey=676.

**Succeeding in This Course**

The purpose of the organic chemistry laboratory is to demonstrate the concepts learned in lecture, to expand your chemical intuition, to give you opportunities to practice scientific communication, and to help you develop practical skills used that will be useful in either industrial or academic careers.

Some hints and tips for succeeding in the Organic Laboratory:

- Read the experiment in your textbook before it is introduced in recitation. This way you will already know which chemical concepts or experimental protocols you do not fully understand, and you can pay extra attention to those points during the recitation. If they are not covered, ask!
- Watch the videos illustrating new laboratory operations as recommended by your instructor during recitation each week. These videos are posted on the course LMS. Also, read about each new operation in the Lehman text (Part V). This will help you visualize the operation before you perform it in the lab.
- Complete your pre-lab write-up several days before your scheduled laboratory section so that you have time to ask questions of TAs or instructor. However, it is important that the goals and procedures of the experiment are fresh in your mind when you enter the lab. So, whenever you write your pre-lab, take time to carefully review what you will be doing less than 24 hours before your laboratory section. This will save you both time and stress in the laboratory.
- Don’t hesitate to ask questions! Losing a technique point or two for incessant question-asking is far preferable to being unsafe or completing procedural steps incorrectly.
- When studying for the quiz, make sure you understand the experimental purpose, the procedure you followed, and the chemical concepts, reactions, and mechanisms involved. (If you already finished your post-lab report, then you have already forced yourself to review these things. Re-read your report as part of your review!). To assess your preparation, practice answering the study questions introduced during recitation.
