## Course Description

### Additional Course Description :

Superheroes have taken over motion pictures, and much of the rest of entertainment media. Will we beat them, join them, or attempt to change their ways? What do they mean to us, individually and as cultures? Since their origins in comics around 1938, their popularity has waxed and waned across their 80+-year history; are we at the end of this current cycle dominated by cinematic universes, or is it with us for a while longer? Do they (and other iconic media) shape or reflect their cultures of origin? In this class, you will hone your critical faculties beyond those of most humans as we struggle to unlock the secrets of this most pervasive of transmedial genres. We will apply tightly-focused beams of theory to mainstream work and seek out exceptional specimens of the genre that lie mysteriously hidden. Through collaborative learning strategies, you will influence the direction of this class and shape the creative and critical projects that we undertake, so that you become ‘heroes’ of your own learning. [This course engages students in a critical examination of superheroes, looking at their historical and present status as a dominant popular culture genre.]

## Course Goals

### Goals:

Overarching Objectives

1\) You will develop a faculty for critical media engagement with superheroes, learning how to apply aesthetic, historical, and critical frameworks as tools towards deepening your understanding of them. Of particular importance is learning how to make connections between conceptual/theoretical/historical issues and ‘superheroes’ as a transmedial cultural phenomenon. Although this class focuses on superheroes, its lessons can be applied to and contextualized in the larger mediascape; you should gain a deeper understanding of how media shapes us, as individuals, as groups and communities, and as cultures.

2\) As a complement to these critical tools, you will enhance your reflexive media awareness—your processual and dialogic understanding of your relationship to superheroes—so that you can more fully articulate your experience, contextualize it, and empathize with others’ perspectives. You will develop your communicative/generative skills by articulating and refining your thoughts in oral, written, and presentational forms, and (if you are so inclined) by creating media works in various formats as a way of engaging with concepts we encounter in this class.

3\) Finally, this class uses a collaborative learning process; the course will be shaped by your interests and prior knowledge as a community, as well as by my disciplinary, personal and pedagogical expertise. We’ll engage in meta-examinations of the learning process, considering how the course functions as a ‘medium’ for learning.

## Learning Outcomes

### Course (Student) Learning Outcomes (CLOs):

- CLO 1 Explain the parameters of the superhero genre as a significant part of the media landscape; this understanding will be contextualized by knowledge of the genre’s history, economic and socio-cultural foundations, and by explorations of academic and critical approaches to the genre.
- CLO 2 Articulate, from both a personal perspective and through dialogue with others, how the genre can shape our personal, group, and cultural identities. Extrapolate how popular culture media as a whole affects us and shapes our society.
- CLO 3 Communicate with others working on parallel, contrasting, or collaborative investigations.
- CLO 4 Develop an in-depth research, critical or creative project that investigates, explores, or challenges some aspect of the genre; share that project with the class, and consider how to take that work to a larger audience or apply it to other venues.
- CLO 5 Examine how educational processes/strategies shape learning experiences/outcomes.

PERSONAL ESSAYS on “What do Superheroes Mean to Me?”

[20,000 total = 10,000 Draft for Peer Review Process ; 20,000 Revised Essay]

These essays will consist of finely polished 750-1000 word (3–4 page) personal essays inspired by discussions we have in weeks 1 and 2 of the class, and in response to readings from the essay collections What is a Superhero? and Last Night a Superhero Saved My Life. You will have various options on how to engage with the central question “What are superheroes and/or what do they mean to me (or us)?” I’ll provide you with detailed guidelines for the assignment, and I’ll give you examples of personal and academic approaches that students have taken in the past.

WONDER WOMAN TIMELINE & WATCHMEN ANALYSIS

[Up to 35,000 total; 15,000 for contributions to both slides decks plus 20,000 for side quests related to articles on Wonder Woman and/or Watchmen]

We will spend the end of week 2 and a good part of week 3 examining the evolution of Wonder Woman (over 80+ years now!). As a group, we will construct a google slides document tracing how she has been portrayed across time—in comics, TV, film, games, and other media. You may gain points for being a ‘leader’ in this process, constructing initial drafts of slides for discussion. This examination will naturally focus on gender representation, but intersect with other identities and connect with questions of the popular culture and its media industries. Each of you will choose comics to cover; you will also choose other media through which to examine her character’s portrayals; you’ll choose academic/critical articles to apply as ‘critical lenses’ to the timeline. As a focus for week 3, we’ll be looking at several iterations of the ‘Watchmen’ superhero story (comics, in film, and on TV), comparing and contrasting how they attempt to deconstruct the superhero genre. We will have a collaborative slides deck that you can contribute to as a part of that discussion by reading and encapsulating articles about ‘Watchmen.’

SUPERHEROES AND ANALOG / BOARD GAMES SPECIAL UNIT [Up to 20,000 total]

[5,000 for participation in games days; 5,000 for contributions to the slides deck;

10,000 for participation in the Superheroes ‘Game Night’(or Day) and reflection]

There has been a vibrant ‘game renaissance’ in board games and tabletop RPGs; in this class, there will be a special unit on transmedial connections between tabletop/board games and the superhero genre. Here you can explore games in media studies, and it may steer you toward RPI’s games studies classes. We will discuss possibilities for which kinds of games we’ll play, based on student interest and availability, and determine the exact structure for that activity collaboratively. However, at its foundation, you will most certainly play games, think about them from a critical standpoint, and construct a slides deck or writings on your experience (which the participants will then share with the rest of the class).

MEDIA AWARENESS AND CRITICAL ACTIVITIES*

Superhero Texts - Journal and Reflection [20,000]

You’ll create a journal of your superhero-related reading/viewing/play over a designated period (recorded in whatever format you choose, as long as you can share it), and then write a reflection as a journal.

How am I (or Something I Care About) Portrayed in Superheroes [20,000]

You’ll consider how some aspect of your identity, or something that you care about, has been represented in the superhero genre.

‘Revisiting a Superhero from my Past’ Short Essay [20,000]

Anytime during the semester, you may revisit a superhero text that you remember from your past (childhood, for example), and reconsider that essay/media/phenomenon in light of what we’ve studied.

Deconstructivist/Revisionist Superhero Stories [20,000]

Inspired by the work that we do with ‘Watchmen,’ you can look for and examine what you might consider a ‘deconstructivist’ or ‘revisionist’ superhero story, and consider what makes it ‘tick.’ Recent examples might include ‘The Boys’ or ‘Invincible,’ but many superhero texts could be approached this way.

Critical Engagements with [New] Superhero Stories [20,000]

Here, you are quite simply asked to watch, read, play, or otherwise engage with some new product of the superhero genre, and then consider it using the critical tools we’ve been using in the class. For example, you could do this with a newly released movie or video game, or go grab a newly published comic.

SOLO ADVENTURES, SPIN-OFFS, OR RABBIT HOLES*

[10,000 TO 20,000 each, depending on the scope of your work]

These are opportunities for you to explore your interests in superheroes in adventurous ways. In general, you would either engage in a creative solo adventure or a critical spin-off or side quest, but in the end this kind of project is limitless, kind of like the ‘multiverses’ that seem to have become a popular trope these days (both inside and outside of the superhero genre).

For creative solo adventures, you might write a script, or you might design a poster for a non-existent superhero story, or you might write a song that your favorite superhero sings on their time off. For critical spin-offs or side quests, you might select one of the optional articles I’ll put in a folder, or find a book about an aspect of superhero history that fascinates you. I’ll give you examples of what students have done in the past. You are encouraged to use these solo adventures and spin-offs as a way to find a focus for your final project; I’ve had many students in the past create excellent final projects that were inspired by or based on the work they’d done in their solo adventures.

Final Project - [60,000]

For the final week of the semester, many of you will choose to prepare a culminating final project. You will then present, describe, or share that project in class. This project will be an in-depth engagement with some aspect of the superhero genre that you are deeply passionate about. It could be tied to the (tongue-in-cheek) themes of “Why not save the world of superheroes?” or “How are superheroes already saving the world?” but you will have an enormous amount of leeway in the shape of the project: It could be an academic research paper or a highly developed personal or critical essay; it could be a presentation of a point of view or a persuasive piece through a ‘video essay’ or in another medium; it could be a research work or survey data presented as a poster or set of charts; it could be a creative project that somehow transforms or gives a new insight into superheroes. We will start talking about the projects from week 1 of the class, and I’ll show you and/or describe numerous examples that students have produced in past classes. In many cases, you will be able to expand on work that you’ve done earlier in the class (for activities or on your ‘solo adventures’).

Presentations - in ‘Superhero Team’ Panels [20,000]

As a part of the final project process, you will be arranged into 3-4 person ‘superhero teams’ based primarily on commonalities of your final project proposals. You will work as ‘cohorts,’ meaning that while in general you will create projects on your own, you will help each other by providing feedback during the process of creating your projects, and by organizing the presentations of your projects so that they flow smoothly from one to the next, and so that you can encourage dialogue and discussion in the class. (If you have a particular degree of chemistry and find a way to create a truly collaborative project, that is great…please discuss that possibility with me.)

## Policies

### Academic Policies:

- ### Academic Integrity

  Student-teacher relationships are built on trust. For example, students must trust that teachers have made appropriate decisions about the structure and content of the courses they teach, and teachers must trust that the assignments that students turn in are their own. Acts that violate this trust undermine the educational process. The Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities and The Graduate Student Supplement define various forms of Academic Dishonesty and you should make yourself familiar with these. In this class, all assignments that are turned in for a grade must represent the student’s own work. In cases where help was received, or teamwork was allowed, a notation on the assignment should indicate your collaboration.

  Violations of academic integrity may also be reported to the appropriate Dean (Dean of Students for undergraduate students or the Dean of Graduate Education for graduate students, respectively).

  If you have any question concerning this policy before submitting an assignment, please ask for clarification. In addition, you can visit the following site for more information on our Academic Integrity Policy: [Students Rights, Responsibilities, and Judicial Affairs](https://info.rpi.edu/dean-students/student-rights-responsibilities-and-judicial-affairs).

- ### Disability Services

### Additional Academic Integrity Course Policy and Penalty Information:

Academic Integrity and Misconduct:

Student-teacher relationships are built on trust. For example, students must trust that teachers have made appropriate decisions about the structure and content of the courses they teach, and teachers must trust that the activities that students turn in are their own performance. Acts that violate this trust undermine the educational process. The Rensselaer Handbook of Student Rights and Responsibilities and The Graduate Student Supplement define various forms of Academic Dishonesty and you should make yourself familiar with these. In this class, all activities that are turned in for a grade must represent the student's own work. Submission of any assignment that is in violation of this policy will result in a penalty. If found in violation of the academic honesty policy, students may be subject to two types of penalty and be reported to the Dean of Students or the Dean of Graduate Education as appropriate. The first violation may result in an F grade or zero points for that assignment, and the second violation will likely result in failure of the course. If you have any questions concerning this policy before submitting an assignment, please ask for clarification.
